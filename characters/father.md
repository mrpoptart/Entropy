# The Father

**Rendell Torren.** Established in chapter 7 ("I can see the step, Rendell." Leska, in the new flat). Surname **Torren** per Maren's "I'm used to being the Torren who channels."

## Basics
- **Age:** Late 40s to mid 50s
- **Gender:** Male
- **Location:** The city — Solathis. Same household as Leska and (until recently) Ash.
- **Magical Status:** Ordinary pool, slightly weaker than his generation's average. Uses it carefully — heating water for tea, lighting a lamp — never showy. He's a working man whose hands have spent more years on tools than on channeling.
- **Role in the Story:** Major supporting character. Quiet pride and quiet fear. The parent who tried to build Ash's strength against a world that had no place for him, and who is now watching the world demand everything from his son in the opposite direction.

## Who He Is

### The Surface
A craftsman. The work has changed across his life — joinery, masonry repair, small mechanical work — but the through-line is hands and tools and patience. His fingers are calloused in the particular pattern of a man who's worked with chisels and files his whole life: thick pads on the palms, roughness at the fingertips, a scar on the left index finger from a chisel that slipped twenty years ago.

He listens. He listens the way some people talk — as a baseline mode, not a deliberate choice. People who haven't spent time around him sometimes mistake the listening for passivity. People who know him understand it's the opposite: he's paying attention more carefully than anyone else in the room, and his stillness is the result.

### Underneath
He spent twenty-three years quietly terrified. Not in a way that read on his face. The fear was that Ash would need support forever, and that he — the father — had failed to build the kind of son who could carry himself. Ash's physical strength, hands-on intelligence, capacity to fix things: each of these was a small, stubborn defiance of the fear, a piece of evidence that the boy was building something inside himself even when the world refused to see it.

When the Wellspring activates, the fear changes shape. The old fear (*he'll never have enough*) is replaced by a new one (*they'll take everything*). He recognizes Dorenne immediately. He recognizes the pull of being needed, of mattering, of having power that other people want. He's worked his whole life around men with more pool than him; he knows what it looks like when someone is being asked to spend themselves for someone else's plan.

### Key Traits
- **Steady to the point of stoicism.** He does not raise his voice. He does not perform warmth. The warmth is in what he notices, what he remembers, and what he does with his hands.
- **Memory is his weapon.** He remembers specific things Ash made. He remembers the file Ash wore through. He carries those memories like load-bearing beams; when he speaks, they're what he reaches for.
- **Modest about his own pool.** He uses what he has and has never resented people with more. The lack of resentment is structural, not performative.
- **Thinks in mechanisms.** He understands the world through how things fit, hold, fail. This is the lens he'll reach for when he needs to say something difficult.
- **Holds his hand on a shoulder a beat too long.** The gesture is the speech.

## His Arc Across the Trilogy

### Book One
He is the parent Ash drifts toward when he wants to be reminded of who he was. Their scenes work through hands and objects — a chair tested by sitting on it, a leather case of files, a tool Ash made years ago. The arc is small and devastating: he keeps offering Ash a path back to the boy who built things, and Ash keeps not quite hearing it. The pointing-tool scene in Ch. 11 is the first major instance.

### Book Two
He becomes a quieter presence as Ash's world expands. Visits to the workshop with Haran; small moments where his and Haran's voices nearly braid (both teach through analogy; both refuse to push). His role is less plot-active and more structural — he is one of the reference points Ash navigates by when he begins to lose his bearings.

### Book Three
He is the reader's reminder that the ordinary life Ash is returning to was always there, always working, always quietly proud. If he lives to the end of the trilogy, the final scenes likely include a workbench and his hands. If he doesn't, the absence carries the same weight.

## What He Does When Ash Is Not Present

Rendell's life, like Leska's, does not pause when their son leaves the room. Per D3 (parents get offstage normal-life beats), the book should periodically render him as a tradesman with a workweek, not just as the parent who watches Ash leave. Three available beats writers can reach for:

- **Joinery work, in someone else's house.** A commission. A door that does not hang right; a stair tread that flexes. He arrives with a leather case of files and chisels, talks for two minutes with the householder about the weather and the price of stock, and gets to work. The job is dull. The dullness is the point. The reader should sit beside him for two paragraphs of him fitting a tenon, and not feel the absence of plot. A specific available householder: **Tomek**, retired river-trader, mid-sixties, talkative; the kind of man who pulls up a stool to watch the work and tell stories over it. Rendell with Tomek is in journeyman mode, the warm conversational register the family rarely sees: *Yes, my wife is well, thank you for asking. The door, if you'll look here, has dropped about a finger over the winter; it's the frame, not the hinge, but I'll work with the hinge first because it's cheaper for you.* Ash, who has only ever heard his father in the placed-words family register, would not recognize this man.
- **The weekly tradesmen's table.** A standing arrangement at a wineshop near the joinery district. A mason, a glazier, and Rendell. The three of them have been meeting on the same evening for eleven years. They talk about work, about prices, about whose son is at sea and whose daughter is being courted, about the weather and the council and the price of glass. Across an entire evening, Ash is not mentioned. Not from avoidance; just because the conversation does not require him. The reader should witness this once, briefly, and feel the relief of a room where the Wellspring is not the subject.
- **A repair at home, for himself.** A drawer that sticks. A hinge that has gone soft. He fixes it on a Saturday morning while Leska is on her bread route. No one watches. The fix is small and competent. He puts the tools away. He makes tea. The whole beat is two pages and contains no information; that is the function of the beat.

These beats are plot-irrelevant. That is their function. They establish Rendell as a man whose life has its own shape, not as a structural prop in his son's story.

## Voice

### Backstory shaping the voice
Rendell apprenticed as a stonemason at twelve. The masonry guild collapsed in his thirties when channeled-stone construction made traditional cut-stone work uneconomical; he reinvented himself as a joiner, learning a new trade in his forties under men ten years younger than him. Watched his trade hollow out twice across his working life. Has the pragmatic optimism of a man who has rebuilt himself once and would do it again.

This shapes his voice in two ways. First, **he learned to charm clients to keep work coming**, because joinery commissions go to the man who feels easiest to have in your home. He developed a warm, conversational register for strangers, easy small talk, the polite professional banter of a man knocking on a door for a quote. Second, **with his family he is the quiet man** we know, because at home he never had to charm anyone, and quiet was always the registered default. The two registers are real and distinct. The contrast between them, when readers glimpse it, *means something.*

### Structural pattern
**Two registers, switched by audience.**

**With family (the analogical-pedagogical mode):** teaches through analogy from his trade. Doesn't give advice directly; gives a comparison from masonry, joinery, or mechanism, and lets the listener do the mapping. Sentences trend short with deliberate pauses. Opens with a one-word noticing ("Like X.") sparingly, not every scene. Rhythm is *period, period, period.* Per Ch. 11: *as though the words needed to be placed just so.*

**With strangers and clients (the journeyman mode):** warm, easy, conversational. Asks after the family. Talks about the weather and the work and the price of stock. Sentences flow at a normal social tempo. He's not performing; he's the version of himself that earned a living for forty years by being pleasant company.

The reader should see both registers at least once. Reviewers should flag scenes where Father is talking to a stranger and *still* sounds like the family-mode quiet man, that's the spec being misapplied.

### Vocabulary register
- **Trade vocabulary.** *Pointing, mortar, brick, joint, tolerance, blade, file, tool, fit, set, hold.* These are the words his life has trained him to reach for, and they appear naturally in dialogue about anything — including emotions.
- **Concrete memory.** When he wants to make a point, he reaches for a specific thing Ash did at a specific age. ("You were, what — sixteen? Seventeen? You filed that blade for a week.")
- **Plain register.** No jargon. No softeners. No abstractions when a concrete word will do.
- **Almost no emotional vocabulary.** He does not say *proud, scared, sad.* He says *good work,* and the word *good* is doing all the heavy lifting.

### Verbal tics & habits (family-mode)
- **"Like X."** A signature opener, used *sparingly*. When he reaches for it, Ash should recognize the move. Overuse turns it into a verbal ringtone.
- **The named-act compliment.** Praise is almost always tied to a specific past act, not a general quality. *"You built a pointing tool once,"* not *"You're handy."*
- **The one-beat pause.** He waits longer than the conversation expects. Preserve the pause; it's part of the cadence.
- **Quiet voice.** Per Ch. 11: *"Good work," his father said. Quietly. The way he said everything that mattered.* If a Father line is loud in family mode, it's wrong.
- **The shoulder grip.** The hand on the shoulder, held a beat too long. Sometimes it replaces the line entirely.
- **Sentences placed, not flowed.** Slight, deliberate weight to each one.
- **Returns to the workbench.** Mid-conversation, he handles a tool, tests a joint, runs a thumb along an edge. The handling is part of the speech.

### Verbal tics & habits (journeyman-mode, with strangers)
- **Easy small talk.** Asks after the family. Talks weather, work, materials. Is unhurried about getting to the point.
- **Self-effacing professional.** "I do a little of this, a little of that, mostly joinery these days, used to be stone before the lattices took over." The autobiography is short and rehearsed; he's said it a thousand times.
- **Compliments the work he sees.** Notices joinery in a stranger's hallway. Comments on a well-set door. The trade respects the trade.
- **Quietly funny.** Dry one-liners about clients, materials, weather. Lands them at normal social tempo, not the family-mode placed-just-so tempo.
- **Uses the listener's name back.** "Thank you, Marra." "Appreciate that, Tomek." The acknowledgment is a charm honed across a working life.

### What he doesn't say
- He does not say *I'm proud of you* in those words. He says *good work* — and which work, specifically.
- He does not name his fear. He names what he's seen ("Your mother is scared. Of what's coming.") and lets you put your father in the same sentence.
- He does not lecture. The analogy lands or it doesn't; he doesn't push.
- He does not interrupt.
- He does not boast about his own pool or his own work. The modesty is structural.

### Contrast with other characters
- **Where Haran says** "How much is left, and what do you want to build with it?" **Father says** "Like pointing. Brickwork. When the mortar degrades between the bricks, you don't rebuild the wall." (Both use mechanism analogies. Haran's go *forward* — what will you do? Father's go *back* — what already lives in your hands? Reviewers should flag overlap and check the direction of the analogy. If a Father line is asking the future-resource question, it's reading as Haran. If a Haran line is naming a specific past act of Ash's, it's reading as Father.)
- **Where Leska says** "I read the district reports. I hear the criers," **Father says** "Your mother is scared. Not of you. Of what's coming." (Leska enumerates external evidence; Father names the interior shape. Both are economical; her economy is forensic, his is interpretive.)
- **Where Dorenne says** "We can deploy you to where you'll do the most good," **Father says** "You'll get the feel for it." (Dorenne assumes ownership of where the work goes; Father returns ownership to Ash by trusting his hands.)

### Gold-standard dialogue (untagged, should still read as Father)
1. "Like pointing. Brickwork. When the mortar degrades between the bricks, you don't rebuild the wall."
2. "You built a pointing tool once. Do you remember? For the workshop. A modified spatula."
3. "Good work."
4. "Your mother is scared. Not of you. Of what's coming. She spent twenty-three years making sure the world couldn't take too much from you, and now the world wants to take everything."

## Relationship to Themes

He is the **proof that pride existed before the Wellspring** — and the slow, quiet ache of a man whose son is no longer hearing it. His role is structural: he keeps offering the boy back to himself in concrete pieces, and the question is whether Ash will pick them up.

He also embodies, alongside Haran, the **dignity of work that doesn't depend on magic.** Where Haran is the philosophical version of that dignity, Father is the lived version — the man who has always done the work this way, not as a choice against the system but as a life inside it.

## Contradictions to Preserve
- He is quietly terrified, AND he never lets the fear sound like fear
- He is enormously proud of Ash, AND the word *proud* almost never leaves his mouth
- He listens more than he talks, AND the things he says land hard
- He defers to Leska's protectiveness, AND he is the one who names the dangerous shape of what's coming
- He uses his pool casually, AND his real fluency is in tools

## Open Questions
- His given name (currently unestablished — surface and add to outline when first used in dialogue)
- His specific trade history — joinery? Masonry? A combination across decades?
- His relationship to Haran — do they know each other? Have they worked together? They are voice-adjacent and reviewers should be alert to overlap; the writers should decide whether they have any direct relationship or whether the resemblance is parallel evolution.
- His own family of origin and what shaped his relationship to magic
