# Chapter 16

The letter came up from the lower gate with the morning post, and Haran read it standing, and did not sit down.

Ash was at the stove with the kettle. He had banked the forge the night before and come down at first light to unbank it, and the shop had been cold in the way the shop was cold at the end of a long thaw, with the cold sitting low on the flagstones and the warmth of yesterday's fire only a memory in the iron. He had warmed the kettle the way he warmed the kettle, and his palm had been a finger's width from the base of it, and he had felt, this morning, for the thread. He had not found it. He had found, instead, the wide loose press he had been finding for a year and a half, and the kettle had warmed, and the ring under his hand had warmed with it, and he had stopped.

He had been pouring tea when the boy came in with the post.

The boy was the boy Haran kept for letters, a long thin child of about eleven from the lane below, who came up the curve twice a week with whatever had arrived for the workshop and who took, in payment, a piece of bread and whatever Haran had on the bench that could be wrapped in paper. He came in without knocking. He set the bundle on the corner of the bench. He stood a count, looking at the forge, which was the thing he liked to look at, and Haran reached into his apron and put a small wound coil of copper into the boy's hand and said, "Mind the edge," and the boy nodded and went out.

Haran took the bundle to the bench.

He sorted it the way he sorted post, which was to say he laid the letters down in the order he picked them up and looked at the hand on each, and put two aside without opening, and slit a third with the small knife he kept in his apron pocket. He read it standing. He read it once. He read it again. He set it on the bench.

He did not say anything for a count.

Then he said, "Ardenmere."

Ash had set the cup down. He did not remember setting the cup down. The cup was on the bench beside him, and his hand was at his side, and the warmth in his palm from the kettle was the warmth in his palm, and Haran had said a word that was a place.

"Ardenmere. That's, sorry, that's the, that's out past the rye country, isn't it. Ardenmere."

"Three days' ride. South and east. Out past the rye country."

"Yes. Yeah. I, I know it."

"Gone."

"Gone. I mean. Gone, as in, gone what."

"That's the word he uses." Haran tapped the page with the back of two fingers, twice, as if testing whether the page was still there. "Tomas. Tinkerer out that way. Has been writing me twenty years. He's not a man who uses a word that isn't the word."

"What does he say."

Haran looked at the page.

"Pools too weak for the irrigation. Crops in the second field went in three days. Healing house can't hold a working. Crystals silent four days. Heating mains in the town quarter cold. Mill won't take a channel. Bakery on the square went to wood and the wood is wet. He says they have a week of grain in the public store and after the week he doesn't know."

"How. I mean, how, sorry, how at once. How."

"All at once."

"How. How does a, how does a whole, "

"That's the question." Haran set the page down. He smoothed it with the heel of his hand the way he smoothed a thing he did not want to touch any more than he had to. "He says the elders aged. He says the young aren't strong enough. He says the pools have been shallowing for years and they didn't, "

He stopped.

"Didn't what."

"Didn't change anything."

Ash took a step toward the bench. He did not, until he had taken it, know he was going to take it. He took another. He stood at the bench beside Haran and looked at the page, and the page was a page in a hand he did not know, and the hand was a careful hand, and the careful hand had set down, in a list at the foot of the page, the failures, in the order Tomas had walked through them, and the list was a list of the kind a man made who was trying to stay calm while he made it.

Ash read it.

He read it twice.

"I have to go," he said.

Haran did not look up. He took a clean cloth from the shelf and folded it and laid it over the page, the way a man covered a thing on a bench he was going to come back to but did not want to look at while he was thinking.

"Mm."

"Three days. I can be there in two if I..."

"Mm."

"Haran."

Haran looked up.

He looked at Ash the way he looked at a piece of iron at the wrong temperature, which was without unkindness and without hurry, and with the small tilt of his head that meant he was not going to say the next thing until the next thing was the thing.

"What would you do when you got there," he said.

Ash opened his mouth.

"Pour the reservoir into their fields until you left. And then what."

The shop was quiet.

The forge had not yet caught. Ash had laid in kindling and had been about to light it when the post came in, and the cross of tinder was on the cold coals and the bellows handle was in the position Haran had left it last night, and the only sound in the shop was the small tick of the kettle on the stove, which was cooling now, in the corner, because the channeling had stopped and the ring was giving its borrowed warmth back to the air.

Ash closed his mouth.

He turned to the stove. He took the kettle off the ring with the cloth at the handle. He set it on the iron plate at the side of the stove where Haran kept the warm things warm. He did this slowly, the way a man did a small task slowly because he was not yet ready to do the next task.

"Two days there," he said. "A day pouring. Two days back."

"And then what."

"And then I..."

"And then what, Ash."

He stood at the stove with the cloth in his hand.

He stood with the cloth in his hand a count, and the cloth was the cloth, and the kettle was on the iron plate, and the cup he had poured was on the bench behind him with the steam coming up off it in a thin rising line that bent at the ceiling, and he did not, in the count, answer.

"Five days," Haran said. "Maybe six. Maybe a percent of the pool. Maybe two. And in six days the second field is gone again, because the irrigation is the irrigation it is, and you're back on the road or you're staying, and if you're staying you're staying until the pool you brought is dry, and the pool you brought is also the lattice in the upper terraces and the mains in the second district and the press where Hira works."

"Yes."

"It's a real question."

"Yes. Yeah. I know."

Haran turned back to the bench. He lifted the cloth off the page. He looked at the page again.

"Sit down."

"I, "

"Sit down. Drink the tea. We're not going today."

Ash sat down.

He sat on the high stool at the corner of the bench. He took the cup. The cup was warm in his hands the way a cup was warm in cold hands, which was the small steady warmth of a thing that had been made for the warming, and he held it, and he did not drink it for a count, and then he drank it, because Haran had said to drink it.

The forge was cold.

The forge would not be cold long. He would light it when the tea was finished. He would light it because the work of the day was the work of the day, and the work did not stop because a place three days' ride away had stopped, and Haran was at the bench and the bench was where Haran was, and the cloth was over the page, and the page was on the bench, and the page would still be on the bench in an hour.

\* \* \*

The letters came over the next four days.

Not all from Tomas. Tomas wrote again, and again, in the same careful hand, on a paper that had been folded too many times for the road, and his second letter was the list of what had failed since the first letter, and the list was longer, and the list ended at a place where Tomas had begun to write a sentence and had not finished it, and the unfinished sentence sat at the bottom of the page in the way an unfinished sentence sat.

But there were others.

Haran's network was a network in a way Ash had not, until the letters came, understood. He had known there were tinkerers. He had known Haran wrote to them, in evenings, in the corner of the workshop, on a stack of small sheets he kept under the lamp. He had known the names because Haran had said the names, in passing, in the way Haran said things, which was to leave them on the bench and go back to the work. He had not known the size of the thing.

The size of the thing was the post.

The post came twice a day for four days. The boy came up the curve in the morning and again at the second hour after noon, and each time he set a bundle on the corner of the bench, and each time Haran sorted it, and each time there were more letters than Ash had thought there would be. They came from the rye country and from the hill country and from the coast and from the highland villages above the second pass, and they came in hands Ash did not know, and Haran read each one standing, and each one he read he set in one of three small piles on the bench, and the piles grew through the day, and at evening Haran sat at the bench with the lamp and went through the piles again, and wrote.

Ash read what Haran put in front of him.

Haran did not, on the first day, put much in front of him. Haran put Tomas's second letter. Haran put a short note from a woman named Beska in a settlement called Low Combe, which was a hill country place Ash had never heard of, and Beska's hand was small and even and her note was three lines.

*The Ardenmere news has reached us. We are well. The wheel held the spring planting. We will write at the new moon as agreed.*

That was all.

Ash read it twice. He did not, the first time, understand it. He understood it the second time the way a man understood a thing by reading it slowly, which was to say he understood that *the wheel* was a thing that had a name in Beska's house, and that the name was a name because the wheel was a wheel that had been there long enough to be named, and that *the spring planting* was a thing Beska's settlement had done, this spring, with the wheel and not with a channel.

"What's the wheel," he said.

Haran did not look up.

"Mechanical irrigation. Lift wheel on the creek above the settlement. Tomas's brother put it in two summers ago."

"Two summers."

"Mm."

"They've been running it two summers."

"Mm."

Ash set the letter down.

He picked up the next one. The next one was from a man named Curran in a coast village called Hask, and Curran's hand was large and slanted and he wrote in a kind of clipped hurry, and his letter was four lines.

*We are out three boats this week. The new sail held in the squall on Tuesday. Two of the lads wanted the old engine and I told them no. The second mast is cracked at the step and we will need a piece. If you have the iron we have the fish.*

Ash read it.

He did not, this time, ask. He understood, sitting on the stool with the cup of tea cold in his hand, what *the new sail* meant, and what *the old engine* meant, and what *we are out three boats this week* meant. He understood that Hask was a coast village that, until two years or three years or some number of years ago, had channeled propulsion to its boats, and that the channeled propulsion had failed, and that Hask had built sails. He understood that *the new sail* was new because nobody in Hask, or nobody alive, had used a sail before, and that two of the lads had wanted to wait for a man like Ash to come and channel the old engine back to working, and that Curran had said no.

He understood that Curran had said no two years ago.

He set the letter down.

\* \* \*

The second day brought more.

The boy came at the morning hour with a bundle of seven, and at the afternoon hour with a bundle of four. Haran sorted them and read them and made his piles, and on the second day he began, after the sorting, to lay certain letters out in front of Ash on the long bench, in a row, the way a man laid out parts of a thing he was assembling.

He laid out, on the second day, a letter from a woman named Iren in a highland village called Shen Pass. Iren's hand was a deliberate hand, in a thick black ink, and her letter was longer than the others, because Iren was the village's record-keeper and her letters had the small even cadence of a record-keeper's letters, and her letter went:

*Haran, the two houses below the meeting hall are now wrapped in the sheep-wool batt with the tarred linen over, in the pattern Joren laid out last autumn. The wrap is not pretty. The wrap is functional. The two houses held three degrees warmer than the unwrapped houses through the cold week, and we burned a third less of the wood ration. Joren says the linen is the part that matters. The wool alone wets through. We will wrap the meeting hall before next winter if the linen comes. If you can send a roll with the carter, we will send a wheel of the hard cheese back, which is not a fair trade, but is the trade we have.*

Ash read it.

He read it standing, with his hand on the bench at the edge of the page, and he did not, when he was done, pick up the next letter.

"Three degrees. That's, sorry, that's, "

"Mm."

"In the cold week."

"Mm."

"How many houses in Shen Pass."

"Forty. Give or take."

"And they're wrapping them. I mean, they've been, yeah."

"Two so far. They'll do the meeting hall this year. They'll do the rest as the linen comes."

"How long has Joren been working on this."

Haran took the letter off the bench. He folded it. He set it in the pile on his right, which was the pile he was going to answer first.

"Three years," he said.

Ash sat down on the stool.

He sat with his hands on his knees. The forge was lit. He had lit it when the morning kettle was warmed, and Haran had worked at it for an hour on a small piece, and the heat of it was the heat of the shop now, and the heat came across the floor, and the bench was warm under his palm where he had laid his palm down. He looked at the row of letters. There were six of them, on the long bench, in a row, and each one was a place, and each place was a place where ordinary people had, two summers ago or three years ago or four years ago, started doing a thing differently because they had to.

Ash had not, two summers ago, been doing anything differently.

Two summers ago he had been in his father's kitchen with a number that had just shown up on a paper, and he had been afraid of the number, and the fear of the number had been the only thing in the room. Three years ago he had been a boy with a bucket in a town with a working press. Four years ago he had been a boy.

Haran had been writing to Joren.

Haran had been writing to Joren for thirty years.

\* \* \*

On the third day the letter from Tomas came that did not have the unfinished sentence. It had, instead, a small list at the bottom of the page, in the same careful hand, and the list was the list of what was holding.

*The bakery on the square has gone to a wood oven. The wood is from the south wood, which is wet, and the bread is poor, but the bread is bread.*

*The well in the town quarter is being drawn by hand. The crank is the old crank from the smithy. It works.*

*The healing house has sent the worst cases to Selvern, two days east. The road is open. The cart is the cart.*

*Two of the elders have begun to teach the young the work that does not require a channel. There is a great deal of this work. The teaching is going.*

*This is what we should have done five years ago.*

Haran read it. He set it on the bench. He did not put it under a cloth. He left it open on the bench in the place between his hand and Ash's, and he went on with the work he was doing, which was the working of a small bracket for a fitting at the corner of the forge, and he worked at it with the small hammer and the small chisel and the small adjustments of a man working a small thing, and he did not, while he worked, look at the page.

Ash looked at the page.

He looked at it the way he had been looking at the letters for three days, which was the way of a man learning to read a thing he had not known he was illiterate in.

\* \* \*

Ryn came on the fourth day.

She came in the late afternoon, when the light through the high window was the long gold kind that came in through the high window only in the hour before evening and made the dust in the workshop visible the way only that light made the dust in the workshop visible. She came in without knocking. She set a cloth bundle on the corner of the bench, which was bread from her gate, because Ryn never came to the workshop without bringing the bread her aunt had left at the gate that morning, and which Ryn always claimed she could not eat alone.

"Tea," she said.

"Yes," Ash said.

He went to the stove. He warmed the kettle. He warmed it the way he had been warming it for four days, which was the way he had been warming it for a year and a half, which was not the way Haran warmed it, and the ring under his palm warmed with the kettle, and he did not, this evening, feel for the thread. He had been feeling for the thread on the kettle in the morning and not finding it, and the not-finding had been the not-finding it had been on the rod at the forge, and the not-finding had built, over four days, into the small steady heaviness of a thing he had not yet named.

He poured.

He brought Ryn her cup. She took it in both hands and turned on the stool to look at the long bench, where the letters were now in three uneven rows, because Haran had laid more out, and because Ash had been going through them in the evenings, and because the bench had become, over four days, the thing the bench was now, which was a kind of map.

She looked at the letters.

She did not, for a count, ask.

Then she said, "Ardenmere."

"Yes."

"Bad."

"Yes."

"How bad."

Ash looked at the bench.

"Tomas writes that the bakery has gone to wood. The wood is wet. They have a week of grain. The healing house is sending its worst cases to Selvern."

"Selvern."

"Two days east. Tomas says the road is open."

"For now."

"For now."

Ryn drank from the cup. She drank the way she drank, which was small, and slow, and in counts. She looked at the letters.

"And these," she said.

"These are the others."

"Tell me one."

He picked up the letter from Iren. He did not, for the first count of holding it, read it aloud. He held it. He held it the way a man held a thing in front of him in a room with a person he wanted, for once, to see the thing too.

He read it.

He read it slowly. He read the part about the sheep-wool batt and the tarred linen and the three degrees and the third less of the wood ration, and he read the part about Joren, and he read the part about the wheel of the hard cheese, which was not a fair trade, which was the trade they had, and he set the letter down.

Ryn sat with her cup.

She did not, for a count, say anything. Then she said, "Three years."

"Three years."

"Joren."

"Joren."

"And Haran's known him."

"Thirty years."

She set the cup down on the bench beside her. She set it carefully, in the way Ryn set a cup down, which was straight, and centered, and with the handle pointing the way she had decided the handle should point.

"Ash."

"Mm."

"They were ahead of you."

He did not say anything.

"All of them. The wheel. The sails. The wrap. They were ahead of you."

"Yes."

"They're not, "

"They're not thriving."

"No."

"Iren writes that the wrap is not pretty. Curran writes that the second mast is cracked at the step. They're holding. They're holding by their teeth and by Joren's three years and by Curran saying no to two of his lads, and they're holding."

"Yes."

She looked at him.

She looked at him for a count. Ryn had a way of looking at him that was the way she had had since they were children, which was without any pressure on the looking, and was not asking anything of him, and was the kind of looking that a man could, if he chose, look back at, and could, if he chose, look away from, and the looking did not change. She looked at him this way now.

"So the question isn't whether your approach works," she said.

"No."

"It's whether you can get out of the way fast enough for other people to do it themselves."

He set his cup down.

He set it down on the bench, and the cup made the small sound a cup made on a bench, and he sat with his hands on his knees, and he looked at the letters in the long gold light, and the dust in the high window was the dust in the high window, and Ryn's question was on the bench between them the way the cloth had been on the bench over Tomas's letter on the first day.

He did not, for a count, answer.

"Yes," he said.

"Yes what."

"Yes that's the question."

"Mm."

"Ryn."

"Mm."

"I don't know how to do that."

"No."

"I don't know how to get out of the way."

She picked up her cup again. She drank from it. She did not, in the drinking, look at him, because Ryn knew, from the time they were children, that there were counts in which a man did not need to be looked at.

"You'll learn," she said.

"Will I."

"You learned the other thing."

"What other thing."

"The thing in the south block. The thing where you stopped doing it for them and started doing it with them. Tesha. That was the other thing."

"That was Tesha."

"No, Ash. That was you. Tesha asked the question. You did the thing. You'll do this one too. It'll be slower. Slower than you want. You'll do it."

He looked at the bench.

He looked at the letters and the cloth and the cup and the bracket Haran had been working on at the corner of the forge before he had gone up the stair an hour ago, and the bracket was on the cooling rack now, and the rack was the rack, and the bench was the bench, and Ryn was on the stool beside him in the long gold light.

"Mm," he said.

"Mm," she said.

She finished her tea.

She set the cup down. She unwrapped the cloth bundle on the corner of the bench. The bread inside it was the small dark bread her aunt made on Tuesdays, and Ryn broke a piece off and put it in his hand, and broke a piece off for herself, and they ate the bread on the stools at the corner of the bench in the long gold light, and they did not, while they ate, say anything more.

\* \* \*

Maren's note came on the fifth day.

It came not by the boy but by a runner from the Hall, a tall woman in the gray wool coat of the Hall messengers, who climbed the curve from the lower terraces in the middle of the morning and stood at the door of the workshop until Ash came to the door, and held out the note, and waited the count it took for Ash to read the front of the folded sheet, which had his name on it in Maren's hand, and then went back down the curve without saying anything, because the note was not a note that wanted an answer.

He took it inside.

He stood at the bench. Haran was at the forge, working a length of stock at a low heat, with the bellows in his left hand on the slow rhythm of a man holding a temperature, and Haran did not look up when Ash came in, because Haran was holding the temperature and the temperature was the temperature.

Ash unfolded the note.

It was short.

Maren's hand was the hand it was, which was the hand of a woman who had been writing in the Hall for fifteen years, and who wrote the way she had been taught to write at the Hall, which was small, and even, and without flourish, and which made of every page she wrote a kind of ledger. The note was a list. The list was the list of the Ardenmere failures. She had got it, Ash understood, from the Hall's professional channels, which were not Tomas's channels and were not Haran's network, and which were the channels through which the city's senior administrators learned the things the city's senior administrators learned.

The list was eight lines.

*Crop irrigation: failed. Second field lost.*

*Healing house: capacity zero. Worst cases dispatched to Selvern.*

*Communication crystals: silent four days, now five.*

*Heating mains, town quarter: cold.*

*Mill: nonoperational. Grain stocks one week.*

*Bakery, central square: reverted to wood. Output a third of capacity.*

*Public lighting: dark.*

*Channeled water main, lower district: failed. Hand-pumped from the old well.*

He read the list.

He read it the way he had been reading the letters for five days, which was slowly, and which was now the way he read the things on the bench, because slowly was the speed at which the things on the bench gave up what they had to give.

The last line was beneath the list. It was set apart from the list by a small space, the way Maren set a thing apart on the page when she meant the thing to be read after the list and not as part of it.

*This is what it looks like.*

He stood at the bench.

He stood with the note in his hand and the list in front of him and the small space and the last line, and the workshop was the workshop, and Haran was at the forge holding the temperature, and the bellows made the small slow sound the bellows made on the slow rhythm, and the rod in the coals was the deep red-orange that was the temperature Haran was holding.

He read the last line again.

*This is what it looks like.*

Maren had written it the way Maren wrote things. She had written it as a sentence that named a thing and did not decorate it. She had not written *I am afraid*. She had not written *I think of you*. She had not written *what are we going to do*. She had written what she had written, which was four words that did the work of all of those, and which did the work because Maren had, in her life, not been a woman of the four-word sentence, and the four words sat on the page in a way that the longer sentences would not have sat.

He folded the note.

He folded it in half, and in half again, and he held it. He did not, for a count, put it in his coat pocket with the bench paper. He held it in his hand. He looked at the letters on the long bench, in the rows, and he looked at the cloth bundle from Ryn the day before, with its remaining heel of dark bread, and he looked at the cooling rack with Haran's bracket on it, and he looked at the forge.

Haran had not looked up.

Haran was holding the temperature.

Ash put the folded note in his coat pocket. He did not put it in the inside pocket where the bench paper was, with the two lines. He put it in the outside pocket, on the other side, where it would lie against him in the walk down the curve, and he could feel it against his side as he walked, and he could feel that it was there.

He went to the forge.

He stood beside Haran a count without speaking, because Haran was holding the temperature, and the temperature was the temperature, and you did not speak to a man at the bellows on a slow rhythm.

Haran finished the rhythm.

He let the bellows up. He took the rod out of the coals with the tongs and laid it on the anvil and looked at the working length, and the working length was the color it should have been, and Haran nodded the small nod he nodded when a temperature had come out where he had wanted it.

"Mm."

"Maren wrote. She had the list, the, from the Hall."

"Mm."

"She has the list. From the Hall."

"Mm."

"She wrote at the bottom, *this is what it looks like*."

Haran set the rod back in the coals. He took up the bellows again and started the slow rhythm again, because the temperature was a thing that had to be brought back if you wanted to work it again, and Haran was going to work it again.

"Mm," he said.

"Haran."

"Yes."

"I'm not going to Ardenmere."

"No."

"I don't know what I'm going to do. I mean. I'm not, sorry, I don't, "

Haran worked the bellows.

He worked them through three counts of the slow rhythm before he answered, and the answer when it came was the answer of a man who had not stopped working while he answered, which was the way Haran answered things, which was to leave the answer on the bench and go on with the work and let the answer be picked up or not.

"Write to Joren," Haran said.

"Joren. Right, the, the wrap, the, "

"Shen Pass. The wrap. You read Iren's letter. You'll have a question for Joren if you sit with the letter. Write it. I'll send it with the next post."

"I haven't met Joren."

"No."

"He doesn't know me."

"He knows of you."

"What does he know."

Haran worked the bellows. He held the temperature. He looked at the rod in the coals, and the rod was coming back to the orange Haran wanted, and Haran did not, for the count of the bringing back, look at Ash.

"He knows you've been pouring the reservoir into the city for a year and a half," Haran said. "He knows you stopped, in the south block, doing it for them, and started doing it with them. He knows there's a man in the city who built a kettle ring that bleeds, and who is now trying to learn to channel a thread, and who has read his letter today. He knows what Tomas has written, and he knows what Iren has written, and he knows the difference between them."

"Yes."

"Write to him. Ask him a question. The question doesn't have to be the right question. The question has to be a real one. He'll write back."

"And then. And then I, "

"And then there's a letter on the bench. And then there's another. And then there are forty. And then there's a winter where you've thought, with Joren, about what the wool batt does that the linen doesn't, and you've thought, with Curran, about why the second mast cracks at the step, and you've thought, with Beska, about why the wheel held the spring planting and won't, on its own, hold the autumn one. And then it's a year from now and you've not been to Ardenmere, and Ardenmere is what Ardenmere is, and the rest of the country is, in some places, not Ardenmere, and the not-Ardenmere is the thing you have been part of, and is not the thing you have done."

Ash stood at the forge.

He stood with the warmth of the forge on his face and the slow rhythm of the bellows in the air beside him, and the rod in the coals coming back to working orange, and Haran's hand on the bellows handle moving in the rhythm Haran had been moving it in for forty years.

"The catalyst," he said.

"What."

"Nothing. Something I read once."

Haran nodded. He let the bellows up.

He took the rod out of the coals with the tongs. He laid it on the anvil. He worked it. He worked it with the small hammer, in the rhythm of a man who had been hammering this iron at this anvil at this temperature in this rhythm for forty years, and the rhythm was the rhythm, and the iron came under the hammer, and Ash stood at the forge a count, and then went to the bench.

He took a piece of the cheap paper Haran kept for letters from the stack under the lamp.

He sat at the bench.

He took a stub of pencil from the corner. He sat with the paper in front of him and the pencil in his hand, and he did not, for a count, write. He looked at the letters on the long bench, in the rows, and he looked at the cloth from Ryn at the corner, and he looked at the forge and Haran's back at the forge and the slow steady working of the hammer, and he looked at his own hand on the paper, which was the hand he had been pouring the reservoir with for a year and a half, and which was, today, holding a stub of pencil.

He thought about Iren's letter.

He thought about the wool batt and the tarred linen and the three degrees and the third less of the wood ration. He thought about the meeting hall, which they were going to wrap before next winter, if the linen came. He thought about the rest of the houses, which they were going to wrap as the linen came. He thought about *the linen is the part that matters*, and he thought about *the wool alone wets through*, and he thought about how a man came to know that the wool alone wet through, which was by wrapping a house in wool alone and watching the wool wet through, which was the kind of knowing that took a winter.

He thought about how many winters Joren had.

He thought about how many winters anybody had.

He wrote.

He wrote *Joren*, and then he stopped, and then he wrote, *Haran has shown me your letter to Iren. I have a question*. He stopped again. He looked at the question that was not yet a question. He sat with it. He sat with it the way Haran sat with a temperature, which was to wait until the temperature was the temperature, and not before.

The question came.

He wrote it.

He wrote it slowly, in the careful hand he had been writing in since he was a boy at Maren's table doing his lessons, and the question was a question about the linen, and about the way the wool wet through, and about what Joren had tried before he had tried the linen, and what Joren had tried that had not worked, and the question was, when he had finished writing it, three short lines on the cheap paper.

It was not the right question.

It was a real one.

He folded the paper.

He set it at the corner of the bench, in the place where Haran put the letters that were going out with the next post. He set it carefully. He set it with the folded edge facing the wall and the edge that opened facing the room, which was the way Haran set the going-out letters, because the boy who came for the post took the letters from the room side and not from the wall side, and Haran was a man who, having found a way to set a letter, set it that way.

He sat back on the stool.

The forge was warm. Haran was working the rod. The hammer was the rhythm it was. The light through the high window had moved, in the count of the writing, off the long bench and onto the wall above the forge, and the dust above the forge was, in the long gold of the light now, the slow visible dust it was at this hour, and the workshop smelled of iron and oiled wood and the small clean smell of the forge at working temperature.

He took the folded note out of his coat pocket.

He unfolded it on the bench in front of him. He smoothed it with the side of his hand. He read the last line again.

*This is what it looks like.*

He sat with it.

He sat with it the way he had sat, in the cool of the shop the night before last, with the second line on the bench paper. He did not, this time, draw a line. He did not, this time, let the relief come back. He sat with the four words and the small space above them and the list above the small space, and the workshop was the workshop, and Haran was at the forge, and the letter to Joren was at the corner of the bench in the going-out place, and the long gold light was on the wall above the forge.

He folded Maren's note again.

He put it back in his outside pocket, on the side away from the bench paper.

He stood up.

He went to the long bench. He looked at the rows of letters. He looked at Tomas's first letter, which was at the head of the leftmost row, and at Tomas's second letter beside it, and at Tomas's third letter beside that, and at the careful list of the failures and the careful list of the holdings and the line at the bottom of the third letter that said, *This is what we should have done five years ago*.

He looked at Iren's letter, in the middle row, with the wool and the linen and the three degrees.

He looked at Curran's letter, with the new sail and the lads and the second mast cracked at the step.

He looked at Beska's three lines. *We are well. The wheel held the spring planting.*

He stood at the bench a count.

He did not, in the count, channel.

He did not feel for the thread.

He stood with his hand on the edge of the bench and the letters in front of him in their three uneven rows, and he understood, the way a man understood a thing that arrived not as a revelation but as a recognition, that the people who had written the letters were not waiting for him. They had not been waiting for him. They were not, today, waiting for him. They were, today, in their houses and their boats and their fields and their meeting halls, and they were doing the work, and the work was the work they were doing without him, and his name, in their houses, was a name that came up in the way names came up, in the corner of a letter to Haran, on a Tuesday evening, at the lamp.

He went back to the stool.

He sat. He picked up his cold cup. He held it in his hands the way he had held the warm cup three days ago when Haran had told him to sit down and drink the tea.

The forge sang.

Haran had brought the rod back to the temperature and had taken it out and was working it, and the working was the working, and the rhythm was the rhythm, and the workshop, in the long gold light of the late afternoon of the fifth day, was the workshop it had been on the first day and would be tomorrow, and the letters on the long bench were a thing the workshop now contained, and the letter to Joren was at the corner of the bench in the going-out place, and Maren's note was in his outside pocket, and the bench paper with the two lines was in his inside pocket, and the two pockets were on opposite sides of his coat.

He sat with his hands around the cold cup.

He looked at the forge.

He did not, for a long count, do anything else.
