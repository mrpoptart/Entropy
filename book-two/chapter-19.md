# Chapter 19

The broadsheets came in at the workshop on the second morning of the week, three of them, folded the way the boy from the print row folded them, in the small precise quartering he did, and Haran took them at the door and brought them to the long bench and set them in the row at the corner where the letters went.

Ash was at the bench with the cheap paper book.

The book was further on now. The book was on the page that began with *plate seven, breathing,* and went on through *Mira, drain at Building Four, low corner not high,* and *Pell, wrap on plate three replaced, watch for slip,* and the page after, which was the page Tesha had, the day before, named at the end of with the small flat naming, *the man on the upper terrace says the second basin should have a lid.* He had underlined *Tesha* on the page. He had underlined *the man on the upper terrace* on the page under it, and he had not yet, in the morning, gone back to the south block to ask the man what kind of lid.

Haran set the broadsheets down.

"Levelers," he said.

"Mm."

"All three."

"Mm."

Haran went back to the long bench. He had a stack of country letters at the side of the lamp. He had the small paring knife. He set the broadsheets at the corner of the bench, in the row at the going-in place, where a thing was set when a thing was set for a man to come to.

Ash came to the bench.

He set the cheap paper book down. He picked up the first of the broadsheets. The paper was the cheap paper of the print row, soft at the fold, the ink a little loose where the press had run wet, and the masthead was the masthead of the broadsheet that had been, in the spring, the broadsheet that ran the council's notices and which had, in the summer, started running the Levelers' notices, and which now, in the autumn, ran both with the small careful neutrality of a print row that was not going to take a side until the side had won.

The headline was a column headline. *Council Splits On Contribution Statute.*

He read it.

He read it a count.

The Levelers, in the spring, had passed a contribution statute. The statute set a minimum contribution from any channeler with a pool above a stated weight, and the contribution went into the communal grids in the districts the statute named, and the grids had, in the spring, run on the contribution and the elder pools and the small redistributive accounting that the Levelers had, after a year of pushing for it, written into the law. The grids had worked. The article said the grids had worked. The article said, in the careful sentence the print row used when the print row was reporting a thing that was true and a thing that was not enough at the same time, that the contribution statute had, in its first three quarters, *delivered measurable warmth to districts that would otherwise have been cold.*

Then the article said the contribution had thinned.

It said the elder pools had thinned. It said the autumn measurements at the grids the statute fed were running at six tenths of the spring measurements, which was a number, and which was the number a man read twice. It said the council was meeting the next week to vote on three motions, of which the first was to raise the statutory minimum, and the second was to amend the statute to permit the inclusion of mechanical and catalytic systems in the communal grids, and the third was to suspend the statute pending review.

The first motion was Davan's.

The second motion was a councilor named Renat's, of whom Ash had not heard, who had come up in the summer through the heating committee and who, the article said in the sentence that did the small careful work, *has visited several proof-of-concept districts and has called for the integration of building approaches with redistribution.*

The third motion was a councilor named Hesh's, who was the oldest of the Levelers' council members, who had, the article said, *stated in committee that the movement has fought the wrong fight.*

He read it again.

He set the broadsheet down.

He picked up the second.

The second was the broadsheet from the lower quarter, smaller, looser, the column shorter. The headline was *Contribution Grids Weaken; Petitions Up.* The column was a list. It was the list the crier had been crying on the walk down from the south block the week before, the list of districts where the heating had failed entirely and the petitions had gone to the Levelers and the Levelers had not, at the petitions, been able to do the thing the petitions had asked for. The list was longer than it had been the week before. He counted. The list was eleven districts. The week before, in the list the crier had cried, the list had been seven.

He set the second broadsheet down.

He picked up the third.

The third was a single-sheet print, not the regular run, the kind a faction printed when a faction wanted, in a hurry, to put a thing in front of the city. The masthead was a Leveler masthead. The text was a Leveler text. It called, in the plain hard sentences the Levelers used when they were calling for a thing, for the council to pass the first motion. It said the elder pools had not thinned because the magic was thinning. It said the elder pools had thinned because the elder channelers had, under the cover of the thinning, *withheld their contributions in defiance of the statute.* It called for the minimum to be raised. It called for the contribution to be enforced. It was signed at the foot, in the small black block of names, by Davan, and by four of the council members who had come up with Davan, and the signatures were the signatures of men and women who had, in the spring, carried the statute through against everything.

Davan's name was at the head of the block.

The signature was Davan's hand.

Ash set the sheet down.

He stood at the bench a count.

Haran was at the long bench with the country letters. Haran did not, at the bench, look up. Haran read the letter he was reading. Haran set it in the row.

"You read these," Ash said.

"Mm."

"The first one."

"Mm."

"The second."

"Mm. The list."

"The list."

Haran set the letter down. He picked up the paring knife. He turned the paring knife in his fingers a count, the small turning he did when he was about to say a thing.

"Eleven," he said.

"Eleven."

"It was seven."

"Yes."

"It'll be fifteen by the new year."

He did not, at the bench, look up. He set the paring knife down. He picked up the next letter.

Ash stood at the bench.

He looked at the three broadsheets in the row.

He looked at them the way a man looked at a list of things that, taken together, were the shape of a thing he had not, until that morning, had the shape of, and the shape was the shape of a movement that had done what it had said it would do, and which was, in the autumn of the year it had done it, finding that the doing had not been the thing.

He picked up the cheap paper book.

He turned to a fresh page.

He wrote, in the careful hand, *Council split. Three motions. Davan, Renat, Hesh.* He wrote, under that, *eleven districts on the list.* He wrote, under that, *the contribution has thinned.*

He did not, after that, write *I told them.* He did not, after that, write anything.

He set the pencil down.

He looked at the page.

The page was the page it was.

They went to the heating row, the plate, and Tesha's door. They did not go in.* He underlined *did not go in.*

The grey at the high window was the grey of a morning that had not committed to weather, the grey it had been the day before, and the day before that, and the workshop was the workshop, and Haran, at the long bench, read his letters.

He went to the peg.

He took his coat.

He went up to the south block on foot.

He went up the curve and across the lower terrace and into the south block at the gate, and at the gate he did not, this morning, stop and look up the lane. He went up the lane. He went past Building Six and past the chalk mark on the back wall where the second basin would go, which was in now, in chalk, the fuller chalk Tesha put on a line when the line was the line, and he went past the heating row and he saw Pell at the row with his hand on plate seven, and Pell did the small lift of the chin, and he went up to the end of the lane.

The communal plate was warm.

There was no one at the plate. The plate sat warm in its housing and the housing sat in the small attentive cold of a lane in late autumn, and the lane went on past the plate to the upper terrace, and on the upper terrace, at the door of the second-from-last building, two men were standing.

He knew one of them.

The man was a man named Joss. Joss was a grid worker who had been at the warehouse on the lower terrace in the spring and who had, in the summer, been at the heating committee on the council, and who had, the broadsheets had said in the autumn, come down from the heating committee onto the floor with the second of the three motions. Joss was Renat's. Joss had Davan's coat, the heavy gray coat the men who had come up with Davan had worn at the demonstrations in the spring, and he had the coat unbuttoned at the throat the way he wore it, and he was talking, in the low voice he talked in, to the other man.

The other man was Pell.

Pell had come up the lane while Ash had been at the plate. Pell had his arms folded and his head tilted a count, the small tilt he did when he was listening to a thing he had not, until the listening, decided about, and Joss was talking, and Pell was listening, and Joss had a small folded paper in his hand which he did not, while talking, open.

Ash stood at the end of the lane.

He did not go up.

He stood at the end of the lane and he watched, and after a count Joss handed Pell the folded paper, and Pell took it, and Pell turned the folded paper in his hand the way Haran turned a paring knife, and Pell said something Ash could not hear, and Joss said something back, and Joss did the small precise nod a man did when he had said the thing he had come to say, and he turned, and he went down the lane the other way, toward the upper gate.

Pell stood at the door.

He did not, for a count, open the paper.

He put the paper in the inside pocket of his coat.

He turned and he came down the lane.

Ash stepped to the side of the lane at the housing of the communal plate. Pell came down. Pell did not, coming down, slow his pace. Pell came down to the plate and he stopped, and he looked at Ash, and he did the small lift of the chin.

"Morning."

"Morning."

"You see that."

"I saw a man at the door."

"Renat's man."

"Yes."

"He's been up at the upper terrace twice this week."

"Mm."

"He came up to me at the row last week. He stood at plate three. He asked about the wrap. He asked about the noise on plate seven. I told him I didn't know what made the noise. He said he didn't either. He said he'd ask."

"Mm."

"He came back yesterday. He said he'd asked. He said a man at a heating row in the third quarter had a plate that did the same noise, in, out, slow, and the man had taken the plate apart, and the man had found a hairline in the flange where the flange seated. He said it could be a flange. He said he didn't know."

"Mm."

"He brought it to me. He didn't bring it to you."

He looked at Pell.

He had, at the bench, written *plate seven, noise like breathing, slow* under Pell's name, and he had, in the morning at Tesha's door, written *ask Tesha about plate seven, the breathing,* and he had not, in the writing, asked. He had not been to plate seven. He had been to the basin. He had been to the joint. He had been to the broadsheets and the long bench and the high window, and a man named Joss whose name had been in the heating committee in the summer had been to plate seven twice in a week.

"What's in the paper," he said.

"A name. A grid worker in the third quarter who has the flange to spare. Joss said the man would bring it up at the end of the week."

"Mm."

"He said he'd come help take the plate apart."

"Mm."

"He said his hands were a grid worker's hands and his hands were the hands he had."

Ash looked at the plate.

"All right," he said.

"You all right with it."

"Yes."

"Mm."

Pell did not, for a count, say more.

He looked at the plate. He looked up the lane to where Joss had gone.

"He's a Leveler," he said.

"Yes."

"He's not pushing the Leveler thing."

"No."

"He's pushing the flange."

"Yes."

"Mm."

He took his hand from his arm. He folded the arms again, the other way, the way a man folded arms when he had taken a thing in.

"All right," he said.

Ash went to Tesha's.

He came down the lane the way he had come down it the morning before, past the new annex and past the back wall of Building Six, and he came to the door of the converted storage room at the back of Building Four, and the door was open, and the lamp was lit at the bench, and there were three women at the bench, and one of them was not a woman from the south block.

She was older than the women from the south block. She wore the heavy gray coat the men who had come up with Davan had worn at the demonstrations in the spring, the coat Joss had been wearing at the upper terrace, but on her the coat was a coat she had had a long time, and it was patched at the elbow with the small careful patching of a woman who patched her own coat, and the patch was a Leveler patch, the cross-stitch they used in the lower quarter, which was not, in the cross-stitch, a flag, but a thing a person who knew the lower quarter knew.

She had her hands at the bench.

She had a pressure valve in her hands.

Tesha was at her elbow. Tesha was watching the woman's hands. Tesha was, in the small flat voice she used, naming the seat of the valve.

"Here," she was saying, "and here, and you feel the give there, and that's gone, you feel that."

"Mm."

"And that's when you change it."

"Mm."

The woman worked the seat. Her hands were steady. Her hands were the hands of a woman who had worked her hands a long time at things, not at valves, but at things, and the woman's eyes were on her hands the way Tesha's eyes had been on her hands the morning Ash had stood at the doorway and not gone in, and the small thing a person knew when a person was watching a person learn a thing was the thing in the lamp.

He stood at the door.

He did not, for a count, go in.

He stood at the door the way he had stood at the door the morning before, and Tesha did not, for a count, look up, and after the count she said, without looking up, "Morning."

"Morning."

"This is Liesel."

"Morning."

The woman looked up.

She looked at him a count. Her face was a face he had not, until that morning, seen, and the face had the small careful evaluating look of a woman who had spent a life evaluating men in coats at doorways, and after the count the look went, in the small unpracticed way a careful look went when the looker had decided it did not, in the moment, need to be careful, and she did the small short nod of a woman who had, in her life, given a small short nod to many men.

"Ash."

"I know."

"Mm."

"Tesha said you'd come."

"Mm."

He came down the slope to the lower lane and he was at the gate at the end of the south block when he saw Davan.

Davan was on the other side of the lower terrace.

He was at the corner of the small public square at the foot of the curve, the square where the bell hung that the Levelers had, in the spring, rung at the passing of the contribution statute, and which, in the autumn, the city did not, on most days, ring. The square was empty. The light at the square was the colored grey of a city evening with the cooking fires lit, the light it had been the evening Ash had walked back through the district and the lamps had been on at the doors, and the square was empty except for Davan.

Davan was alone.

He had no aide. He had no one with him from the council. He was not in the heavy gray coat. He was in a plainer coat, a dark coat the color of wet stone, and his hair was longer than it had been in the spring and there was a grey in it at the temples Ash had not, until that count, seen, and he was standing at the edge of the lower terrace where the terrace looked down the slope at the south block, and he was looking at the south block.

He was looking at the chalked back wall of Building Six.

He was looking at the upper terrace where the basin was.

Ash stopped at the gate.

He did not, for a count, go through.

He stood at the gate and he looked across the lower terrace at Davan, and Davan did not, in the looking, see him, because Davan was looking at the south block, and a man at a gate was a small thing to a man looking at a south block.

He had not seen Davan since the summer.

He had not seen him, in the flesh, since the day at the warehouse on the lower terrace when the Levelers had come for the meeting and Davan had stood at the head of the long table and made the case, in the plain hard sentences he made cases in, for the contribution statute, and Ash had sat at the side of the table and listened, and the case had been a good case, and the case had been a case that did not, even then, account for the thing the broadsheets were now reporting.

He had heard Davan. In the summer he had heard him at a distance, on the curve, calling a question across a square. He had read his name in the broadsheets. He had read his signature at the foot of the single sheet that had come in that morning. He had not, since the summer, seen the man's face.

The man's face was a face he had to look at a count to read.

Davan had lost weight. The line of the jaw was sharper than it had been in the spring. The mouth was the same mouth. The eyes were the same eyes. There was, around the mouth, a small set Ash had not, in the spring, seen, the small set of a man who had been holding a thing in his teeth a long time and had not, in the holding, found a place to put it down.

Davan walked down the slope to the lower lane.

He walked along the lower lane to the gate at the end of the south block.

He went through the gate.

He looked at the heating row down the slope, where Pell had finished at plate three and had gone home, and where the row ran in the small sound a row made when it was running. He looked at the communal plate at the end of the lane. The plate sat warm. There was no one at the plate. There was, at the end of the upper terrace, a man at his door who had stepped out to take in a length of washing, and the man saw Davan, and the man, in the small unsurprised way a man took in a thing he had not expected, took in the washing and did not, at the door, say anything, and went in, and shut the door.

Davan did not, at the basin, move.

Ash came up the lane.

He came up to the upper terrace.

He stopped at a pace from Davan.

Davan did not, for a count, turn.

He turned.

He turned slowly. He turned with the slow turning of a man who had known, in the count, that the man at his shoulder was the man at his shoulder, and who had taken, in the count, the small interval he needed to be ready for the turning. His face was the face Ash had seen at the corner of the square, the face with the set at the mouth, and his eyes were the eyes at the temples with the grey, and he looked at Ash, and Ash looked at him.

Ash did not, for a count, speak.

Davan looked at the basin.

Davan looked at the heating row.

Davan looked at the communal plate at the end of the lane.

Davan looked at the chalk line through the old basin on the wall above the inside stair.

He looked at Ash.

"This isn't what I built," he said.

"No," Ash said. "It's what they built."

Davan did not, for a count, answer.

He looked at the basin.

He looked at the basin a count.

He nodded once, the small short nod of a man who had received a thing he did not want to receive, and he turned, and he walked away.

He walked down the slope past the heating row, past the new annex at the back of Building Four, past the door of Tesha's workshop where the lamp was lit at the bench and the door was open. He did not, at Tesha's door, stop. He did not, at the door, look in. He walked down the lane and out through the gate at the end of the south block, and he did not, walking, look back.

Ash stood at the upper terrace.

He stood at the upper terrace a count.

He looked at the basin.

The basin sat full. The water sat still. The line came in at the side. The bracket held. At the door of the second-from-last building, the man had gone in and shut the door, and the lane was the lane, and the heating row ran, and the communal plate sat warm at the end of the lane, and somewhere up the slope a child was being called in for the second time.

He stood at the upper terrace.

He did not, for a count, go down.

He had, when he had walked up the lane the first time with no paper under his arm and a stub of pencil in his pocket, thought he was at the beginning of a thing. He had been at the beginning of a thing. The thing was further on now. The thing was at the upper terrace with a basin on a bracket and a line that came in at the side, fitted by a woman from the lower quarter who had done a hundred fittings on a contribution grid and who had, that afternoon, done one on a building. The thing was at the heating row with a flange that a grid worker in the third quarter would bring up at the end of the week. The thing was at Tesha's door where Tesha had, at the bench, said the small flat *try the give again* to a woman who patched her own coat.

The thing was working.

He stood with that.

He stood with it and he stood with the small thing under it that he had not, until the count at the corner of the square, looked at, which was the man in the dark coat the color of wet stone with the grey at the temples and the set at the mouth, who had stood at the corner of a square at a bell that the city did not, on most days, ring, and had looked across at a south block, and had walked across, and had stood at the basin, and had said the thing he had said.

*This isn't what I built.*

It wasn't.

It was the thing the man had built the ground for. It was not the thing the man had built. The man had built a movement that had won council seats and passed a contribution statute and warmed people who had been cold, and the movement had done the thing the movement had said it would do, and the doing had not been the thing, and the man at the corner of the square had been honest enough to come down across the lower terrace and through the gate and up the lane to look at the thing the doing had not been, and proud enough to walk away from it without going through Tesha's door.

Ash did not, for the count, go down.

He stood at the upper terrace with his hands in his coat pockets, with the cheap paper book in the inside pocket against Maren's note and the bench paper and Joren's letter, and he did not, in the standing, feel the thing he had thought he might, before this autumn, have felt at a man like Davan walking away from a thing like this. He had thought he might, before this autumn, have felt the small mean lift a man felt when a man who had been wrong about a thing came to the place where the thing the man had been wrong about was working. He did not feel it. What he felt instead was the small heavy steady thing of a man watching another man, who had fought harder than he had ever fought and given up more than he had ever given up, learn at a basin on a bracket on an upper terrace, on a grey evening in late autumn, that the fight he had given up the things for had not been, at the end, the whole fight.

Davan would not, Ash thought, come back.

He thought it, and he thought it without the lift, and he thought it with the small steady weight, and he stood at the upper terrace a count, and he went down.

Tesha was at the door of the workshop when he came down.

She was standing at the door with the rag at her belt and the lamp warm at the bench inside, and Liesel was at the bench, and Pell was at the bench with Liesel, and the three of them had a length of pipe and a fitting and a small heap of waxed cord at the bench between them, and the three of them were not, at the bench, talking.

Tesha looked at him.

"That was Davan," she said.

"Yes."

"He came up to the basin."

"Yes."

"He didn't come in."

"No."

"Mm."

She did not, for a count, say more.

She looked up the lane.

"Did he say anything."

"He said it wasn't what he'd built."

"Mm."

"I said it was what they'd built."

She looked at him.

She looked at him a count, the steady evaluating look.

"All right," she said.

She turned back to the door.

She stepped in.

He stood at the door a count.

Liesel was at the bench with the fitting in her hands. Pell was at her elbow. Tesha came to the bench. The three of them did not, at the bench, say anything for a count, and then Tesha said, in the flat voice, "Show me where you'd put the seal."

"Here."

"Mm."

"And here."

"Mm."

"And you feel the give there."

"Mm."

He stepped back from the door.

He went down the slope to the lower lane, and out through the gate at the end of the south block, and he did not, walking, take Maren's note out of his coat. He did not take the book out. The inside pocket sat warm against his ribs, and the outside pocket on the other side was empty, and the gate was at his back.

He went down the curve.

He came in at the workshop.

The forge was banked. The lamp was at the corner. Haran was at the long bench with the letters and the small paring knife, and he had the broadsheets at the corner of the bench in the row at the going-in place, the three of them, the way he had set them in the morning.

Haran looked up.

"South block."

"Mm."

"Davan."

He looked at Haran.

Haran did not, at the bench, look back. Haran turned the paring knife in his fingers a count, the small turning he did, and he did not look up.

"How did you know."

"The boy from the print row came up the curve. He'd been at the lower terrace at the bell."

"Mm."

"He saw a man at the corner of the square."

"Mm."

Haran set the paring knife down.

"You spoke to him."

"At the basin. He spoke. I spoke. He walked."

"Mm."

"He won't come back."

Haran looked at him then.

He looked at him the way Haran looked at a thing, which was the small unhurried unsentimental look of a man who had read a country letter and had set it in the row and had not, in the setting, said the thing the letter said.

"So," Haran said.

"Mm."

"That's the start of his."

"Yes."

"And the start of theirs."

"Yes."

"Mm."

Haran picked up the paring knife. He turned it. He set it down.

"You all right."

He thought about it.

He thought about the walk down from the south block with the inside pocket warm against his ribs and the outside pocket empty. He thought about Maren's note in the inside pocket with the bench paper and Joren's letter. He thought about Davan at the corner of the square, the set at the mouth, the grey at the temples. He thought about Liesel at the bench with the fitting in her hands and the cross-stitch patch at the elbow of her coat. He thought about Tesha at the door of the workshop saying *that was Davan* the way she said a thing.

He thought about the small heavy steady thing he had felt at the upper terrace.

"No," he said.

"Mm."

"I'm not."

"Mm."

Haran did not, for a count, say more.

He picked up the paring knife. He picked up the next country letter. He cut the seal. He read it. He set it in the row.

The forge ticked.

Ash hung his coat on the peg.

He went to the bench.

He took the cheap paper book out. He set it on the bench. He sat at the stool. He opened it to the page after the page with the three motions and the eleven districts and the *Renat's people in the south block. A woman and a man.* He turned the page.

He wrote, in the careful hand, *Liesel. Lower quarter. Burr Street grid. A hundred fittings on a contribution line. Did the upper end on the second basin. Will come back to learn the catalytic side. Patches her own coat.* He underlined *Liesel*.

He wrote, under that, *Joss. Renat's. Plate seven. Flange end of the week. Brings it up himself.*

He wrote, under that, *Davan at the corner of the square.* He did not underline the name.

He wrote, under that, on its own line, *It's what they built.*

He looked at the page.

He sat with it.

He set the pencil down.

The forge ticked. Haran read his letters. The grey at the high window had gone, in the count he had been at the bench, to the grey of an evening in late autumn that had not, in the day, committed to weather, and the workshop was the workshop.

He sat at the stool a count.

He took the cheap paper book in his hands, and he closed it, and he put it in the inside pocket of his coat at the peg, against Maren's note and the bench paper and Joren's letter, and he came back to the bench, and he warmed the kettle, and he poured a cup for Haran, and he poured a cup for himself, and he set Haran's at the iron plate at the side of the lamp.

He sat at the stool with his cup in both hands.

He did not, for a count, speak.

Haran did not, for a count, speak.

The forge ticked. The lamp burned. The country letter Haran had set in the row sat in the row, and the broadsheets at the corner of the bench sat at the corner, and the workshop was the workshop, and outside, in the city, the light went grey, and the lamps came on at the doors of the south block, and a child had had her hand on a warm plate, and a woman from the lower quarter had her hands on a fitting at a bench in a converted storage room, and a man in a dark coat the color of wet stone had walked out through a gate without looking back.

Ash drank his tea.

The cup was warm in his hands.

The inside pocket of his coat at the peg held what it held.

The outside pocket was empty.

That was the thing.
