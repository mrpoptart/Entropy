# Chapter 17

The shop was cold and the forge was banked and the only light was the grey at the high window, and Ash stood at the bench with his palm a finger's width from the iron of the small offcut he had laid there and tried to find the thread.

He had come down the curve in the dark. He had come down the way he had been coming down for six mornings, which was the way a man came down a stair when he wanted to be at the bench before the bench was anyone else's, and he had unbarred the door and laid his coat on the peg and laid the offcut on the bench and stood over it. The offcut was a piece of mild stock the length of his hand. He had chosen it because it was a piece that did not matter. He did not want, on the mornings, to spoil work.

He set his palm above it.

He breathed.

He had been doing the thing Haran did, or trying to, for six mornings now, and the trying had a shape, and the shape was the shape it was. The wide loose press came easy. The wide loose press had been coming easy for a year and a half. It came at his palm the way water came at a tap, and it warmed the iron, and the iron warmed, and the warming was the warming of a thing through a fog. The fog was the waste. He had not known, until Haran, that the fog was the waste. He had thought the fog was the channeling.

He narrowed.

He narrowed the way Haran had shown him in the autumn at the rod, which was not a clenching but a quieting, and he tried to find, under the quieting, the thin line of the thing itself. Some mornings he found a hint of it. Some mornings he did not. This morning he did not. He narrowed and the offcut went cold under his hand and there was nothing, and he held it a count, and he held it another count, and he pulled back.

The offcut had not warmed.

He had spent, in the holding, perhaps the smallest fraction of the smallest fraction of what he would have spent on the wide loose press. He had also warmed nothing. He had also, at the back of the trying, felt the small clean ache he had been feeling for six mornings at the back of the trying, which was the ache of a man asking a muscle to do a thing the muscle had not been asked to do.

He set his palm down again.

He widened, a little.

This time something caught. It caught for the count of a breath, maybe two, and the iron warmed under his hand in a way that was not the fog and was not the nothing, and was a third thing, and the third thing was the thing he was trying for, and he held it, and as soon as he understood he was holding it the holding broke, and the fog came back, and the iron went on warming the way iron warmed under his hand, which was the way it had warmed for a year and a half.

He pulled back.

He stood with his hand at his side.

The offcut sat on the bench, warm now in the wide loose way, and the warmth would be gone in a count, and the morning would go on, and he had had, for the count of two breaths, the thing.

A third less waste, on the best of these mornings. He had been measuring it the way a man measured a thing he could not measure, which was by feel, and by the slow unwilling math at the back of the feel, and the math said a third, on the best ones. On most ones it said nothing. On some it said worse than nothing, because he had narrowed too far and the channel had not opened and he had spent the small spending of the trying and got no work at all, and the no-work was a waste of a different kind.

He stood at the bench.

He looked at the offcut.

He picked up the offcut in his bare hand. It was the temperature of a thing that had been warmed and was now cooling, which was the temperature of every offcut on every bench in the shop at this hour, and there was nothing in his hand that told him whether the morning had been a good morning or a bad one, and he set it back on the bench in the place he had taken it from.

He went to the stove.

He warmed the kettle.

He warmed it the way he had been warming it for a year and a half, because the kettle was not the morning's work. The kettle was the kettle. The ring under his palm warmed. The kettle warmed. He did not feel for the thread. He had decided, on the second morning, that he would not feel for the thread on the kettle, because the kettle was warm tea for Haran when Haran came down, and the kettle was not a thing he was willing to spoil while he learned.

He poured.

He drank standing, at the stove, with the cup in both hands, and the warm came up off the cup, and he watched the grey at the high window go a degree paler, and he listened for Haran on the stair.

Haran came down at the hour Haran came down.

\* \* \*

The forge took an hour to come back, and Ash worked the bellows on the slow rhythm while Haran sorted post, and the post was thinner now than it had been in the days after Tomas, but it was not nothing. There were two letters. Haran read them standing. He set one in the answer-first pile and one in the answer-when-time pile, and he did not, this morning, lay either out on the long bench. The letters on the long bench from the week before were still in their three uneven rows. They had become a thing the bench contained, the way a vise was a thing the bench contained, and Ash had stopped, in the morning, expecting them to be cleared.

"Mm," Haran said.

"Mm."

"Joren wrote back."

Ash set the bellows handle down.

He set it down too fast and the rhythm broke, and Haran did not look up, and Ash put his hand on the handle again and brought it back to the rhythm, because the temperature was the temperature.

"What does he say. I mean, sorry, what, "

"He says he'd been waiting for the question."

"The question. The one I, "

"The one you wrote."

"What does he say about it. I mean, beyond, yeah."

Haran set Joren's letter on the bench. He smoothed it with the heel of his hand. He took up the bellows on the rhythm Ash had broken and brought it back inside two counts to the rhythm it had been, because Haran was a man who, when a rhythm broke, did not stop to mention that it had broken.

"Read it after," he said. "When the rod's out."

Ash worked the bellows.

He worked them through the bringing-up, and through the holding, and through the working, and he did not, while he worked, look at the letter on the bench. He looked at the rod. He looked at Haran's hand at the tongs. He listened to the small clean sound of the small hammer on the working length, and the rhythm was the rhythm.

When the rod was on the cooling rack and Haran had gone to the stove for his cup, Ash went to the bench.

He read the letter.

Joren's hand was a slow hand, and a small one, and the letter was short, and the short letter was the letter of a man who did not, on the page, waste a word. Joren had written about the linen. He had written about three winters of wool that wet through. He had written about a fourth winter where he had thought of the linen and had not had the linen, and a fifth winter where he had had the linen and had not had enough of it, and a sixth winter where he had had enough, and the wrap had held. He had written about the houses he had wrapped first, which had been the wrong houses, because the wrong houses were the houses of the men who would let a stranger wrap their houses, and the wrong houses were not the houses where the wrap most needed to be. He had written that he had not, in six winters, wrapped a single house with a channel, because the channel was a thing nobody in Shen Pass had had to spare, and the wrap had been built for a place that did not have a channel to spare.

At the bottom of the letter Joren had written, *Your question was a real one. I will answer the rest when you have the next.*

Ash sat with the letter on his knee.

He sat with it a count.

He did not feel, sitting with it, the thing he had felt in the days after Tomas, which was the thing where his hands had wanted to be doing and had not been doing. He felt instead a thing he had not yet named, which was the small steady pressure of a question that was waiting on him for the next question, and the next question had not yet come.

He folded the letter.

He set it in his inside pocket, with the bench paper. He did not, in the setting, take the bench paper out. He had not, since Maren's note, taken the bench paper out. The two pieces of folded paper sat against each other in the inside pocket, and the outside pocket on the other side of his coat held Maren's *this is what it looks like*, and the coat had a weight to it now in both pockets, and the weight was the weight he had been carrying down the curve in the morning and up the curve in the evening for six days.

He went back to the bellows.

\* \* \*

He went to the south block in the afternoon.

He went the way he had been going for two months, which was on foot, with the small canvas bag of the small tools he had been bringing, and without a plan, because Ryn had said, in the autumn, that he should stop coming with plans, and he had been trying, since the autumn, to come without one. The walk down through the lower terraces was the walk it was. The light through the streets was the light of an afternoon that had not committed to weather. Somewhere a vendor was calling the price of pickled greens, and the call came up the lane the way calls came up lanes, which was as a sound that had no particular meaning until a man was hungry, and Ash was not hungry.

He came into the south block at the corner where the old press had been.

The press was running. He could hear it before he could see it, which was the new thing, because for a year and a half the press had not run without him standing at it, and now the press ran in the afternoons whether he was there or whether he was not, and he could hear it from the corner and he could tell, from the rhythm of it, that Pell was on the lever and not Mira, because Pell took the lever a count slower than Mira and the slower count made the small change in the sound of the wheel that a man could hear from the corner if a man had been listening to the press for a year and a half.

He went past the press.

He did not stop at the press. He had stopped going first to the press in the autumn, because Tesha had said, in the way Tesha said things, that he should stop, and he had stopped, and the not-stopping at the press had been part of the not-coming-with-a-plan, and the press now was Pell's and Mira's and a young woman named Sera's, and the press was the press, and Ash walked past.

He went to the heating row.

The heating row was the run of small dwellings at the north edge of the block where he had set the elements in the spring, which were the small mechanical resistance elements he had built to the design he had worked out with Haran, and which the district crew had been maintaining since the autumn when he had handed the maintenance over. The elements were not channeled. The elements were heated by a small wound coil running on a current that came off a wind catch on the roof above the row, which was a thing the crew had built themselves, with Ash standing back, and which had been running since the autumn, and which had failed, this morning, in the second dwelling.

He had come because Tesha had sent word.

Tesha was at the second dwelling.

She was on her knees at the wall plate, with the cover off, and Mira was beside her with the small lamp, and a man Ash did not know was at the door with a basket of tools that had come from the carpenters' shed at the corner. They were looking at the plate. They were not looking at Ash.

"Tesha," Ash said.

"Mm."

"You sent word."

"I did."

She did not look up. She had the small driver in her hand and she was working a screw at the bottom of the plate, and the screw was being slow, and she was being slow with it, because Tesha had learned, in the autumn, that working a screw fast was the way a man stripped a screw, and she had stopped working screws fast.

"Tell me. I mean, sorry, when you can. Tell me."

She finished the screw. She set it on the cloth at her knee. She sat back on her heels.

"Element's cold," she said. "Was warm last night. Was cold this morning. Mira pulled the cover. The coil's intact. The wire from the catch is intact. The plate's the plate."

"And."

"And we don't know."

Ash crouched beside her.

He did not put his hand on the plate. He looked at the coil. The coil was the coil he had wound in the spring, and the coil was wound the way it had been wound, and the wire from the catch came in at the lower terminal the way it had been coming in. He looked at the upper terminal. The upper terminal had a small dark spot at the post where the wire wrapped, and the small dark spot was the small dark spot of a thing that had been hot and was no longer hot.

He did not say it.

He waited.

Tesha looked at the plate. Mira held the lamp closer. The man at the door, whose name Ash did not know, came two steps into the room with the basket and set the basket down where Tesha could reach it, and Tesha said, "Pell's brother," without looking up, which was the way Tesha introduced people in the south block now, which was as if a man had been there all along.

"Pell's brother. Right. Hi."

"Yeh," the man said.

Tesha tapped the lower terminal with the back of the driver.

"It's the coil," she said.

"Mm," Ash said.

He did not say it was not the coil.

She unscrewed the lower terminal. She lifted the wire off. She drew the coil out of its slot, slowly, because the slot was tight and Tesha had learned not to bend the coil at the slot, and she laid the coil on the cloth beside the screw. She turned it over. She looked at it. She held it under Mira's lamp. The coil was the coil it had been when he had wound it, which was a coil with no break in it, and Tesha looked at it a long count, and Tesha said, "Hm."

"Hm," Mira said.

"It's not the coil."

"No. Yeah. No, it isn't."

She set the coil back in the slot. She put the wire back on the lower terminal. She tightened the screw, slowly. She sat back on her heels again.

Ash put his hands on his knees.

He did not put them on the plate. He did not put them near the upper terminal. He looked at the upper terminal as if he were looking at the coil, because if he looked at the upper terminal Tesha would look where he was looking, and Tesha was, this afternoon, looking where she was looking, and where she was looking was not yet the upper terminal, and Ash had been told, in the autumn, that he had to stop standing at people's elbows pointing at the answer.

He had, in the autumn, agreed to stop.

He did not, this afternoon, point.

Tesha tapped the lower terminal again with the back of the driver. She looked at the wire. She traced the wire with her finger from the lower terminal back along the run of it to where it came through the wall, and she went out the door, and Mira followed with the lamp, and they were gone a count, and Ash sat on his heels in the small dwelling with Pell's brother at the door, and Pell's brother said nothing, because Pell's brother did not yet know him.

The voice in him said it, then.

It said, *the upper terminal, Tesha*. It said, *I can see it from here. I could put my hand on it and have it warm by the time you came back from the wall*. It said, *I could fix the coil, if it's the coil. I could fix the wire. I could fix every plate on the heating row in an hour. I could go through the whole block before evening*.

It said, after that, the other thing.

It said, *and you could stop doing this. You could narrow on the trying in the morning. You could keep the thing for the things that matter. Tesha will learn the upper terminal in another month, or another year, and the world is not in another month, and you have, in your hand, the answer to today*.

He put his hands flat on his knees.

He pressed his palms against his knees the way a man pressed his palms against his knees when his palms were a thing he did not want to use.

He waited.

Tesha came back in.

She had a length of the wire in her hand that she had cut from the run between the wall and the catch, and she had a small frown on her face that was the frown she made when a thing did not match what she had expected, and she said, "Wire's good. Whole length. Tight at the catch."

"Mm," Ash said.

"So it's the plate."

"Mm. Yeah. Has to be."

She looked at the plate.

She looked at it the way she had looked at it for a count when she had first taken the cover off, which was the look of a woman taking in a thing whole. Then she looked at it the way she had not, until now, looked at it, which was piece by piece. She started at the lower terminal. She looked at the screw. She looked at the wrap of the wire on the post. She looked at the post. She moved her eyes up the coil. She moved them past the coil to the upper terminal.

She stopped.

"Mira."

"Mm."

"Bring the lamp back."

Mira brought the lamp.

Tesha leaned in. She looked at the dark spot on the upper post. She put the tip of the driver against it, very lightly, and she looked at it, and she said, "Huh."

"Huh," Mira said.

"It's the upper post. It's burned at the wrap."

"Burned."

"Look. There. The wire's gone black at the contact. There's a gap. That's the gap."

"That's the gap."

"That's why it's cold. The contact's burned through. The current's not making the coil."

She sat back.

She wiped her forehead with the back of her wrist. She looked at Ash.

"You knew," she said.

He took a count.

"I saw it," he said.

"When."

"When I came in."

"And you didn't say."

"No."

She looked at him a count.

She did not, in the count, smile, because Tesha did not smile at this kind of thing, and she did not, in the count, frown either, because Tesha had stopped frowning at this kind of thing in the autumn. She looked at him the way she looked at a coil she was deciding about, which was without pressure, and with the small careful attention she gave a thing she was figuring out.

"Took us an afternoon," she said.

"Yes. Yeah, it did."

"Would have taken you twenty minutes."

"Yes. I mean. Yeah."

"And next time."

"Next time will be faster."

"Mm," Tesha said. "And the time after."

"Faster."

"Mm."

She turned back to the plate.

"Pell's brother," she said. "Hand me the small file. The one with the wood on the handle."

Pell's brother handed her the small file with the wood on the handle. Tesha set to the upper post. She filed the burn off the post, slowly, in the way she had learned to do small careful work, and Mira held the lamp, and Ash sat back on his heels and watched, and the watching was the watching it was, which was the watching of a man holding his hands flat on his knees while another person did, slowly, a thing he could have done fast.

He did not channel.

He did not feel for the thread.

He sat with his palms on his knees and the small dwelling in the late light through the window, and the file went through the burn on the post in the small clean rhythm Tesha had been learning, and the rhythm was a slow rhythm, and the rhythm was the rhythm.

The pull was real.

It sat in him the way the bench paper sat in his inside pocket, which was without speaking, which was without going away, which was the kind of weight a man learned to walk under. He did not name it. He did not, for the count of the filing, think the words *fix and preserve*, and he did not think the words *stay extraordinary*, and he did not think the words *stay safe*. He thought, instead, about the file, and the burn, and the post, and the next time, which would be faster, and the time after, which would be faster still.

Tesha wrapped the wire on the cleaned post. She tightened the screw. She put the cover back on the plate.

"Mira," she said. "Try it."

Mira tried it.

The element ticked. The coil began to warm. The plate, under their hands, came up to the small steady warm of a plate that was working again, and Tesha sat back on her heels and looked at it, and she looked at Ash, and she said, "Huh."

"Huh," Ash said.

"Took an afternoon."

"Yes."

"We'll know it next time."

"Yes. Yeah, you will."

She stood up.

She brushed her knees. She picked up the cloth with the screw, and the small file went into the basket Pell's brother held, and Pell's brother carried the basket out the door, and Mira took the lamp, and the small dwelling was, for a count, only Tesha and Ash, and the warm coming up off the plate.

"Ash."

"Mm."

"Don't, "

She stopped.

She looked at him.

"Don't, "

"I won't," he said.

She did not say what she had been going to ask him not to do, and he did not ask, because he knew, and she knew that he knew, and Tesha did not say things twice when she did not need to.

"Tea at the corner," she said. "Pell's making it."

"In a count," Ash said.

She went out.

He sat in the small dwelling alone.

He sat with his hands on his knees. The plate was warm. The wind catch on the roof was a sound he could hear at the edge of hearing, in the small wooden hum it made when the wind was steady, and the wind was, this afternoon, steady. The element ticked once, the small settling tick of a wire coming up to working temperature, and the tick was the tick of a thing he had not made tick.

He stood up.

He went out.

\* \* \*

He took the long way home.

He took it without deciding to take it. He had come out of the south block at the press corner with the cup of tea Pell had given him still warm in his hand, and Pell had not said much, and Pell never said much, and Pell had nodded at him the way Pell nodded, and Ash had come out of the corner and turned, at the lane, the way he did not usually turn, and the turning was the long way home.

He went past the Levelers' grid.

The grid ran along the lower terrace at the back of the warehouse row, in the line of the old wall, and the grid was the communal heating grid the Levelers had put in two summers ago and had been tending since, in the way the Levelers tended things, which was as a duty rota with names on a list at a door. The grid was warm. He could feel it from the lane. The warmth came up through the cobbles in the way the grid's warmth came up through the cobbles, which was not a heat but a not-cold, and a man walking past in the wrong shoes felt the not-cold through his soles and did not, until he had gone past, know that he had felt it.

He went past.

He did not stop at the grid.

He went up the curve into the elder districts. The elder districts were warm in the way the elder districts had always been warm, which was through the lattice of their channeled mains, and the mains were running the way the mains ran when the channelers in the district were the channelers the district had had for forty years, and the lamps at the corners were lit, and the windows were lit, and the streets had the small steady sound of streets where the heat held and the lamps held and the work of the holding had been done, this evening as every evening, by men and women who had been doing it long enough that the doing was no longer the question.

He went up further.

He came into the young districts at the top of the second terrace.

The young districts had been the young districts since he was a boy, which meant the young districts had been the young districts long enough to be called the young districts by men who had been calling them that for forty years, but the young districts now were not the young districts of his boyhood. The young districts of his boyhood had been bright. The young districts of this evening were not bright. The lamps at every other corner were unlit. The windows at the upper floors were dark. The wind came along the lanes the way the wind came along lanes that were not warmed by mains, which was as wind, and the streets were colder than they should have been at this hour, and there were people in the streets in coats that were not enough.

He went through.

He did not stop. He did not, walking through, channel. He did not, walking through, feel for the thread. He walked the lanes the way a man walked lanes, which was at the pace of a man walking, and the lanes were the lanes they were, and the cold was the cold it was, and the lamps that were not lit were not lit because the channelers in the young districts were not strong enough to hold the lamps and the mains and the heat, and they had been making, in the young districts, the choices a man made when a man could not hold all three.

He came out at the upper gate.

He stood at the upper gate a count.

The hill country was dark in the way the country at evening was dark, which was without lamps, because the country at evening had not, in his lifetime, had channeled lamps to lose, and the country was dark the way the country had always been dark. Somewhere out there was Shen Pass. Somewhere out there was the meeting hall Joren and Iren had not yet wrapped. Somewhere out there was a house wrapped in sheep-wool batt and tarred linen, three degrees warmer than it had been last winter, and a third less of the wood ration, and a man named Joren who had spent six winters figuring out that the wool wet through alone and that the linen was the part that mattered.

Joren had been doing it without him.

Joren had been doing it without anyone like him.

He put his hand in his coat pocket.

He felt the bench paper in the inside pocket. He felt Joren's letter beside it. He felt, in the outside pocket on the other side, Maren's note. He stood at the gate with his hand on the bench paper through the cloth of the coat and the wind coming off the country, and he did not, for a count, take any of it out.

He turned.

He went back down the curve.

He came into the workshop at the hour Haran was banking the forge.

Haran was at the bellows on the slow rhythm a man worked when he was bringing a forge down, which was the rhythm that let the heat go out the way a man wanted heat to go out, which was slowly, and Ash hung his coat on the peg without taking the papers out of the pockets, and he went to the stove. The kettle was on the iron plate at the side, warm. He poured. He brought a cup to Haran at the forge. He set it on the small ledge beside the bellows where Haran kept his cup at this hour.

"Mm," Haran said.

"Mm."

He went back to the stove and poured his own. He came to the bench. He sat at the corner stool.

He drank the tea slowly.

He did not, for a count, speak. Haran banked the forge. Haran set the bellows handle in the position Haran set it in at this hour. Haran came to the bench and took up his cup and stood at the bench with the cup in both hands, in the way Haran stood at his bench at the end of a day, which was without sitting, because Haran did not sit at his bench at the end of a day until he had drunk the first half of his cup standing.

"South block," Haran said.

"Mm. Yeah. The heating row."

"Heating element."

"Yes."

"They got it."

"Tesha got it. I mean. She, yeah. She did."

"Mm."

"Took an afternoon."

"Mm."

He drank.

Haran drank.

The forge ticked, in the small ticking of a forge that was cooling, and the shop was warm with the held warmth of the day, and the long bench with the rows of letters was in the half-light, and Joren's letter was in Ash's inside pocket against the bench paper, and Ash sat at the stool and looked at his cup.

"I almost did it," he said.

Haran did not look at him.

"Mm."

"At the plate. I saw it from the door. The upper post. I almost said it. I almost put my hand on it."

"Mm."

"And I almost, "

He stopped.

He did not say the other thing. He did not say *I almost, in the morning, started narrowing for a different reason*. He did not say *I almost started keeping it*. He did not say the words he had been saying to himself, in his own head, for six mornings, and which he had not, until tonight, said in the hearing of another man.

Haran drank.

Haran set the cup down on the bench.

He did not, for a count, say anything. He looked at the forge. He looked at the cooling rack with the bracket on it. He looked at the long bench with the letters.

"Mm," he said.

"Haran."

"Yes."

"Did you, "

He stopped again.

He had been about to ask Haran a question and he did not, with the question forming, want to ask the question, because the question was the question of a man wanting the answer to be one thing, and the answer might not be the thing, and the asking of the question was a thing he did not want, this evening, to do.

Haran waited.

Haran was a man who waited.

Ash set the cup down.

"Did you ever," he said, "think about keeping it. For yourself. The thing. The narrowing."

Haran looked at him.

He looked at him for a count, in the way he looked at a piece of iron at the wrong temperature, which was without unkindness and without hurry, and Ash sat with his hands on his knees and let the looking be the looking it was.

"Keeping it," Haran said.

"Holding it. Not spending. Letting the technique stretch what was there. Going slow. Going slow enough to last."

Haran nodded the small nod he nodded when he had taken in a thing.

"I thought about it," he said.

"And. I mean. Yeah. And."

"And I wasn't a man who had a lot to spend. I had what I had. The narrowing wasn't keeping. The narrowing was what let me get the rod up to working temperature in a forge that wouldn't bring it on the wide press. It was the bench. The bench was the point. The narrowing was for the bench."

"For the bench."

"For the work I wanted to do. If I'd had it for keeping I wouldn't have learned it. There was nothing in keeping. Keeping was a man at a stove with a kettle warming his palms. I wanted the rod."

Ash sat with it.

He sat with it a count.

He sat with the words *the narrowing was for the bench*, and he sat with the small clean shape of what Haran had said, which was not a thing Haran had said for him, and was a thing Haran had said because Ash had asked, and the saying of it was the saying it was, which was without weight beyond the weight the words carried on their own.

"Mm," Ash said.

"Mm."

Haran picked up his cup. He drank the second half of his cup standing. He set the cup down on the bench, at the place he set the cup down at this hour, and he turned, and he went up the stair the way Haran went up the stair, which was without saying goodnight, because Haran was a man who, having ended a day at the bench, did not require a goodnight to end it.

Ash sat with his cup.

He sat at the stool with the half-light of the cooling forge on the wall and the long bench with the letters in their rows and the empty space at the corner where Joren's outgoing letter had sat the week before, and he sat a count, and he sat another count, and the shop was the shop, and Ash was at the stool, and his hand was on his knee, and the cup was in the other hand, and the cup was cold.

He set the cup down.

He went up the stair.

He came down before the grey at the high window.

He had not slept the way he had slept the other nights. He had lain in the small room at the top of the curve and listened to the wind and to the slow settling of the timbers and to the small sounds of the shop below, and he had not thought, while he lay there, the words he had been thinking for six mornings. He had thought, instead, about the upper post, and the gap at the wrap, and Tesha's hand at the file, and Joren's six winters, and the wool that wet through alone.

He had thought about the bench paper, in the inside pocket of the coat on the peg, with its two lines.

He had thought about Maren's note in the outside pocket on the other side.

He had not, lying there, taken either out.

He came down at the hour before the grey. He unbarred the door. He laid his coat on the peg. He went to the bench. He had laid an offcut at the bench the night before, after Haran had gone up, in the small accidental gesture of a man who had not, when he laid it there, known he was preparing for the morning, and the offcut was the offcut on the bench.

He set his palm above it.

He breathed.

He did not narrow.

He stood with his palm above the offcut a count, and he did not, in the count, narrow, because he had been narrowing for six mornings without knowing what he was narrowing for, and this morning he knew what he was narrowing for, and the knowing was the thing he had not had yesterday, and the knowing changed the work.

He thought about the upper post.

He thought about the heating element on the heating row. He thought about the plate he had not put his hand on. He thought about the next time, which would be faster, and the time after, which would be faster still. He thought about Joren's letter. He thought about the question he had not yet written. He thought about the meeting hall in Shen Pass, which had not yet been wrapped, and the linen that had not yet come, and the carter who would take a roll of linen out at the next post if the linen were at the bench.

He thought about the offcut.

He set his palm above it.

He narrowed.

He narrowed slowly. He did not narrow the way he had been narrowing in the six mornings, which was the way of a man trying to find a thing. He narrowed the way Haran had narrowed at the rod in the autumn, which was the way of a man bringing a thing to a use. He did not, in the narrowing, close all the way. He narrowed to the edge of the narrowing, and at the edge he held, and he held the edge a count, and at the count he found, under the holding, the thin line.

The thin line was thin.

It was thinner than the wide loose press. It was thinner than the thread he had been trying for. It was the thin line he had found, for the count of two breaths, on the third morning, and which he had not since, and which was, now, in his palm, holding.

The offcut warmed.

It warmed in a way that was not the fog. It warmed in the way of a thing being warmed by the warming itself, and not by the warming around the warming, and the warmth came up through his palm clean, and he held it a count, and he held it another count, and he did not, in the holding, break.

He held it three breaths.

He held it four.

The offcut was the temperature it was. He pulled back. He pulled back the way Haran pulled back at the end of a working, which was not a stopping but a letting-go of the held thing into the letting-go, and the thin line went, and the wide loose was not under it, because he had not, this morning, opened the wide loose, and the offcut sat on the bench warm and the warmth was the warmth the thin line had given.

He stood with his hand at his side.

He did not, for a count, do anything else.

He had spent, in the holding, a small amount. He had spent less than he had spent on the wide loose press, by a margin he did not yet have the feel for, but which his hand had felt in the holding. He had warmed an offcut. He had also, in the warming, done a thing he had not done in six mornings, which was to find the line and hold it for the work, and the work had been done.

The offcut was an offcut.

The offcut did not matter.

But the line had held.

He set his palm above the offcut again.

He did not narrow.

He stood with his palm above it and the warmth coming up off the iron, and he thought about the heating row. He thought about the upper post on the second plate. He thought about the third plate, which had not yet failed, but which had the same wrap on the same post, and which would, in a season or two, fail in the same way. He thought about the plate he could go to in the afternoon, with Tesha, and the things he could do at the plate that would not be the things he had been doing for a year and a half, which were the wide loose press into a coil that was not asking for a wide loose press. He thought about the things he could do at the plate that would be the thin line at the contact, and a small clean working of the post, and the wrap done right the first time, with a fraction of what the wide loose would have spent.

He thought about Joren.

He thought about the question he was going to write to Joren today.

He thought about how many winters Joren had.

He stood at the bench in the dark before the grey, with his palm above an offcut he had warmed, and he did not, for a count, channel. He did not, for a count, do anything but stand, and breathe, and look at the offcut, and feel, in the inside pocket of the coat on the peg, the bench paper with the two lines.

The fear was there.

It sat at his shoulder the way Maren's note sat in the outside pocket of his coat, which was without speaking, which was without going away, which was a thing that had a weight and a place and was not, this morning, going to leave.

The countdown was there.

It had not changed.

He had, in the six days since Tomas's first letter, channeled twice, neither for more than a count, and the reservoir was the reservoir it was, which was a thing he could feel at the back of him as a quantity, and the quantity was the quantity it was, and the math was the math, and the math said what the math had said for a year. He had not, this morning, made the math better. He had not made the reservoir last longer. He had not chosen to keep.

He had chosen the bench.

He had chosen, in the not-narrowing-for-keeping, to spend, and to spend with the line, and to make the spending count for more than the spending had counted for in the year and a half before, and the choosing was not a thing he had said aloud, and was not a thing he was going to say aloud, and was a thing that sat in him this morning at the bench in the way a thing sat in a man when the man had, at last, decided.

He took the offcut off the bench.

He set it on the cooling rack with the things from yesterday.

He went to the stove. He warmed the kettle. He warmed it the wide loose way, because the kettle was the kettle, and he was not, this morning, going to spoil the kettle, and the ring under his palm warmed and the kettle warmed, and the warming was the warming.

He poured a cup for Haran.

He set it on the iron plate at the side of the stove, where Haran kept the warm things warm.

He poured a cup for himself.

He drank standing, at the stove, with the cup in both hands, and the grey came at the high window the way it came at the high window, and the shop took on the faint shapes of its shapes, and Haran's stair was quiet because Haran was not yet down, and would be down at the hour Haran came down, and the morning was the morning.

He went to the bench.

He took a sheet of the cheap paper from under the lamp.

He took the stub of pencil from the corner.

He sat.

He wrote *Joren* at the top of the page, and then he stopped, the way he had stopped the first time, and he sat with the pencil over the paper, and he thought about the linen, and about the wool wetting through, and about the upper post on the second plate, and about Tesha's hand on the file, and about the next time, which would be faster, and the time after, which would be faster still.

The question came.

He wrote it.

He wrote it slowly, in the careful hand, and the question was a question about the wrap and the contact and the small place where two materials met and one of them gave, and he did not, when he had written it, know whether it was a right question or a real one, and he did not, this morning, mind.

He folded the paper.

He set it at the corner of the bench in the going-out place, with the folded edge facing the wall and the edge that opened facing the room.

He sat back on the stool.

The grey at the high window was pale grey now, the grey of a morning that had not committed to weather.

He drank the rest of his tea.
