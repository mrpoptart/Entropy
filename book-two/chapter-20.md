# Chapter 20

The apartment was empty when he let himself in, and the emptiness had a shape to it.

Leska's coat was gone from the hook. Rendell's boots were not by the door. The kettle stood on the stove, dry and turned half a degree off the spot Leska always set it. The window in the front room was cracked an inch to the spring air, and the air came in carrying the smell of the lower terraces, lime dust and someone's dinner two floors down.

Ash stood in the hall a moment and listened.

A footstep in the kitchen. The sound of a cupboard closing without the small theatrical thump Leska gave cupboards. Then nothing.

"Maren?" he said.

"In here."

He set his coat on the hook. He put his hand once against the inside pocket, where the folded note had lived for six days now, and then took the hand away.

She was at the kitchen table when he came in, sitting sideways to it, one arm laid along the wood. She had not stood up. She had not turned. She had a cup in front of her that was empty and a saucer beside the cup that held nothing.

"They're at the Halvers'," she said. "Back at six."

"Right."

He pulled out the chair across from her and sat.

* * *

For a while he did not say anything, and she did not either, and he watched her without seeming to.

She was thinner than the last time he had seen her. Not in any way Leska would call thin. In the way the face shows it first, around the eye, the small hollow beside the bridge of the nose where the skin set against bone when a person had been holding something for a long time. Her hair was tied back the way she tied it for work. She was not at work. It was the middle of the afternoon on a weekday and she was at the kitchen table in their parents' apartment with an empty cup in front of her and her boots still on.

"You're home early," he said.

"Mm."

"Day off?"

"Something like that."

He did not push it. He looked at the cup instead.

"Tea?" she said.

"Sure."

She got up.

He watched her cross the kitchen because there was something in the way she crossed it. Not slow. Careful. The way a person walked who was conserving something. She took the kettle off the stove and went to the sink and ran water into it and came back and set it on the burner. Then she stood at the stove and reached for the small metal striker on the shelf above and struck the burner alight by hand.

The striker scraped twice before it caught.

He watched her not look at him while she did it.

He thought about the dozen times he had sat in this kitchen and watched her bring water to the boil with a flick of two fingers, the small precise pulse of heat going into the kettle and nowhere else, the way her father had taught her to do it when she was nine. He thought about the way she had done it last winter, standing in this same spot, with the same kettle, while she told him about a promotion at the corps. The pulse had taken less than a breath. She had not even broken sentence.

He did not say anything.

She did not either.

She stood with her back to him and watched the kettle and did not turn around until the water began to move inside it.

Then she got down two cups. She got down the tin of leaves from the second shelf. She measured a spoon into each cup, evenly, and she set the spoon back in the tin without tapping it, and her hand on the handle of the kettle as she lifted it had a fine tremor in it that was not there a year ago and was there now.

She poured.

Some of the water went onto the saucer.

She set the kettle down. She did not say anything about the spilled water. She brought both cups to the table and put one in front of him and sat down with the other in front of her and folded her hands around it.

"Thanks," he said.

"Mm."

* * *

The clock on the wall above the doorway ticked. It had ticked through every conversation he had had in this kitchen for as long as he could remember. It ticked now.

He watched her hands. She watched the cup.

The tea was too hot to drink. He wrapped his fingers around the cup anyway, because his hands needed something to do, and after a moment she did the same, and they sat like that, two people holding their tea and not drinking it, and the steam came up between them and curled and was gone.

"How's work," he said. Not a question. Something to say.

"Fine."

"Yeah?"

"Mm."

She turned the cup a quarter on the saucer. She turned it back.

"How's Haran," she said.

"Good. Good. The shop's full. We've got, uh, three commissions ahead of us we won't finish before midsummer. He's happy about it. He doesn't say so. But he's happy."

"Right."

"Ryn's well."

"Good."

He waited. He thought about saying something about the broadsheets, about Davan, about the long week. He thought about the note in the inside pocket of his coat in the hall, the one she had left at the workshop six days ago in handwriting he had recognized before he had read a word. *I'm taking some leave from work.* That was all it had said. No greeting. No signature. Her hand was unmistakable.

He did not say anything about the note.

"Maren."

"Mm."

"You all right."

She did not answer for a while.

She lifted the cup and blew across the surface of it and set it down without drinking.

"Was it like this?" she said.

"Like what?"

"Like the ground is moving. Like the thing you built your life on is dissolving and you're supposed to keep standing."

He did not move.

He looked at her.

She was looking at the cup. She was not looking at him. Her hands were folded around the cup and her shoulders were where she always set them and her face was the face she brought to family dinners, and her eyes, when they came up to his at last, were the eyes of a person who had not slept in some long time and had stopped pretending she had.

"Yes," he said.

* * *

He waited.

She did not say anything for a long time. He let her not say it. He had spent twenty-three years sitting across kitchen tables from people who were trying to find the way to a sentence, and the only thing he had ever learned that was useful was that you did not help them find it. You waited. The sentence came when the person was ready or it did not come at all, and either way the help was the waiting.

She set the cup down on the saucer. She set her hands flat on the table on either side of the saucer. The fingers of her right hand were trembling, very slightly, and she watched them do it for a moment and then she pressed the palm flat against the wood, and the fingers went still.

She saw him see it.

The thing in her face moved.

It was not anything he could have named. Not a flinch. Not a wince. The smallest collapse of a structure that had been held in place so long that he had stopped being able to see it until it dropped a fraction of an inch and he saw, in its absence, what it had been.

Her mouth opened. Her mouth closed.

"I don't know how to do this," she said.

He did not interrupt.

"You, you spent twenty-three years learning how to live without it. I don't know how. I never learned. I never had to learn. I was so busy being grateful I had it that I never imagined."

She stopped.

She did not finish the sentence.

She picked up the cup and put it down again and looked at the spilled water on the saucer.

"How bad," he said.

"Bad enough."

"Tell me."

She shook her head. Once. It was not a refusal. It was the way a person shook their head when the words were too many to choose between.

"The lattice work," she said. "The deep set, on the southern run. I did it last spring in a morning. I did it in a morning in the rain, with two apprentices, and I was home for lunch. I tried it on Eighth Day. I, I had to put down a pulse three times before it took. The third time it took I thought I was going to. I don't know. I sat down on the line. I sat down on the line in front of two apprentices."

"Anyone see."

"They saw."

"What did you say."

"I said the pulse was off. I said the lattice was set wrong. I said I'd come back in the morning."

"And."

"And I came back in the morning and it took on the second pulse, and they didn't say anything, and I haven't been on a southern run since."

"Your senior."

"Hasn't asked."

"Will."

"Will."

She drank, finally. She drank a swallow of the tea and set the cup down and did not look at him.

"I left a note at the workshop," she said.

"I have it."

"You didn't open it."

"I opened it."

"Right."

"Maren."

"Don't."

He waited.

She looked at him.

Her eyes were dry. They had been dry the whole afternoon. They were dry now and they were also the eyes of a person who had run out of the thing she had been running on, and he had seen those eyes on other people but never on her. He had not, in his life, seen those eyes on her.

"I felt guilty every day," she said.

He did not move.

"Every single day. I would wake up and channel the light on and think: he can't do this. My brother can't do this simple thing that I do without thinking. And then I'd work harder, be better, achieve more, because at least if I earned it, if I was the best at what I could do, then maybe it wasn't just luck that I got it and he didn't."

The clock ticked.

He had thought, in the part of himself that planned for things he never let happen, that if she ever said this he would say something. He had rehearsed sentences for it for half his life. Sentences that absolved her. Sentences that made it small. Sentences that turned the moment so they could both walk away from it without having to look at it directly.

He did not say any of those sentences.

"I know," he said. "I always knew."

"You never said anything."

"Neither did you."

She made a sound. It was not a laugh. It was the shape of a laugh from a person who did not have the breath for one.

She put her hand over her mouth.

She took it away.

* * *

For a while neither of them said anything, and the steam came up off the cups and was gone, and a door slammed somewhere two floors down, and a child shouted, and the clock ticked.

He thought about the apartment they had grown up in, the one before this one, and the kitchen there, which had been smaller. He thought about being seven and standing on a stool to reach the cupboard while she stood at the burner at five and channeled the heat by herself for the first time, and the way Leska had clapped, the way Rendell had come in from the front room and said *well there she is, well there she is*, and the way he had gotten down off the stool and gone to his room and looked at the back of his hand for a long time as if the back of his hand could tell him something.

He had loved her that day. He had loved her and he had hated something, and he had not had the words for either, and he had sat on the bed and pulled the blanket over his head and breathed against the inside of the blanket until the not-having-the-words went away.

He had not gone back to the kitchen until dinner. At dinner he had told a joke. He could not remember the joke. He remembered Leska laughing at it, and Rendell laughing at it, and Maren smiling at him over her plate, and the warm clear feeling of having moved them, of having earned his place at the table by the only thing he had that worked.

He had been seven.

"I told a joke," he said.

"What."

"That night you boiled the kettle. The first time. I went to my room. I came back at dinner and told a joke. I don't remember the joke. I remember it landed."

"Ash."

"I made jokes for twenty-three years."

"I know you did."

"I made them because I had to. The cheerfulness was, it was my rent. I was renting the seat at the table. Every dinner. Every family thing. Every time someone asked me how I was, I said good and meant it, and I meant it because if I didn't mean it I couldn't say it convincingly enough to keep the room from going quiet around me, and I couldn't, I couldn't do the room going quiet."

"I know."

"You don't."

"I do."

"You don't know what it cost."

"Tell me."

He drank. He had not meant to drink. The tea was still too hot. He drank it anyway and felt the burn of it across the roof of his mouth and was glad of it.

"It cost the inside of me," he said. "I performed the outside of me so well I forgot there was an inside. I would catch myself, you know, walking home, alone, on a stairwell, and I would notice my face. I would notice my face was set in the small smile I used at dinner. Walking alone. On a stairwell. Nobody to see it. The smile was just there. Like a coat I'd forgotten to take off."

She was watching him now.

"And I hated it," he said. "I hated the smile and I couldn't take it off. Because if I took it off I didn't know what was under it. I had never let anything be under it. I had built the whole front so carefully there was nothing behind the front. I was the front. There was no, there was no Ash behind the Ash."

"Was there."

"Now. There is now."

"Was there then."

"I don't know."

"That's the thing," she said.

"What's the thing."

"That's the thing I'm scared of."

* * *

She got up.

She went to the sink. She did not run the tap. She stood at the sink and put her hands on the rim of it and her shoulders came up, just an inch, just the inch a person's shoulders came up when they were holding something at the edge of holdable, and they stayed there for the count of three breaths and then they went down.

She came back to the table. She did not sit. She stood beside her chair with her hand on the back of it.

"I never failed," she said. "I never let myself. Because if I failed it meant the magic was wasted on me. It meant somebody picked wrong, somebody, the world, whatever picks. It meant I was the wrong sister to give it to. And so I, I never failed. I worked harder than anyone in the corps. I was the youngest senior they ever made. I, I do the work of two engineers. I have done the work of two engineers since I was nineteen years old, and not one of them has ever known I was doing it, because if they knew I was doing it they would think I needed help, and I cannot need help. I cannot. There isn't room."

"There's room."

"There isn't. There's never been. You took it. You took all of it. I don't, I don't mean it like that, I mean, I mean there was a finite amount of it in the house and you needed it, and you should have had it, and I, I made myself a person who didn't need any of it, and I have been that person for so long I don't know how to be a person who needs a thing."

"Sit down."

She sat down.

She put her hands flat on the table again.

"I'm tired," she said.

"I know."

"I'm so tired."

"I know."

"And I can't, I can't tell anyone. I can't tell Leska. She'll, she'll fix me. She'll make it the project. She'll bring me soup. I can't be the project. I have never been the project. If I become the project I, I don't know what I am."

"You're my sister."

"That isn't enough."

"Yes it is."

"Ash."

"It is."

"You don't know what you're saying."

"I know exactly what I'm saying."

She looked at him. She had been looking past him most of the afternoon, at the cup, at the saucer, at the spilled water, and now she looked at him directly, and her face did the thing again, the small fractional drop of a structure that had been carrying weight for so long it had become the wall.

A tear came down her face. One. She did not lift her hand to it. She let it go down the line of her jaw and onto the collar of her shirt, where it darkened a small spot in the fabric and was gone.

She did not make a sound.

He watched it.

He did not reach for her. Reaching for her was not the thing. She would have flinched off the reaching. He had known her his whole life and he knew that you did not reach.

He sat across the table and held the tea he was not drinking and let her cry the one tear, and the second, and the third, and at the fourth he put his hand flat in the middle of the table, palm down, the same way she had her hands on the table, and after a while she lifted her right hand off the wood and set it on top of his, and that was all.

Her hand was cold.

* * *

When she had stopped, she took her hand back. She wiped her face with the heel of it, once on each side, the same brisk gesture she used to wipe down a workbench.

"I need to tell you something else," she said.

"All right."

"It's bad."

"All right."

"I hated you."

He nodded.

"Not you. The thing of you. The fact of you. The fact that there was a brother who didn't get it, and that meant somebody was going to have to make up for it, and I knew, I knew at six, I knew at seven, I knew the whole time growing up, that the somebody was me. I hated that. I hated the arrangement. I hated, I hated that the world had made an arrangement that put us in the shapes it did and there was nothing either of us could do to be out of those shapes. And I loved you. I loved you the whole time. And I didn't, I didn't know how to hold both of those things at once, and so I didn't hold them. I put one of them somewhere I couldn't see it and I stood on top of it for twenty-three years."

"I know."

"And I."

"Maren."

"What."

"I hated you too."

She closed her eyes.

"I hated that you had it," he said. "Not you. The fact. The fact of you having it. There was a, there was a thing in me that hated it. Every time you boiled a kettle. Every time you came home from school with a mark for fine channeling. Every time Leska said *well there she is*. I hated the fact of it and I loved you and I lived in between those for twenty-three years and I did not, I did not know how to put either of them down. So I made the jokes. I made the jokes and I sat at the table and I was the funny brother and I was the easy one, and I, I held that thing in me like something I had swallowed wrong, and I never let it up. Not once. Not in twenty-three years. Not until."

"Until."

"Until the river. Until I came up out of the river and I, the thing I had swallowed was gone, and I didn't know how I felt about it being gone. I was supposed to be free of it. I was free of it and I didn't know what I had under it either."

"Right," she said.

It was the *right* that closed conversations. He had heard her use it at family dinners a thousand times. It was not closing this conversation. It was the only word she had.

"Right," he said back.

* * *

The light had moved across the table while they talked. It had been on the saucer when he sat down. Now it was on the back of his hand. The slat of sun from the kitchen window was the same slat that had crossed this table every spring afternoon of his childhood, at the same hour, and would do it again tomorrow. The sun did not know anything had happened. The sun went on.

"I'm going to lose it," she said.

"I know."

"All of it. Not in a year. Maybe two. Maybe less. The last test I did, the deep draw, I, I held it for a count of four and the count of four used to be a count of fourteen. It is going to keep going. I have looked at the pattern. It is going to keep going."

"Yes."

"And after that I'm going to be."

She did not finish.

"You're going to be Maren," he said.

"That is not, that is not the thing it sounds like."

"I know."

"I have been Maren-the-channeler since I was nine years old."

"I know."

"I don't know who Maren is without it."

"I didn't know who Ash was without it for twenty-three years," he said. "And then for one year. And then for about four months. And I am not, I am not going to tell you I know now. But I will tell you the not-knowing is survivable. It's survivable. I survived it. I survived it without anything. You have, you have more than I had."

"What do I have."

"You have me."

She looked at him.

"You have me," he said again. "I'm not, I'm not the Wellspring offering. I'm the brother. The brother who knows how to do this. I learned how to do this. I learned it the hard way and I learned it slow and I am, I am the only person you know who knows how to do it. So you have that. You have me knowing it. Whatever the not-knowing looks like from inside, I've been inside it. I can sit with you in it."

She did not say anything.

"Do you hear me," he said.

"I hear you."

"All right."

"All right."

* * *

She poured them both more tea.

She did it from the kettle on the stove, which had cooled, and the water was no longer boiling, but she did it anyway, and the tea in the cups was not as hot as the first cups had been, and neither of them said anything about it. She sat back down. She wrapped her hands around the cup again. The tremor was there still. It was smaller. Or he was looking at it differently. He could not tell.

"I haven't been to work in five days," she said.

"I figured."

"I told them I had a fever."

"You don't."

"No."

"You'll have to go back."

"I know."

"What will you tell them."

"I don't know."

"You don't have to tell them. Not yet."

"No."

"You can take the leave. The note, the note you wrote. You can take it."

"Yes."

"How long."

"Two weeks."

"Take three."

"Two."

"Right."

* * *

He thought about asking her if she wanted to tell Leska and Rendell. He did not ask her. The answer was in her shoulders. She would tell them when she told them and not before, and the asking would only require her to say no, and the no would cost her something, and he would not make her spend that.

He thought, too, about the old choreography. About how, an hour ago, an hour and a half, he would have reached for the cheerfulness, the small joke that smoothed a hard moment, the deflection that let them both walk back into the rest of the day without having to sit any longer in the silence. He thought about the joke he might have made. There was always a joke. The joke about the kettle and how she had finally taken his side of the family trade, the no-magic side. He could see the shape of the joke and he could see how it would have landed and he could see Maren saying *Right* and getting up and the conversation being filed and put away.

He did not make the joke.

She did not ask him to.

* * *

The door of the apartment opened at six.

Leska came in first, with a cloth bag from the Halvers' and the small breath of stairs in her, and Rendell a step behind her with his coat already half off and saying something about the joint of pipe outside the second-floor landing that someone had wrapped wrong.

"In the kitchen," Maren called.

"Both of you," said Leska.

"Both of us."

Leska came in and stopped at the doorway, the bag still on her arm, and she looked at the table and the two cups, and he saw her register them, and he saw her decide not to register them, the small economical decision a mother made a hundred times a day in a house she had lived in too long to be surprised in.

"Well," she said. "Sit. Sit. I have. The Halvers gave us beans, the Halvers always give us beans, they have, I think, they have a contract with a bean somewhere."

"Beans," said Rendell from the hall.

"I'll set the table," Maren said.

"I'll help," said Ash.

He stood up. He moved his cup to the sink. He picked up Maren's cup and moved it too. She got the bowls down from the second shelf without comment. He got the spoons. The kitchen, which had been the kitchen of one conversation for two hours, became the kitchen of dinner, and the change happened without anybody saying anything, the way light changed in a room when the sun moved across a window, and he watched it happen, and Maren, on the other side of the small space, watched him watch it.

She did not smile.

She did not have to.

* * *

They ate.

Leska talked about the Halvers' new grandchild and the trouble with the staircase paint and the price of leaf this spring and a woman she had seen at the market who used to live on their old hall. Rendell ate with the patient deliberation he had eaten with for forty years. He waited for a pause in Leska's account of the staircase paint, and then he set down his spoon and said, in the considering voice he used for analogies, "It's like the joint of pipe outside the second-floor landing."

"The paint is."

"The whole building. The whole building is one badly wrapped joint and we keep painting over it."

"Rendell."

"I'm only saying."

"You're always only saying."

"Pass the salt."

Maren passed the salt.

Ash watched her pass the salt. There was nothing in the passing. Her hand went out, the salt went across, his father took it. There was no half-beat in her. The half-beat that had been there for twenty-three years, the small delay in which she turned a shoulder a quarter of a degree away from him before she handed a thing across the table, the small turning-aside that he had not, until this afternoon, ever had a name for, was not in the gesture. The salt went across the table in a straight line.

"How's the shop," Rendell said to him.

"Full."

"That's the way to be."

"Mm."

"And the boy."

"Ryn's good."

"Good."

Leska looked at Maren over the rim of her cup. "You're eating," she said.

"I'm eating."

"Good."

"Mm."

It was a small *mm*. It was not the *mm* of the afternoon. It was a kitchen *mm*, an ordinary one, and Leska took it and was satisfied with it and went on talking about the grandchild.

He looked, once, at the place by the stove where the kettle stood. It was where Maren had set it down two hours ago. Nobody had moved it. Nobody had said anything about its being a quarter degree off the spot. The striker was on the shelf above. Leska had not yet noticed that the burner had been lit by hand, and might not, and if she did notice she would not, he thought, say so. The afternoon was inside the kitchen the way an afternoon was inside a kitchen, in the small displacements nobody named.

Maren said something dry about the bean contract.

Leska laughed. Rendell laughed a beat after Leska, the way he always laughed at Maren a beat after Leska did, and Maren did not laugh, because Maren did not laugh at her own jokes, and Ash did not laugh either, but he caught his sister's eye across the table for the second of a second, and she caught his, and that was the laugh.

The clock ticked.

The light went off the table.

The kitchen was the kitchen.

* * *

After dinner he helped his mother with the bowls. Maren sat with Rendell and listened to him talk about the joint of pipe again. Ash dried each bowl and stacked it. He had dried these bowls when they were the new bowls. He had dried them for fifteen years.

"You'll come Saturday," Leska said.

"Saturday."

"Your father wants to look at the upper hall."

"All right."

"Bring Ryn."

"All right."

She paused. She had a bowl half-dried in her hand. She did not look at him.

"Maren's been quiet," she said.

"Mm."

"Has she said anything to you."

He took the bowl out of her hand and finished drying it.

"She's all right," he said.

Leska looked at him.

He had, in his life, told her this exact sentence about himself about six hundred times. She had taken it every time. She took it now.

"All right," she said.

"All right."

She handed him another bowl.

* * *

He left at half past eight.

Maren walked him to the door, the way she had walked him to the door of this apartment a thousand times. At the door she reached up and adjusted his collar. It was not crooked. The collar fix was not for the collar. She had been doing it since they were children. He stood and let her do it, and she did it, and then she stepped back, and her hand stayed half-raised for a fraction longer than usual, and then it came down.

"Saturday," she said.

"Saturday."

"Bring Ryn."

"All right."

She did not say anything else.

He did not either.

He went down the stairs slowly. On the second-floor landing he passed the joint of pipe Rendell had been talking about. It was wrapped in old cloth and the cloth was painted over and the paint was peeling. He put his hand against the wall beside it for a moment, for no reason, and then he took the hand away and went on.

The note was still in the inside pocket of his coat. He did not take it out. He did not need to. He knew what it said.

Outside, the spring evening had cooled. The lamps along the lower terrace were lit, and someone two doors down was channeling a small light onto a workbench in a window, the warm narrow point of it visible from the street. He stood for a moment and watched it. Then he went home.

He did not, on the walk, catch his face arranged in the small smile.

He noticed not catching it.

He kept walking.
