# Chapter 9

The note came at the second hour.

Haran brought it in from the square the way he brought in the broadsheets, under his arm, without ceremony, and laid it on the bench. The paper was good paper. The fold was clean. There was no seal. The hand was a hand Ash had not seen before, and the hand was, in the way a hand was when its owner had written a great many letters and stopped trying to make them beautiful, plain.

*I would like to see the work. I would come on Fourth-day at the fourth hour, with two colleagues, if that hour suits. If it does not, name another. Davan.*

Ash read it twice.

He set it on the bench beside the file. He looked at Haran. Haran was at the stove. Haran did not turn.

"He wrote."

"Mm."

"Fourth-day."

"Day after tomorrow."

"Mm."

Haran took the kettle off. The kettle was not yet at the boil. He set it on the cold side of the stove and went out into the square.

The note lay where Ash had set it. The hand was the hand of the man on the cart. Ash registered this without being able to say how he knew. The letters leaned the way the sentences had leaned, forward, weight on the back foot, the small space at the end of each word the space of a man who had decided where the word ended and was not going to argue with himself about it.

He took a square of bench paper and wrote the answer.

*Fourth-day at the fourth hour. Come to the workshop. The basin is in the square if you arrive early.*

He signed it Ash.

He did not, in the signing, write *Wellspring.* The word had been on every letter the Council had sent him in the year. He had never signed it. He had, in the year, signed Ash, and the signing had been, on most letters, the smallest correction he could make against a register he did not own.

He folded the answer.

Haran came back from the square.

"Tell the boy to take it," Ash said.

"Mm."

He held the answer out. Haran took it. Haran turned it in his hand once and looked at the address Ash had not written on the outside.

"He'll know where," Ash said.

"He will."

Haran went back out.

* * *

Fourth-day was cold.

The wind came up from the canal in the morning the way wind came up from the canal in the third week of the season, thin and steady, with the small grit the towpath threw up when the towpath had not been swept. Ash went down to the basin at the third hour and turned the tap and ran a count of three and closed it. The water came. The water was not warm. The pump did not warm the water. The pump moved the water from the cistern to the tap, and the cistern's heat exchange was the cistern's heat exchange, and the water in the morning ran at the temperature the cistern's pipes ran at, which was the temperature of a building's ground floor in the third week of the season.

Vell came across the square at the half-hour.

She had her cap on. She had the cap on the way she had the cap on when she had been at the Cooperage two nights before, low to the eyebrow, and Ash had not, since the rally, said her name, and she had not, since the rally, said his. She had come to the morning crew at the third hour yesterday and again today. She had primed the pump. She had checked the manifold. She had said *good morning* to Reska and *morning* to Jora and nothing, in either morning, to Ash, and Ash had said *good morning* to her on each morning the way he had said *good morning* to her on every morning before the Cooperage, and the answer had been a nod and a *morning,* and the *morning* had been the *morning* it had always been, except that it was a *morning* now in which both of them knew where the other had been on Second-day at the seventh hour.

She came to the basin.

"Tap's good," she said.

"It's good."

"Reska said you said someone's coming."

"Three someones."

She nodded.

"At the fourth?"

"At the fourth."

"Mm."

She looked at the tap. She did not look at him.

"I'll be on the pump," she said.

"You don't have to be."

"I'll be on the pump."

She crossed the square to the pump. She uncovered it. She set the cap on the bench beside the crank and ran the count under her breath, which Ash heard, because the morning was quiet, and the count was the count Jora had taught her in the second week, *one, two, three, prime,* and the count was a count she did not need anymore and ran anyway.

He went back to the workshop.

Haran was at the bench. Haran had the file in his hand. Haran had, since the morning, been finishing the brass blank he had not finished on Second-day.

"Vell will be on the pump," Ash said.

"Mm."

"I told her she didn't have to."

"Mm."

Haran filed.

"She wants to be," Haran said.

"Yes."

"Mm."

The brass shaving curled and dropped to the bench. Haran swept it, with the side of his hand, into the small tin he kept for shavings.

"Three of them," Haran said.

"Three."

"Two organizers with him."

"Yes."

"Mm."

Haran filed. He did not look up. The file moved the way the file moved when Haran was, behind the file, thinking of the next thing he was not going to say, and Ash had been in the workshop long enough to know the file's two registers, the file that was filing and the file that was a man's hands keeping busy while a man kept the rest of himself out of the room.

"You'll want the back bench clear," Haran said.

"I'll clear it."

"Mm."

Ash cleared the back bench. The back bench had, in the year, become the bench he laid letters on, the bench under the high window, with the under-shelf where he had set the broadsheet and the transcript and the platform, and the bench was not empty when he was finished, but it was the bench it had been before the year, with two clamps and a vise and the small leather roll and the brass blank Haran had set down to cool. He laid a clean cloth across the under-shelf. He did not, in the laying, look at what was under the cloth.

"Tea," Haran said.

"At the fourth."

"Mm."

* * *

They came at the fourth.

Ash was at the workshop door. He had not, in the morning, decided to be at the door, and at the fourth hour the small sound the gate made when the gate opened was the sound that put him at the door without the deciding, and he was there, with one hand on the frame, when the three of them came across the square.

Davan was the tallest.

He registered this first. He had registered this at the Cooperage too. The cart had been a cart, and Davan on the cart had been on a cart, and the height had been the height of a man on a cart, and Ash had not separated the man from the platform until he saw him on the stones of the square in the cold flat light of the fourth hour without a cart under him.

Davan was tall. He was, on the stones, perhaps a hand taller than Ash. The coat was the coat from the cart, the working coat in the second year of its life, and the cap was off, in his hand, and the hair was the hair of a man who cut his own hair when he remembered to, dark, plain, not careful. He walked the way a man walked who had walked a great many city blocks on the same boots, and the boots were the boots of a man who had walked them.

The two with him were a man and a woman.

The man was older. He was perhaps forty. He was the man who had warmed the crowd at the Cooperage, the man in the canal-quarter coat who had said *Davan will speak,* and the man's coat today was a different coat, and the canal-quarter cut was the same. He was shorter than Davan and broader at the shoulders and his hands, which Ash registered before he registered the face, were the hands of a man who had worked at the canal locks before the canal had stopped working. The hands had the small white scars at the knuckles a lock-hand had, and a thumb on the right hand that did not, Ash registered when the man came up to the door, fully straighten.

The woman was younger. Mid-twenties. Sharper.

She was the kind of person Ash registered through a small assembly of details before he registered her as a person, which was the way he registered most of the people Dorenne had brought into rooms with him in the year, which was, he registered with a small cold turn in the chest, an unkindness, and he set the unkindness aside and looked at her. She had a black coat, well-cut, not new. Her hair was short. She had a satchel on her shoulder, leather, scuffed, the kind of satchel a person carried who carried papers. She was, in the way she stood, the lieutenant who stood the closer of the two to her principal, and she was, in the way her eyes had moved across the square before they had landed on the door, the lieutenant who saw the room before the principal did.

"Ash," Davan said.

He said the name the way he had said his own name on the cart. He said it and he stopped, and the stop was the stop of a man who did not, in introduction, give more than he had decided to give.

"Davan."

"Korel. Liss."

He named them in the order they stood, the older man first. Korel inclined his head a quarter-inch. Liss did not. Liss's eyes had finished the square and were on Ash now and were taking a measurement Ash could feel without resenting.

"Come in," Ash said.

He stepped back from the door. They came in. Davan came in first. Davan ducked, slightly, at the lintel, the way a tall man who had been in a great many short doorways ducked, without thinking about it. Korel came in second. Liss came in third and turned, on the threshold, to close the door behind her, and the closing of the door was the closing of a person who closed doors behind herself by habit.

Haran was at the bench.

Haran was, at the fourth hour exactly, doing the thing he was not going to be doing two minutes later. He was working brass. He had the blank in the small jaw of the vise and the file in his hand, and he did not look up when the three came in, and the not-looking-up was the not-looking-up Ash had watched him do with a hundred apprentices' fathers and a dozen Council clerks and, once, a year ago, with a man from the second terrace who had come to the workshop to offer Haran a sum of money to teach somewhere else. The not-looking-up was a courtesy. It made the room a room a man could enter without being met.

"This is Haran," Ash said.

"Haran," Davan said.

Haran's hand came off the file. He set the file on the bench. He turned.

"Mm," Haran said.

He nodded the quarter-inch. He looked at Davan. He looked at Korel. He looked at Liss. The looking was, in the way Haran looked, neither a measurement nor an absence of one. He looked at each of them the way he looked at a piece of brass he had not yet decided what to do with.

"Tea's hot," Haran said.

"Thank you," Davan said.

Haran turned back to the bench. He did not pour. He picked up the file.

Liss looked at Ash. She had registered, Ash registered, that the tea was an offer and a not-offer at once, and she had filed the registering somewhere her face did not show.

"I thought," Ash said, "we'd start at the basin."

"The basin," Davan said.

"It's where the work is."

"Lead."

* * *

He led.

He took them across the square. He took them the long way, because the long way ran past the cellar door of Block Three with the small lit lamp on the inside of the doorframe, and the lamp was the lamp Reska had put there in the second week, and he wanted them to see it without his pointing at it. He took them past Vell's pump. Vell was on the crank. She did not look up. The crank was at the count Jora had taught her, slow, even, and the small mechanical tick of the manifold under the pump was the tick a manifold made when the manifold had been set right at the third hour.

"That's the western pump," Ash said. "Vell runs the morning crew on it."

Davan stopped.

He stopped a half-pace short of the pump and stood and watched Vell crank. He watched her for the count of ten. He did not say anything. Vell did not look up. Vell was not, Ash registered, performing not looking up. She was running the count.

Davan looked at the pump.

"How does it draw?"

"The manifold."

"From the cistern."

"From the second feeder. The cistern itself is above us, two streets up, but we tapped the second feeder because the second feeder runs through the wall here and the head is right."

"What's the lift?"

"Twenty-two feet to the basin level. Twenty-six to the upper flats."

"And she's lifting how much per stroke?"

Ash registered the question. The question was the third question. It was the question a person asked when they had asked the first two questions to find out whether they were going to ask the third.

"Two and a half pints."

"At what rate?"

"Forty strokes a minute, sustained. Sixty for a short pull."

"So a hundred pints a minute on long crank."

"A hundred."

"That's a household at the basin in two minutes."

"Two and a half. The basin holds two-fifty."

"Mm."

He had said *mm.* Ash registered the *mm.* It was not Haran's *mm.* It was, in the cadence, the *mm* of a man who had been around tinkerers before, or had borrowed the tic from someone he respected, and was not, in the using, performing it. He had said it the way a person who said *mm* said it, which was the way Haran said it, which was a person registering and not yet speaking.

Davan looked at the cap on the bench beside the crank.

"May I?"

He was, Ash registered, asking Vell, not him. Vell looked up.

"Yes."

Davan picked up the cap. He turned it in his hand. He looked at the inside of the cap, where the thread bit, and at the outside, where the small chip in the lacquer was. He set the cap down.

"Who taught her the count?" Davan said.

"Jora."

"Who's Jora?"

"Crew lead. She's at Building Four this morning."

"And who taught Jora?"

"Reska."

"And Reska?"

Ash paused.

"I did," he said.

"Mm."

Davan moved on.

Korel stayed at the pump a beat longer. Korel was watching Vell crank. Korel had, Ash registered, the canal-quarter eye for the question of *can the person on the crank crank for an hour,* and Korel was answering the question by watching, and the answer was the answer Ash would have given.

"It runs all day?" Korel said.

"Crews of three," Vell said, without stopping. "Hour rotations."

"And you train the next crew."

"We train them."

"Mm."

Korel nodded. Vell ran the count. Korel walked on.

Liss had not stopped at the pump. Liss was at the basin, ten paces ahead, reading the slate in the rack by the door of the bakery. The slate was the slate Reska had hung in the second week. The slate had the morning's pressure reading and the count of buckets drawn since the third hour and the small mark for the cistern indicator. Liss was reading it the way the woman two paces from Ash at the Cooperage had been reading the platform, which was the way a person read a thing she had read about and was checking, against memory, the edges of what she had read.

"Who does the slate?" Liss said.

"Reska, mornings. Jora, evenings."

"Every day."

"Every day."

"How long has it been kept?"

"Eight weeks."

"You have the older slates."

"In the workshop. Ledgers."

"I'd like to see them."

It was not a question. It was not a demand. It was a sentence with a small flat at the end the way a sentence was flat when its speaker had decided the answer would be yes and was not going to make a thing of getting there. Ash looked at her. He did not, in the looking, dislike her. He registered, instead, that she was the lieutenant who was good at exactly the kind of being a lieutenant her principal needed her to be good at, and the registering was the registering of a man who had spent a year watching Tessaly do, for Dorenne, a sharper version of the same thing.

"Yes," he said.

"Thank you."

She moved on.

* * *

Building Four was warm.

He took them in by the southern door because the southern door was the door the morning crew used, and the southern door opened on the small back hall where the riser came up from the cellar. The riser was the riser Jora had insulated in the third week. He had not, in the year, found a Council inspector who looked at the riser the way Davan looked at the riser.

Davan crouched.

He crouched at the base of the riser and put his hand against the wrap and held it there for the count of five. He took his hand away. He looked at the wrap. He looked at the joint where the wrap met the wall. He looked at the small pin Jora had set into the wall to hold the wrap's bottom edge.

"Who wrapped it?"

"Jora."

"With?"

"Rag wool and pitch. Two layers, with a layer of lath between."

"Where does the rag come from?"

"The dyers' quarter. Off-cuts. Cheap."

"And the pitch?"

"The harbor."

"Old stock?"

"Yes."

Davan looked at him.

"There's only so much old stock," Davan said.

It was not, in the saying, an objection. It was a statement that named a fact the way a man named a fact when he had walked the same accounting Ash had walked and had reached, in his accounting, the same step.

"There is," Ash said.

"What's your sourcing for the next district?"

"There isn't one yet."

"Mm."

Davan stood. He brushed his palm against his coat. He walked along the hall and put his hand on the wall and held it there, the way he had held it on the wrap, and Ash registered, without saying anything about the registering, that the man was reading temperatures by hand the way Haran read temperatures by hand, which was the way a person read temperatures who had spent enough years near walls to trust the hand.

"It's warm," Davan said.

"It's warm."

"How much did the wrap cost you?"

"Five percent."

"Of pool?"

"Of the building's allocation. Less than half a percent of pool."

"For the building."

"For the building."

"And the building holds heat."

"It holds it. The wrap and the new flue and the eastern wall reseal are the three pieces."

"And you channeled the wall reseal."

"I channeled it."

"Mm."

He did not, in the *mm,* do the thing Ash had been waiting for him to do. He did not say *and the wall is the part nobody else can do.* He registered it. He let the registering be the thing.

They went up to the second floor.

Korel went into the kitchen of one of the flats, by invitation, because a woman Ash did not know by name had opened her door at the sound of feet on her landing and had said *come, see how warm it is,* and Korel had gone in. Ash heard the woman's voice through the door. He did not hear the words. He heard Korel's voice answer, low. He heard Korel laugh, once, at something the woman said. The laugh was the laugh of a man who laughed when something was actually funny.

Liss stayed in the hall.

Liss was looking at the hinge of the second door on the landing. She looked at it the way she had looked at the slate. She did not touch it.

Davan stood at the top of the stair.

He stood with his hand on the rail and his head turned slightly to the right, listening. The building was, at the fourth-and-half hour, the building it was at the fourth-and-half hour, which was a building with a child's voice on the third floor and a door opening on the first and the small high creak of a window sash being lifted and the clatter of a pot being set on a stove. He listened to it for a long beat.

"How many flats?" he said.

"Twelve in this building. Forty-eight in the block."

"All warm."

"All of them."

He nodded once.

"Show me the workbench," he said.

* * *

They went back to the workshop at the fifth hour.

Korel did not come. Korel had stayed in Block Three with the woman from the second-floor flat, who had said *I have a cousin who lives in the canal quarter, and we have not been warm in the canal quarter in twelve years,* and Korel had said *tell me about your cousin,* and Ash had heard, on the stair on the way down, the beginning of an answer he did not stay to listen to. Liss had gone with Korel. Liss had said *I'll take the ledgers later,* and Ash had said *they'll be on the bench when you come,* and Liss had nodded and gone.

Davan came alone.

He came alone the way a man came alone who had brought two people and had, by the fifth hour, decided that the visit was no longer the visit, and the visit was no longer the visit because the work had spoken for itself in the first hour the way Ash had said it would and the second hour was a different hour. They walked back across the square. Vell was off the crank. The second crew was on. The basin tap ran.

Haran was at the bench.

The brass was no longer in the vise. There was a kettle on the stove. There was the brown pot. There were three cups, not two, on the bench. The third cup was the cup Haran kept for the boy from the bakery who came down sometimes at the evening hour to sit. Haran had set the cups out without looking at the door.

"Tea," Haran said.

"Thank you," Davan said.

Haran poured.

He poured Davan's first. He poured Ash's. He poured his own. He set the pot back on the bench. He picked up the file. He did not, in the picking up, return to the brass. He held the file. He looked at it the way a man looked at a thing he was about to set down somewhere else.

"I'll be in the back," Haran said.

"Stay," Ash said.

It came out before he had decided.

Haran looked at him.

"Stay," Ash said. "You can work."

"Mm."

Haran set the file on the back bench. He took a small length of wire from the box on the wall. He took a pair of pliers. He sat on the stool by the back bench, with his side to the front bench, and began to wind the wire on a former. The winding was the slow patient winding of a man who was, for the next two hours, going to be in the room, working, the way Haran had been in the room, working, when the man from the second terrace had come a year ago.

Davan registered Haran. Davan did not, in the registering, say anything about it. He took his cup. He sat on the crate by the bench, the crate Ash had sat on at the third hour two days before, and he set the cup on the bench beside his hand, and he looked at the bench.

The bench was the bench.

The vise was at the right end. The clamps were laid down along the back edge. The small leather roll was open. The brass blank was there, finished, smooth, with the small turn at the lip Haran had been working on. The light from the high window was the light a workshop window threw at the fifth hour, which was a level light, gold-pale, that did not reach the under-shelf at the back of the bench but reached the bench itself fully.

"Good bench," Davan said.

"It is."

"Walnut."

"Beech."

"Mm. I never know."

He smiled. The smile was the first smile Ash had seen on his face, and it was a small smile, half-formed, the smile of a man who had said *I never know* about something he could have known in two seconds because he wanted, in that two seconds, to be a man who did not know a thing.

Ash sat on the crate opposite.

* * *

They talked.

They talked the way the first half-hour of talking had been the tour, and the second half-hour had been the building, and the third had been the climb back across the square, and now there was a bench between them and tea in the cups and the light at the window, and they talked.

Davan started.

He started with the pump. He had questions. He had questions Ash had not been asked by anyone in the year, not by the Council inspectors and not by the engineers Dorenne had sent and not by the broadsheet writers who had come to write about the proof of concept and had written, instead, about the man who had built it. Davan asked about the manifold and the second feeder and the lift and the rate, and he asked about what failed first on the pump on a cold morning, and he asked about what Vell did when the count broke, and he asked about who taught Jora the wrap and who had made the small pin in the wall, and he listened.

The listening was the thing.

Ash registered the listening the way he had registered the cap-turning at the pump, which was the way he had registered the hand-on-the-wall at the riser, which was the way he had registered, on the cart at the Cooperage, the half-beat after *we are done asking.* Davan did not, in the listening, prepare his next question while Ash was answering. He listened to the answer until the answer ended. Then he was, in the small visible turning of his attention, with the answer for a beat. Then he asked the next thing.

The next thing was, in every case, the thing the answer had not yet covered.

"And the rag wool," Davan said. "The dyers' quarter. How long does that supply last for one district?"

"Eight months. Maybe ten."

"And how long for ten districts?"

"It doesn't."

"Mm."

He drank tea.

"You've been running the math."

"I've been running it."

"And the answer is not eight months at one tenth."

"The answer is no more than eight months. The shortage is on the dyeing side. If we work ten districts, the dyers stop having off-cuts because the off-cuts are the off-cuts of a quantity of cloth that doesn't change. We buy bolts. The bolts cost. The cost is a different cost."

"And nobody's funding the bolts."

"Nobody."

"Dorenne."

"Dorenne hasn't said no. Dorenne hasn't said yes."

"Mm."

He did not, on the *mm,* offer an opinion of Dorenne. He registered Dorenne the way he had registered the pump.

"Tell me about the boy who quit," Davan said.

Ash put his cup down.

He did not, in the putting down, look at Haran. Haran was winding wire. Haran's hands moved on the former at the same rate they had moved at the third hour and at the half-hour before the third and at, Ash registered, the fourth hour two days ago when Ash had read the transcript and had said *they have a platform.*

"Which boy," Ash said.

"There are more than one."

"There are two."

"Tell me about both."

Ash told him about both.

He told him about Aren, who had stayed three weeks and had said, in the third week, that he was tired of being taught like a child, and had quit. He told him about the second boy, who had gone with Aren, who had been at the Cooperage on Second-day standing alone against the western wall with his arms folded across his chest in the way the man at Building Four had stood at the third hour. Davan did not ask the boy's name. Ash did not give it. The boy was, for the conversation, a person whose grievance had been a real grievance and whose departure had been, in the shape of a thing Ash had been failing at, the visible edge of the failing.

"He's not wrong," Davan said.

"He's not."

"You taught them how to operate. You did not teach them how the system was made."

"I didn't."

"And now they don't know how the system was made."

"They don't."

"Mm."

He did not, in the *mm,* push. He had said the sentence. He had said it the way a man said a sentence he had said in a great many rooms in the year, calmly, without weight on the verb. He let it sit.

Ash drank his tea.

* * *

Davan's story came in pieces.

It came in pieces because Davan did not, in the way of a man who told his story to take the room, tell his story. It came in pieces because Ash asked. It came in pieces because the questions Ash asked were the questions a man asked who had spent a half-hour being asked very good questions and had begun, in the second half-hour, to ask them back.

"Where are you from."

"The mill quarter. South side. By the third bridge."

"Your parents."

"Mother's a weaver. Father was a stove-fitter."

"Was."

"Twelve years ago."

"The bad winter."

"Yes."

He said *yes* the way a man said *yes* about a thing he had said *yes* about a great many times.

"And your pool."

Davan looked at the cup.

"I have a pool," he said.

"How much."

"Enough to know what it should be."

"Mm."

"I can light a lamp. I can warm a cup of tea. I cannot, on my best day, do what an apprentice elder did at twenty without thinking about it."

"You trained."

"My mother paid for two years of teaching when I was twelve. The teacher told her, in the second year, not to keep paying. He said it kindly. He said *the boy is bright. The boy is not strong.* My mother stopped paying and sent me to my uncle to learn fitting."

"You fit."

"I fit for six years. I went to the Council gallery the first time at nineteen because my uncle's shop closed when the city stopped repairing the lower mains. I sat in the back. I listened. I came back. I have been coming back."

He paused.

"I did not, in the listening, become a fitter again."

"No."

"I became this."

He gestured with the cup, a small inward gesture, at himself, at the bench, at the room. The gesture was the gesture of a man who had not, in many tellings of this story, made the gesture, and did not, in the making of it, perform.

"My parents' generation," Davan said. "They could light a room without a lamp. They could heat a flat in a cold snap with a flat hand on the wall. My mother does not, anymore, but she did when I was a child, and I remember it. I remember the hand on the wall. I watched her do it the way a child watches a parent do a thing the child is going to do later. I was waiting to be old enough to do it. I was waiting for the pool to settle. They told us the pool settled in the late teens."

"It didn't."

"It did. It settled at a quarter of what hers had been. And hers, by then, had also settled. She stopped doing the wall. The wall went cold. We bought a stove. The stove was the stove my father fitted, which, when my father went, was the stove the household had, and the stove was the stove."

He drank.

"I did not become angry," he said.

"No."

"I am not, in the way of a person who you would expect to be in this work, an angry person. I learned, somewhere, that anger doesn't build things."

He looked up.

"I think you know that sentence."

Ash held his cup.

He held it the way a man held a cup when he had heard a thing his mouth had said in the year, in his head, walking past Hellen's house and at the long cold table in his parents' kitchen and on the towpath two nights ago. He had not, in the year, said the sentence to anyone but Haran. He had not, in his life, said it to a person who had said it back to him.

"I know it," Ash said.

* * *

They reached the diagnosis.

They reached it the way two people who have built, separately, the same building, reach the building. Davan said *the decline is not natural,* and Ash said *no,* and Davan said *the institutions were not built to ask the question of fairness,* and Ash said *they were built to keep doing what they did,* and Davan said *yes,* and they were, by the second hour of the talking, walking the same hallways through the same building and naming the rooms.

The rooms were the elders.

Davan said the elders were not malicious. Ash said no. Davan said the elders had inherited a system that warmed them and were continuing to be warmed by the system that warmed them, and had, in their way, mistaken the warmth for the world. Ash said *yes.* Davan said *and the institutions know.* Ash said *they know and they cannot say they know, because saying they know is, for a Council, a different speech than the speech they have permission to give.* Davan said *Dorenne.* Ash said *Dorenne sees the problem more clearly than anyone in the chamber and has decided that the management of the broken system is the only available politics.* Davan said *yes.* Davan said *and the management is brilliant.* Ash said *it is brilliant.* Davan said *and it is not enough.*

"It is not enough," Ash said.

It was the first time he had said it aloud.

He had said it on the towpath two nights ago. He had said it in his head at the basin. He had said it the way a man said a thing he could not yet, in the year, allow into the room with Dorenne, which was the room he had been in for a year, which was the room every Council letter and every reconstruction order and every committee assignment had been a chair in. He said it, this evening, on the crate, with his cup half-finished, with Haran winding wire eight feet away, with a man across the bench who had walked through his work for an hour and had asked the right questions and had said *yes* point for point.

It was a small thing to say. It cost him.

The cost was the cost a sentence had when the sentence had, until the saying, been a sentence the speaker had allowed himself the comfort of not having said. The comfort was over.

Davan registered.

He did not, in the registering, do the thing a lesser interlocutor would have done, which was move forward. He let the sentence be the sentence. He drank tea.

After a long time, Davan said:

"You lived without magic for twenty-three years. You know what's coming for everyone. Why aren't you angry?"

Ash held the cup.

He looked, for a beat, past Davan, at the back wall, at the under-shelf, at the cloth he had laid that morning over the things on the shelf. He looked at the cloth without seeing it. He looked back.

"I was angry," Ash said. "For twenty-three years."

"Mm."

"Being angry didn't build anything."

A pause.

The pause was the pause of two people each hearing what the other had meant. Ash heard, in his own sentence, the shape of the thing he was telling Davan, which was that anger was, in his life, a diagnosis and not a treatment, and a diagnosis you carried for twenty-three years was a diagnosis you stopped expecting to act on, and the stopping had been, when the Wellspring opened, a habit that had become a person.

He heard, in Davan's silence, what Davan had heard in the sentence.

Davan had heard *I am telling you patience is the answer.* He had heard the powerful man telling the powerless man to wait. He had heard, with the precision of a man who had spent six years listening to powerful men, the small clean form of the sentence the powerful had been giving since there were powerful, and he had heard Ash, because he was honest, refuse to dress it up.

Davan did not say any of this.

He said:

"Twenty-three years is a long time to be patient."

"It is."

"Patience is not the same as lack of anger."

"No."

"You are still angry."

"I am."

"Mm."

He drank.

"What did your anger build."

Ash looked at the bench.

"It built," he said, "the sentences I'm using now."

* * *

They did not resolve it.

They did not need to. They sat. Davan asked about Haran's wire. Haran, without turning, said *clock spring,* and went back to the winding. Davan asked Ash about the cistern indicator. Ash answered. Davan asked about the second feeder's pressure curve. Ash answered. They went back to the work the way two people went back to the work after a thing had been said that would, later, be the thing both remembered the afternoon by. The work was a relief. The work was, between them, the place they could both still stand.

Liss came back at the seventh hour.

She came in with the ledgers tucked under her elbow, which she had, by some agreement Ash had not heard, gone and got from the back bench at the half-hour and had taken away to read in the bakery. She set them on the bench. She did not sit.

"Korel's at Building Six," Liss said.

"Mm."

"He'll be another hour."

"Mm."

She looked at Davan. The look was the look of a lieutenant who had read enough ledgers to have a question that was, on the way back, larger than the ledgers.

"The eastern row," she said. "Insulation. It's better in the south buildings."

"Yes."

"Why."

"We did the south first. The wrap technique improved by the third building. We haven't gone back to redo the northern."

"Cost."

"Cost."

"Mm."

She nodded. She did not, in the nodding, soften.

"I have notes," she said. "I'll send them."

"Send them."

"Mm."

She stood at the bench for another beat. She looked at Haran. Haran did not look up. She looked at the pot.

"There's tea," Haran said, without turning.

"Thank you."

She poured the cup. She drank it standing. She drank it in three swallows, the way a person drank tea they had not stopped to decide whether they wanted, and put the cup down.

"Davan."

"Liss."

"Korel."

"Tell him the hour."

"I'll tell him."

She went out.

* * *

Davan stayed.

He stayed past the seventh hour. The light at the high window had gone gold and then thin, and Haran had lit the lamp on the bench at the small hour Haran lit the lamp, which was the hour the brass on the bench began to look the wrong color, and the lamp made the bench the bench it was at the end of a workshop day. Davan did not, at the lighting, stand.

"I should go," he said.

He said it without standing. He said it the way a man said *I should go* fifteen minutes before he stood up. Ash registered the fifteen minutes. He had been, for fifteen minutes, going to say it himself and had been waiting for Davan to say it. The waiting had been mutual. Neither of them had wanted, by going first, to make the going first the answer to a thing.

Davan stood.

He stood the way a tall man stood after sitting on a low crate for two hours, slowly, with one hand on the bench, and he stretched, slightly, once. He looked at the bench. He looked at Haran. He looked at Ash.

"Thank you," he said.

"You're welcome here."

"I'd like to come back."

"Come back."

"I'd like to bring someone. A woman who runs a kitchen in the canal quarter. She has questions about heat I don't have answers for."

"Bring her."

"Mm."

He picked up his cap.

At the door he stopped. He stopped with the cap in his hand and his other hand on the frame the way Ash had stood at the frame at the fourth hour, and he turned a quarter. He did not, in the turning, look at Haran. He looked at Ash. He spoke, and in the speaking his voice came out at the pitch the voice on the cart had carried to the eastern wall of the Cooperage two nights ago, which was a pitch that did not have to be loud to fill what it was filling.

"The problem with building for the future," Davan said, "is that people are dying in the present."

He paused.

"Your pump works. It is also a year away from helping anyone who is not in this district. What do they do until then?"

He did not, in the asking, make the asking sharp.

He asked it the way he had asked the question of the lift at the pump, and the question of the wrap at the riser, and the question of *who taught Jora.* He asked it as a question whose answer he wanted, not as a sentence whose effect he was measuring. The effect was the effect anyway. The sentence was a sentence. It was the sentence the platform had been written to put under a Council seal, in this room, in this workshop, against this bench, on Ash, in the fifth hour of an afternoon in which Ash had agreed with everything else.

Ash stood.

He stood from the crate and he had, in standing, his hand on the bench, the way Davan had had his hand on the rail of the cart on Second-day. The bench was beech. The bench was warm where the lamp had been lit beside it. He felt the grain of the bench under his palm. He felt the cool of the metal vise at the heel of his hand.

He did not have an answer.

He had the answer he had thought, on the towpath, in the dark, two nights ago. The answer was *they wait, because the alternative is worse.* He had thought it the way a man thought a thing he had agreed with and did not, in the agreeing, feel saved. He could say the answer. The answer would be true. The answer would also be, in this room, in his mouth, against this bench, the sentence the powerful gave the powerless when the powerful had decided. He had heard the sentence in his head a hundred times. He had hated the man who said it.

He did not say it.

"I don't have a good answer," he said.

Davan nodded.

"I know."

He held the nod a beat. He set the cap on his head. He pulled it down. He turned.

"Goodnight, Haran," Davan said.

Haran, at the back bench, with the wire on the former and the lamp at his elbow, did not look up. He nodded the quarter-inch.

"Mm," Haran said.

Davan went out.

The door closed.

* * *

The workshop was quiet.

Ash stood at the bench. His hand was on the bench. The lamp was on the bench. The cups were on the bench, three of them, two empty and one half-full where Davan had left it. The brown pot was beside the cups. The pot was cold.

Haran wound wire.

He wound it for the count of forty. The wire turned on the former with the small clean sound the wire made. He set the former down. He did not, in the setting down, turn.

"So," Haran said.

Ash did not answer.

He looked at Davan's cup. The tea in the cup had a small skin where it had cooled. He registered the skin. He registered, beneath the skin, the dark of the tea, and beneath that, the bottom of the cup, which was glazed white and had a small chip at the rim Haran had not, in twenty years, replaced.

"He listens," Haran said.

"He does."

"Mm."

A pause.

"He's right about the boys," Haran said.

"He is."

"Mm."

Haran did not, in the *mm,* say the next thing. The next thing was the thing he had not said since the morning, which was the thing he had been not-saying for two days, since the broadsheet, since the rally. The next thing was the thing Ash could feel against the back of his attention the way he could feel the held weight of the reservoir against the back of his attention.

He did not ask Haran what the next thing was.

He was, he registered, afraid to.

He turned the tap on the small basin in the corner of the workshop. The water came. He rinsed the cups, one and two and three. He set them on the rack. He picked up the pot. He emptied the pot into the basin and rinsed the pot and set it on the shelf. He picked up the file from the back bench. He set it in the file's place on the front bench, in the small leather roll, in the slot the file had been kept in for forty years. He laid the leather roll closed.

Haran wound wire.

"He'll be back," Ash said.

"He will."

"Mm."

He stood at the bench a long minute.

He looked at the under-shelf, at the cloth, at the shape of the things under the cloth, and he did not lift the cloth, and he turned, and he went up the stair to the room above the workshop.

He lay on the bedroll. He did not light the lamp.

The reservoir sat. He registered it. He had not spent, in the day. He had stood at the door at the fourth hour. He had walked across a square. He had laid a hand on a wall the way a man laid a hand on a wall who wanted to know whether the wall was warm. He had answered, on the bench, the questions of a man who had asked the right questions. He had agreed. He had agreed, in the second hour, with the diagnosis. He had said *it is not enough* aloud for the first time. He had not, at the door, had an answer.

The dark windows of the canal quarter were dark.

He thought *I could warm them.*

He thought *and I have not.*

He thought *because the pump is the answer I built, and the pool is the answer Davan built, and I cannot, this evening, give him the pool because giving him the pool is giving him the prescription, and the prescription will not work, and the not-working is the math at three percent in twenty winters.*

He thought *and the people in the dark windows have to be warm in this winter, not in twenty.*

He thought it together, the two thoughts, the way a man thought two things that were both, simultaneously, true.

He did not sleep for a long time.

When he did, he slept the way a man slept who had, in the day, met someone he had been waiting to meet, and had found the meeting was not the relief he had imagined, and was, instead, the small clean opening of a door at the end of a hall he had been walking down for a year without noticing the hall was a hall.

Below, in the workshop, Haran kept winding wire.

The lamp burned.

The brass blank was on the bench, finished, and the brown pot was on the shelf, and the cloth was over the under-shelf, and the door of the workshop was, in the way Haran left the door at the eighth hour when there was a thing on his mind, unbolted.

He did not, by morning, bolt it.

He did not, by morning, say what he was not saying.

He sat at the bench with the wire and the former and the lamp at his elbow, and the workshop was the workshop.

The dark windows of the canal quarter were dark.

The pump in the square was warm.
