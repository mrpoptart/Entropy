# Chapter 15

The forge was cold when he came in, and Haran was at the bench with the wire.

Ash hung his coat on the peg. He had walked up from the lower terraces in a thin morning, the kind of morning that did not commit to a weather, and the walk had been a walk, and he had asked at three doorways and fixed nothing, and the not-fixing had set in him the way a held breath set, somewhere under the collarbone, in a place that was not yet pain.

"Tea," Haran said.

"I'll do it. Yeah, I'll, sorry, I've got it."

"Mm."

He went to the small stove in the corner. The kettle was where the kettle was. He filled it at the basin and set it on the cold ring and stood with his palm a finger's width from the base of it, the way a person stood when he was about to channel and was choosing whether, and he stood that way a count, and then he channeled, and the kettle began to warm.

He felt the warming in his hand before he heard it. He felt it as a small shift in the air around the ring, a thinning of the cold, and he held the channel and the kettle ticked once at the seam where the metal had cooled overnight and was warming unevenly, and the ticking was the small sound a kettle made on a stove, and he held the channel until the water was the temperature he wanted, and he stopped.

The ring under his palm was warmer than the kettle.

He had known this for years. He had not, until this morning, named it. The ring was warmer because the channeling was loose, because the energy he had pushed into the kettle had bled out of his hand as it went, and the bleed had warmed the ring and the air and the cuff of his sleeve, and only some fraction of what he had spent had gone into the water that was the thing he had meant to warm.

He poured two cups.

He set Haran's at his elbow without saying. He took his own to the high stool at the corner of the bench and sat with the cup in both hands and looked at the forge.

The forge was cold.

It would not be cold long. Haran would light it when the wire was where Haran wanted the wire, and the lighting would be the lighting, and Ash had, over the months he had lived in the back of the workshop, watched Haran light the forge on the order of two hundred times. He had watched it the way a man watched a thing that was happening near him while he was thinking about something else. He had watched it as a forge being lit.

He had not, except once, watched Haran.

* * *

He had been thinking, for three weeks, about a number.

The number was the reservoir. The number was thirty-seven, give or take, the way a number was give or take when the man holding it had no instrument and only the long sense of what was inside him and the comparing of one day's sense to the next. Thirty-seven, this morning. Thirty-six and a half, maybe, by the small measure he did not entirely trust.

The number went down whether he spent or not. It went down slower when he did not spend. It went down faster when he did. The slope of the line was the slope of the line, and he had drawn the line on a sheet of bench paper a week ago, in a corner of the workshop, with a stub of charcoal, and he had folded the sheet and put it in his coat pocket and not taken it out since, because the line did not need to be looked at again to be known.

The line ended.

It ended at a date he could put a thumb on. Not next month. Not next season. But not in any year he could imagine being old in.

Against the line, he had set the other numbers. The other numbers were the city. They were the heating lattice in the upper terraces, which Ryn had walked him along, and which a man who came when he came was supposed to maintain, and which would not be maintained by that man and would have to be maintained by somebody. They were the channeled mains in the second district, where the woman with the bucket had filled to three quarters because the carrying was easier. They were the press where Hira channeled eight hours and came home to a cold flat. They were the south block pump, where Branner was at the handle of a system Ash had built brilliantly and that did not include Branner's voice in the building of it.

They were dozens of districts.

They were rural communities Haran had told him about over winter, in the corner of an evening, in the way Haran told him things, which was the way of a man who left a thing on a bench and went on with his work and let the thing be picked up or not.

He had set the line against the numbers and the line was too short.

It had been too short when he had been at forty-four. It was too short now at thirty-seven. It would be too short at twenty-five and at fifteen and at any number he could draw the line down to, because the slope went one way and the city did not get smaller. Working harder did not move the slope. Being smarter did not move the slope. He had spent six months working harder and being smarter and the slope was the slope.

He drank the tea.

The tea was hot and the cup was warm in his hands and the warm was a thing his hands took, and Haran had not looked up from the wire, and the not-looking-up was the not-looking-up of a man who knew Ash was sitting across the bench thinking and was leaving him to it.

* * *

Haran finished the wire.

He set the small pliers in the roll and rolled the roll closed. He stood. He arched his back the small careful way he arched it now in the mornings, and Ash saw the arch, and the arch was the arch of a man who had been at a bench since before the second hour and was sixty-eight, and Haran did not, in the arching, perform anything.

"Forge," Haran said.

"Mm."

Haran went to the forge.

He took the cover off the firebox and set it against the leg of the bench. He laid in the kindling the way he laid in the kindling, in the small cross at the bottom, and then the larger sticks, and then a piece of last winter's split oak that had been drying under the eaves and that Haran had picked yesterday because today was the day the oak had been ready. He laid it in the way a man laid in a fire he had laid in five thousand times.

He put his hand near the kindling.

Not on it. Near it. A finger's width from the dry tinder at the base of the cross, where the smallest pieces met, and his hand was open, palm down, the way a hand was when a man was warming it at a fire that was already lit.

Ash watched his hand.

He did not watch the fire. He did not watch the forge. He watched the hand and the air around the hand and the place where the hand was a finger's width from the tinder, and he watched the way a man watched when a man had decided to watch.

The tinder caught.

It caught the way tinder caught, in a small thread of smoke that twisted out of the cross and lifted and was a flame, and the flame was the flame, and the flame took the kindling, and the kindling took the larger sticks, and the larger sticks took the oak, and the oak began to burn.

Haran took his hand away.

Ash had been holding his cup. He set it down. He set it down without looking at it, and the cup made the small sound a cup made on a bench, and Haran heard the sound and did not turn.

The thing he had watched.

The thing he had watched was that the air around Haran's hand had not changed. It had not warmed. There had been no shimmer, no bleed, no bloom of waste heat the way there was around any channeling Ash had ever watched, his own or anyone else's, in a kitchen or a yard or a hall. The hand had been near the tinder and the tinder had caught and the air around the hand had been the same air as the air across the room.

The expenditure had gone into the tinder.

Only into the tinder.

A whisper. Where any trained channeler in the city would have used a voice and warmed a room, Haran had used a whisper and warmed a thread of dry grass at the base of a cross of kindling, and the thread had been enough, because a thread was what a fire needed, and Haran knew exactly what a fire needed.

Ash had glimpsed this once before. He had glimpsed it in a pump repair in the lower terraces last autumn, in the brief moment Haran had set his thumb to a corroded fitting and the corrosion had eased, and Ash had thought at the time that he had been mistaken about what he had seen, because what he thought he had seen was not possible at the cost of a thumb. He had not, then, asked. He had let it be a thing he had not quite seen.

He looked at Haran.

Haran was at the bellows, working it slow.

"How do you do that."

He said it without choosing to. He said it before he had decided how he was going to say it, and the saying came out lower than he had meant and more bare, and the bareness was the bareness of a man asking a question he had carried into a room without knowing he had carried it, and Haran heard the bareness, and Haran's hand stopped on the bellows handle.

Haran looked at him.

He looked at him a count. His face did not do anything. Haran's face did not do anything in the way a face did not do anything when the person behind the face had been waiting some unspecified time for a question and was now hearing the question and was deciding what to do with the hearing.

"Do what."

"The forge. The, you know. Lighting it. The thing you just, "

"Mm."

"You used almost nothing. I mean. I was watching."

"Mm."

"I've watched you light it a hundred times."

"More than a hundred."

"I never, "

"No."

"I never watched it. I mean, I've, sorry, I've been in the room, but I haven't, "

Haran let the bellows handle go.

He turned from the forge. He went to the small stool at the end of the bench and sat down on it, the way he sat when he was going to sit a count, with his hands loose on his knees and his head tilted a finger toward the ground, and he did not, in the sitting, perform anything.

"Thirty years of practice," Haran said. "Give or take."

"That's not, that's, I mean, that's not, "

"It is."

"Haran."

"It is, Ash. It's thirty years."

He said it the way he said a thing he had decided some time ago was the case and was not going to soften, and the not-softening was not an unkindness, and Ash heard the not-softening and did not push.

"Can you... yeah, walk me through it. Sorry. From the top."

Haran looked at him.

"You sure."

"Yes. I mean. Yeah. Please."

"Mm."

* * *

Haran got off the stool.

He went back to the forge. The forge was burning now, the oak settling into the kindling, the heat coming up through the firebox the way heat came up when a fire had taken, and Haran did not stoke. He pulled a length of iron rod out of the rack at the side of the bench, a piece the thickness of his thumb and the length of his forearm, and he set it on the lip of the firebox so that the end of it lay across the coals. He waited a count for the coals to take the iron. He turned the rod a quarter.

"Watch," he said.

"Yes, I, I think so, I mean I see what you're, yeah, no, the rod, okay."

"Not the iron. Me."

"Okay. You. Yeah."

He set his palm against the rod where the rod lay cool, near the end he was holding, six inches up from the coals. He held his palm there a count.

"Most channelers," he said, "when they want to bring this to working temperature, they push. They push from the hand into the rod and the rod warms and they keep pushing until the rod is hot, and they pull their hand back when the rod is hot, and they call that channeling."

"Yes, that's, I mean, that's what I do. That's what I've been, yeah."

"Watch what they're doing while they push."

"All right."

"They're pushing into the rod. They're pushing into the air around the rod. They're pushing into the tongs, if they're holding tongs. They're pushing into the bench they're leaning on. They're pushing into the floor under their feet, if their feet are firm. They're pushing in every direction the energy will go, because they have not, in the pushing, decided where the pushing is to go. They have decided what they want done. They have not decided what they are doing."

Ash watched.

Haran's palm was against the rod. The rod was beginning to warm at the end in the coals, the natural warming of iron set across a fire. The cool end where Haran's palm was had not changed.

"The energy goes where it can go," Haran said. "Heat, by its nature, goes outward. A channeler who pushes without aiming pushes outward. The aiming is the work."

"Yes."

"The discipline is, you find the thread."

"The thread. Okay. The, yeah."

"The thread of what the work needs. The smallest path from your hand to the result. Most people don't look for it. Most people don't know it exists. They were taught to push. They push."

He held his palm against the rod.

"Watch."

The rod warmed.

It warmed under Haran's palm in a way Ash had not, in his life, watched iron warm. It warmed at the place under the palm and along the line of the rod into the coals, in a single thread, the way a wick took a flame. The air around the rod did not change. The bench did not warm. Haran's sleeve did not warm. The palm warmed because the palm was on the rod and the rod was warming, and that was the only warming.

The rod went from cool to working temperature in a count of fifteen, maybe twenty.

Haran took his palm away.

"There," he said.

Ash did not, for a count, speak.

He looked at the rod. The rod was orange at the end in the coals and red along the working length and dark again at the place six inches above where Haran had set his palm, and the line of the heat was a clean line, as if a man had drawn it.

"How much, I mean, how much did, "

"Mm."

"How much did that cost you."

Haran considered.

"A breath."

"A breath. That's, sorry, that's, "

"Less than warming a cup of tea would cost you."

"A rod that size. Working temperature. And, sorry, just, that, "

"Yes."

"That's, "

He stopped.

He stopped because the number he was about to say was not a number he had a name for. He had warmed iron at this workshop. He had warmed iron because Haran had asked him to, in the early months, when Haran had been showing him the bench and the forge and the basic uses of metal in the shop. He had warmed a rod this size to working temperature once, last summer, and the warming had cost him a thing he had felt in his chest, a small and definite expenditure, the kind that registered against his pool the way a weight registered against a hand.

What Haran had just done had cost a breath.

"How," he said.

"I told you. The thread."

"That's not, "

"It is. It's the thread. There's no trick. There's the thread and there's everything else, and most channelers spend most of their pool on everything else. Sixty out of every hundred. By the rough count."

"Wait, sorry. Sixty? Sixty out of a hundred. That's, I'm sorry, that's, I'd been thinking it was maybe ten, fifteen, I mean I knew it was something but I didn't, sorry, keep going."

"Give or take. That's the figure I came to. It's not exact. Nobody measures it. You learn it by feel, the way you learn most things by feel. Most channelers waste sixty out of every hundred on what isn't the work."

"Sixty."

"The traditions weren't built on saving. They were built on having. The reservoir was deep. People poured. Why wouldn't they. The pour worked. The job got done. You went to bed and your pool came back. You poured again."

"Yes. Yeah. Of course they did."

"Nobody optimized. There was no reason to optimize. The first generations who might have had a reason to optimize were the ones who started running short, and they were the wrong ones to teach themselves a discipline, because they were tired."

"Yes."

"I'm one of them. I started thirty years ago because I had been short for ten years before that, and the shortness was not getting better, and I had a long time to think about it at a bench by myself, and I thought."

"Yes."

"It took twenty of those thirty years before I trusted what I was finding. The first ten years I thought I was fooling myself. I would run a thing and feel it had cost less and not believe my own feeling. I would do the same work the next day and feel it had cost the old amount and decide I had been wrong the day before. The instrument is your own body and your body lies to you. It takes a long time before you stop arguing with it."

Ash stood at the corner of the bench.

He did not, in standing, channel. He did not, in standing, do anything. He held the cup of tea that had gone cool in his hand and he watched the rod begin to dim where Haran had taken his palm away, and the rod dimmed the way a piece of iron dimmed when it was off the heat, and the dimming was the dimming, and he did not think about the dimming, because his thinking was not at the rod.

His thinking was at the line on the bench paper in his coat pocket.

His thinking was at the slope.

If the slope changed, the line changed.

If the slope went from the slope it was to a slope that bled at half the rate, the line ran out twice as far. If it bled at a fifth, five times as far. If it bled at what Haran was doing, at the breath where a voice would have gone, the line went somewhere the line had not gone in any drawing he had drawn.

He felt the relief before he felt anything else.

It came up under his ribs like a hand pressing a bruise from underneath, the kind of pressure that was not pain but was the absence of a pain he had been carrying so long it had become the shape of him, and the absence was a vertigo, and the vertigo was, for a count, the only thing in the room.

He set the cup down.

"You should know," Haran said, "what your choices are."

He said it without looking at Ash. He said it at the rod, with his hands loose at his sides, in the voice of a man putting a thing on a bench and going back to his work.

"Yes."

"That's all I'm telling you. You should know."

"Yes. Okay. Yeah."

"Try it."

"Now. I mean, you want me to, now."

"When else."

* * *

He went to the forge.

He stood where Haran had stood. He took the rod out of the coals with the tongs and laid it on the brick at the side of the firebox to cool, because Haran had said *try it* and the rod that was already warm was not the rod to try it on, and he wanted, in the trying, a cool rod and a clean attempt.

He pulled a fresh rod from the rack.

He laid it across the coals the way Haran had laid his. He set his palm against it six inches up from the coals, where Haran's palm had been, and he stood with his palm against the rod and tried to find the thread.

"I'm gonna get this wrong the first time, I just want to say that before I do it, but, okay, watching."

He could not find the thread.

He knew what the thread was. He had watched Haran for the count of fifteen and he had seen it, the way a man saw a thing once and would, after, know the shape of it, and he had the shape of it in his mind. The shape was a line. The line went from his palm into the rod and along the rod into the coals, and it did not branch, and it did not bleed.

He could not, with his channeling, find the line.

He pushed.

He pushed the way he pushed when he warmed a kettle, and he tried, in the pushing, to narrow. He tried to send the push along a line and not into the air around the line, and he tried it the way a man tried to walk a thread he could not see, and he narrowed, and he narrowed, and he narrowed, and the rod did not warm.

Nothing warmed.

His palm was on the rod and his pool was registering a small steady drain, and the drain was going somewhere, and nowhere he could find, and the rod was cool under his palm, and he held the channel a count more, and the rod was still cool, and he stopped.

"Mm," Haran said.

"It's not, "

"You over-narrowed."

"Yes."

"The thread isn't a needle. It's a thread. It has a width. You closed it down past width."

"How do you, "

"You feel it. There's a place where it's narrow and the work is moving, and a place narrower than that where it's narrow and the work isn't moving. They're a hair apart. Try again."

He tried again.

He opened the channel a hair. He did not know what a hair was. He guessed at a hair, and the guess was a guess, and he pushed, and the pool pulled, and he felt the pool pull harder than it had pulled the first time, and the rod did not warm, and the rod did not warm, and then the rod warmed, suddenly, all at once, in a way that was not a thread.

The forge flared.

It flared the way a fire flared when a draft caught it, except there had been no draft, and the flare came up out of the firebox in a blue-edged tongue that licked over the lip and along the iron of the bench and scorched the wood in a long mark the length of his forearm before the flare collapsed back into the box, and Ash pulled his palm off the rod and pulled his hand back from the bench and stood with his hand at his chest, and the forge settled, and the rod was orange where it had been cool, and the bench was scorched, and the smell of scorched wood was in the air.

Ash's pool registered the loss.

It was not a large loss. It was a loss he could put a thumb on, the way he could put a thumb on the loss of a kettle, the way he could put a thumb on the loss of warming a room. It was three or four times what the work had needed. It was, he understood, on the order of what he would have spent doing the same job by his own usual method, which was to say that the attempt at precision had cost more than the imprecision it had been trying to replace.

Haran reached past him with the corner of his sleeve.

He brushed a flake of ash off his cuff. He did it the way a man brushed a thing off his cuff, without looking, without commentary, with the small reflex of a man at a forge who had been brushing flakes off his cuffs for forty years.

"Mm," he said.

"I, "

"Try again."

He tried again.

He tried it five times. The third time the rod warmed, properly, in a thread, for a half count, and the warming was the thing he had been reaching for, and his chest moved at the warming, because he had felt it, he had felt the line, he had felt the energy go along the line and into the iron and not into the air, and the feeling had been distinct, and the feeling had been right.

The thread closed before the rod was warm.

He pushed to reopen and the push was wide and the rod warmed wide, and the bench warmed under the rod, and his cuff warmed, and the thread was gone. He held the channel anyway and pushed at his ordinary width, and the rod came to working temperature, and the working was a working he had done before, and the cost of it was the cost of it, and the cost was the cost he had been paying his whole short channeling life.

He pulled his hand back.

"Mm," Haran said.

"I had it. I mean. For a, yeah. I had it."

"You did."

"For half a count."

"Yes."

"Then I lost it."

"Yes."

"How do you keep it. I mean. Once you've got it, how do you, "

Haran did not answer at first.

He went around to the other side of the bench. He picked up the rod with the tongs and looked at the working length and set the rod back in the coals at a different angle. He was not thinking about Ash's question. He was thinking about the rod. After a count he looked up.

"Thirty years," he said.

It was not unkind.

It was the thing it was. It was a number that was a number, and the number was the time it had taken him, and the time it had taken him was not the time it would take Ash, and was not less than that, and was not necessarily more than that, and was the only honest answer Haran had.

"Yes," Ash said.

"You were born stronger than I was. You may find the thread faster than I did. Or slower. The pool doesn't tell you. The pool doesn't help with this."

"No."

"The discipline is its own thing."

"Yes. Okay."

"You can practice it. You can practice it every time you channel. You can practice it warming the kettle in the morning. You can practice it lighting a candle. You can practice it sealing a fitting. Every channel you do is a chance. Most of them you'll lose. Some of them, after a long time, you'll keep."

"Yes."

"That's all I have to tell you."

He went back to the bellows.

He worked it slow, and the coals took the air, and the rod in the coals went from orange to deeper orange to the red-orange that was working temperature, and Haran picked the rod up with the tongs and brought it to the anvil and began to work it, and the working was the working, and the rhythm of the hammer was the rhythm of a man who had hammered this iron at this anvil at this temperature in this rhythm for forty years.

Ash stood at the corner of the bench.

He stood with his hand at his side. He looked at the scorch on the wood in front of him. The scorch was the length of his forearm and the color of old tea at the deepest part, and the wood at the edges of the scorch had not gone, and the scorch was a scorch the bench would carry from now on, and Haran would not, he knew, sand it out.

He went back to his stool.

He sat. He picked up the cold cup of tea. He did not drink it. He held it in his hands.

* * *

Haran worked the rod.

He worked it for the better part of an hour. He drew it long. He doubled it back. He worked the seam closed and reheated and worked the seam again. He did it the way he did it. He did not, at any point in the working, channel. The work was hammer and tongs and fire, and the fire was the fire he had lit with a breath, and the rod was the rod he had warmed with a thread, and the rest of the work was the rest of the work, and the rest of the work had not changed.

Ash watched.

He watched the way he had not watched before, which was with the question still in him and the question shaping what he was looking at. He watched Haran reheat, and he watched, in the reheating, the same thread, the same absence of bloom in the air around the iron, the same precision that had been the precision the first time. Haran was not, in the reheating, performing the precision. He was reheating. The precision was the way Haran reheated.

He watched the bellows.

The bellows did not channel. The bellows pushed air. The bellows were a tool that did the work air did, and the air took the heat to the coals, and the coals took the heat to the iron, and the chain of work was, in Haran's shop, almost entirely the chain of work that had been the chain of work in any shop a thousand years ago. The channeling was the smallest thing in the chain. It was the breath at the start.

Tesha had asked him a question.

She had asked it at the next bench, weeks ago, in the south block, and he had not answered, and she had not asked again, and the not-asking-again had, on the walk with Ryn the morning after, been a thing he had carried into the talking.

*Why can't we do both.*

He had not, in the days since, found the answer.

He found the shape of it, now, watching Haran. Not the answer. The shape. The bellows pushed air. The hammer struck iron. The fire took the kindling. The channel, when it came, was a thread. The work was a thousand things that were not the channel, and the channel was a small bright part of the thousand things, and the channel was small because the thousand things were the work, and the work was the work whether the channel was small or not.

You did both because the both was the work.

You found, in the work, the smallest place the channel needed to be. Then you put your channel there. Then you let everything else be the work it was.

The shape was a shape. It was not yet an answer.

He set the cold cup down.

* * *

Haran finished the rod.

He laid it on the cooling rack at the end of the bench. He wiped his hands on the rag at his belt. He looked at the bench and at the scorch and at the rod and at Ash, and he did not, in the looking, make any of them a thing.

"I'm going up."

"Mm."

"You'll bank the forge."

"Yes."

"There's stew on the stove."

"Yes. Okay."

"Eat the stew."

"Yes. I will."

He went out of the workshop.

He went up the stair the way he went up the stair, which was slow and even, and his weight on the second step made the second step talk the way the second step talked, and the talking went up the stair after him, and the door at the top opened and closed, and Haran was in the upper rooms.

The workshop was quiet.

Ash sat on the stool. He sat with his hands on his knees. He looked at the forge.

The forge was not banked. The forge would be banked when he banked it, and he did not, for a count, bank it. He sat. The coals settled in the firebox in the way coals settled in a firebox at the end of a working day. The forge was a low warm thing at the corner of the room, and the warmth of it came across the floor at the level of his ankles, and the rest of the room was the rest of the room, and the rest of the room was cool in the way an old shop was cool at evening.

He took the bench paper out of his coat pocket.

He unfolded it on the bench in front of him, smoothed it with the side of his hand, and looked at the line.

The line was the line he had drawn. It started at forty-four, where he had been at midwinter. It came down through the months in the small steps he had marked, and the steps were honest, and the line met the bottom of the page at a place his thumb had been on, before, more than once.

He took the stub of charcoal from the edge of the bench.

He drew a second line.

The second line started where the first line was now, at thirty-seven, the place he was today. It came down at a slope that was half the slope of the first line. He drew it without checking. He drew it the way a man drew a thing he had been drawing in his head for the count of an hour. The second line, at half the slope, went to the edge of the page and did not yet end, and he did not draw past the edge, because past the edge was a thing he did not yet know how to draw.

He looked at the two lines.

He looked at them a count he did not count.

The relief came back.

It came back the way it had come back at the forge, in the moment Haran had said *you should know what your choices are*, in the under-the-rib press of a hand that was lifting a weight he had not known he had been carrying as a weight. The relief was enormous. The relief was the largest thing he had felt in a year. The relief was bigger than the relief at Maren's table when the stew had been in front of him. It was bigger than the relief at Ryn's gate when Ryn had said *not nothing*. It was a different kind of relief, and it was, he understood, more dangerous than any relief he had felt before, because the other reliefs had been about people, and this one was about a number.

The number was the line at the bottom of the page.

The number was the date he had been able to put a thumb on.

If he learned the discipline, the date moved.

He sat with that.

He sat with it in the cool of the shop, with the forge low at the corner of the room and the bench paper on the bench in front of him, and he sat with it the way a man sat with a thing that had been the worst thing in his life and was, for a count, not the worst thing.

He could see the path.

It was a long path. He was nowhere near Haran. He had failed five times this morning and one of the five had been a half-count of finding the thread, and the half-count had been the nearest he had come, and any honest count of where he stood put him at the start of a road thirty years long. He would not, in any year he could imagine being old in, be where Haran was. He would, he thought, with practice, with time, with the consistent application of the discipline to ordinary work, get to half. Maybe a third of the way. Maybe further. He could not, sitting on the stool, know.

But he could see the slope move.

He could see it move because it had moved, this morning, in a half-count at the rod. He had felt the thread. He had felt it for half a count and had lost it and had spent the next four attempts not finding it again, and the not-finding had been failure, and the failure had been the failure it was, and he had also felt it. He had felt it. The feeling was the thing. The feeling was the place where the path started.

If he practiced.

If he practiced the discipline on the kettle in the morning. If he practiced it on the stove in the evening. If he practiced it on the small repairs in the workshop. If he practiced it not in the south block, not in the upper terraces, not on the lattice and the mains and the pumps and the press and the cold flat where Hira held a baby in her coat against the brick across the lane. If he practiced it on himself.

If he stopped channeling at the scale he had been channeling at.

If he stopped building.

The slope moved.

The line on the page got longer. The line got long enough that he could imagine being old in the year it ended in. The line got long enough that there might be a year in it where the discipline had grown enough that he was capable of building again, more carefully, more cheaply, with more left over. The line got long enough.

He could stay extraordinary.

He sat with that.

He sat with the word. He had not, until this moment, named the thing he had been carrying since the math scene in his father's kitchen the summer the Wellspring had opened in him. The thing had been a fear. The fear had been of a number coming down to nothing. The fear had been of being, after the number came down to nothing, what he had been before the number had been there at all, which was a man with an empty pool in a city of empty pools, a man with no special claim on any room he walked into, a man whose name was a name and not a thing people looked up at when he came down a curve in the morning.

A man who was ordinary again.

He had carried the fear for a year and a half. He had carried it in the way a man carried a thing he could not put down because he had not known he was carrying it, and the carrying had shaped his shoulders and his sleep and the way he stood in a doorway, and he had not, until this morning at the forge, looked at the carrying.

He looked at it now.

The discipline was the way out. Not today. Not in any month he could draw on a page. But over years, with practice, with the patience he had not, in his life, been a man of, the discipline meant the line did not end. The discipline meant the number did not come down to nothing in a year he was likely to be alive in. The discipline meant he could, at some price he was willing to pay, keep being the thing he was now, the thing the city looked up at in the morning, the thing whose name was not a name but a kind of weather.

He could keep being that.

He just had to stop pouring the reservoir into the city.

He looked at the bench.

He looked at the scorch the length of his forearm and the cold cup beside it and the bench paper with the two lines, and the room was the room, and the forge was low at the corner of the room, and the warmth of the forge came across the floor at the level of his ankles, and beyond the warmth the shop was cool.

He looked at the second line.

The line was beautiful. It was beautiful the way a thing was beautiful that gave a man back a piece of his life he had decided he was going to lose. He looked at the line and his chest did something his chest had not done in a long time, which was open, which was the small involuntary opening of a man who had been holding his breath in a way he had not known he was holding it and was, for the first time in a long while, not.

He sat in the open chest a count.

He let the relief be in him.

He did not, in the count, push it away. He did not, in the count, decide. He let the relief be in the room with him, the way a man let a thing be in a room with him because the thing was in the room and pushing it out would have been a smaller honesty than letting it stay.

It was a real choice.

It had been months since he had had a real choice. The choice with Dorenne had been a choice and it had also not been a choice, because by the time she had asked him he had already known what he was going to say. The choice with Davan had been a choice and it had also not been a choice, because by the time the brass cap had been on the bench he had already left the room. The choices in the south block had not, most of them, been choices, because by the time he had heard the question he had already started building. He had been carried, for a year and a half, by the slope of his own want.

This was different.

This was a thing he could choose. He could practice the discipline and he could stop building and he could, at the cost of letting the city be the city it was without him, let the line at the bottom of the page move. Or he could keep building. He could keep pouring. He could let the line end at the date he had put a thumb on, and he could spend the time before the date doing the work the city needed, and at the end of the date he could be, for whatever was left of his life, ordinary again.

The choice was a real choice.

He sat with the realness of it.

The forge settled. A coal cracked at the edge of the firebox and a small spark went up and went out before it reached the height of the bench, and the dark of the room came in a finger toward the firebox as the firebox dimmed, and the cold came up through the floor a finger from the corners.

He looked at the bench paper.

He folded it.

He folded it on the original creases, in thirds, and put it back in his coat pocket. He stood. He went to the forge and took the cover from where Haran had set it against the leg of the bench and laid it across the firebox in the way Haran laid it, and the cover settled, and the forge banked, and the warmth at the level of his ankles began, slowly, to go.

He stood at the forge a count.

He did not channel.

He could have channeled. He could have practiced the thread now, alone, on the cool kettle on the stove or on the candle on the bench or on his own cold hand. He could have started. The discipline was, Haran had said, in every channel he did, and he could begin it any time he chose to begin it.

He did not begin it.

He stood at the forge and he did not begin it, and he did not, in the not-beginning, decide. The not-beginning was a not-deciding. The not-deciding was a thing he was holding, in the cool of the shop, the way a man held a coin in his palm before he turned it over.

He went to the stove.

The stew was warm. Haran had left the lid on. He took a bowl from the shelf and ladled the stew into it and took it to the bench and sat on the stool and ate. He ate slow. He ate the way he had eaten at Maren's, which was the way of a man who had decided to eat. He ate the bowl. He set the spoon in the empty bowl. He set the bowl on the bench.

He looked at the dark forge.

The forge was dark.

The forge would be cold by morning. Haran would come down and lay in fresh kindling and bring a piece of last winter's split oak from under the eaves and put his hand a finger's width from the cross of tinder, and the cross of tinder would catch, and the air around Haran's hand would not change. The morning would be a morning. The work would be the work.

He would, in the morning, watch.

He would watch the thread again. He would, by his own choice or not, keep watching it, because the thread was now a thing he had seen, and seen things did not unsee. He would carry it down the curve when he went down the curve, and across the lane when he crossed the lane, and into the south block if he went to the south block, and into Ryn's yard at the third hour on Tuesday when the third hour on Tuesday came. He would carry it into every channel he did. He would feel for it at the kettle. He would feel for it at the candle. He would feel for it at the small repairs.

He would feel for it.

That was not the question.

The question was the other one.

He sat on the stool with his hands on his knees and the empty bowl on the bench and the forge dark at the corner of the room, and he sat with the second line on the folded paper in his coat pocket, and he sat with the relief in his chest, which had not, in the count of his eating, gone away, and which was, he understood, not going to go away. The relief was the relief. The relief was the size it was. The relief was, he felt now in the cool of the shop, going to be in him a long time.

He looked at the dark forge.

*He just has to stop building.*
