# Chapter 11

The stairs to his parents' flat were the stairs to his parents' flat, and Ash took them at the long stride of a man who was already late.

Workshop on his hands. He could smell it on his cuffs. Pitch and lath-dust and the iron tang the cistern bolt had left on his thumb where he had pulled the wrench out of Reska's hand at the eleventh hour and the wrench, in the pulling, had bitten. He had not washed the thumb. He had washed the rest. The thumb he had registered at the basin, decided was clean enough, and forgotten about by the second flight.

The eight-sheet drawing was in his head. The third joint was still wrong. Pell had said so at the morning meeting and the saying had been right, and the redraft Ash had been working at the bench when his mother's note had come in the boy's hand had been the redraft of the wrong joint, and the drawing was, on the way up the stairs, doing the small particular pull of a thing not yet put down.

He put it down.

He put it down at the second landing, on the windowsill, the way a man set a tool he was not going to use this evening on a shelf where he would find it in the morning. He stood for a beat. He looked at the dirty pane. The lamp from the inner court below put a yellow square on the wall.

Then he climbed the last flight.

The door was not latched. It was never latched at the dinner hour. He pushed it open and the kitchen came at him in the way a kitchen came at a man who had been gone three weeks: bread, onion, the small woody smell of the heating lattice his mother had still not, in eight months in this flat, fully trusted, the way a bird did not, in eight months, fully trust a new branch.

"Ashlyn," his mother said.

She said it from the stove. She said it across her shoulder, with her hands in a bowl, and she did not turn until she had finished what her hands were doing, which was the cumulative unhurry of a woman who had heard his step on the stair before he had reached the landing and had, in the hearing, already started the next thing.

"I'm late," Ash said.

"You're here."

"I am."

"I'm putting the bread on the board. The soup's been on since the fourth hour. Your father is in the chair."

"Mum."

"Wash your hands."

He washed his hands.

The basin was the new basin. The new basin was not the basin he had grown up with. The new basin had a tap that drew from a lattice fed reservoir on the roof, and the tap turned, and the water came, and his mother had, in the eight months, made small concessions to it: she filled the kettle from the tap rather than from the rain barrel below stairs because the rain barrel was now four flights down and her hip would not, she had told him once, do four flights twice. The kettle on the stove was, however, the dented kettle. The dented kettle had come from the old apartment. The dented kettle had come through the earthquake. The dented kettle was on the stove, and the new copper one Maren had brought in the first month was, as it had been every time he had been to the flat, in the cabinet behind the salt jar.

He scrubbed. He got most of the iron off the thumb. He left the rest.

"In a minute, Rendell," his mother said, to no one.

His father did not answer.

His father was in the chair by the window, in his stockings, with the small leather case of files open on the side table and a half-filed striking-piece in his hand. Ash registered the striking-piece. It was, by the look of it, the striker for a flint-and-steel his father had been mending for a neighbor in the third week. The neighbor's striker. Three weeks. His father had taken three weeks on a striker because his father, at his bench, was not in a hurry, ever, about anything.

"Ash," his father said.

"Da."

"Sit when she's ready."

"Mm."

His mother set the bread board down on the table.

The bread was the round loaf. The round loaf was the loaf she made on Fifthdays. She made it on Fifthdays because Maren came on Sixthdays, traditionally, and the loaf's second day, sliced thin and toasted, was the loaf Maren ate. The loaf had not, for three months, gone two days. Ash had not been in the flat for three Sixthdays. Maren had not, that he knew of, come on a Sixthday since the seventh week of the season. The loaf today was the loaf for tonight.

She did not say so.

She put it on the board. She put the knife beside it. She put the soup in the pot on the trivet on the table, because the table, in this flat, would not take a hot pot directly, and she would not, in eight months, ask the lattice to spread the heat across the surface of the table the way the housing manual had told her she could. The trivet went down. The pot went down. The lid came off. The smell of the soup came up.

"Sit," she said.

They sat.

* * *

His father said grace the way his father said grace, which was the small breath taken before the bread, and not a grace at all, and Ash had eaten across this small breath at this kind of table for twenty-three years and he registered it the way a man registered the threshold of his own door, which was not at all, and entirely.

His mother poured.

His mother poured the soup. She poured his bowl first. Then his father's. Then her own. She did not pour a fourth.

The fourth bowl sat at the fourth place, empty, with the spoon laid across it. The fourth place had been laid. The fourth place was always laid on a Fifthday, on the chance, and the chance had been thinner than the loaf was thick for three months.

"Eat," she said.

Ash ate.

The soup was the soup. The soup was lentils and the harbor's onion and a piece of the smoked shoulder his mother got from the man at the corner of the sixth lane who got it from his cousin who came up from the harbor on Threedays. The soup was the soup he had eaten, in a different kitchen, in a different flat, in a different season, on Fifthdays, since he was four.

"Tova's boy," his father said.

"Mm?"

"Brought the note."

"The boy who came to the workshop."

"Mm."

"He's nine."

"Ten."

"Ten." Ash registered the correction. His father made the correction the way he made every correction, which was the way a man set a brick a quarter-inch left of where the apprentice had set it, without comment. "Reska's boy."

"Reska's boy is nine," his mother said, from her bowl, without looking up. "Tova's nephew is ten. The boy who came was Tova's nephew. He had the cap with the patched brim."

"Mm," said his father.

"It was the patched-brim cap," she said, and she said it gently, and she went on with her soup.

A pause.

His father did not, in the pause, defend the nine. His father took the bread. His father broke the bread the way his father broke bread, which was a half-turn at the far edge with the thumb on the near edge, and the half-loaf came away with the small ridge where the crust had set, and his father handed Ash the larger piece without looking up.

"There's a man," his father said.

"Mm."

"In the flat below."

"Mm."

"Took a hammer to the lattice plate in his kitchen on Threeday."

His mother did not look up.

"I said *there's a man,*" his father said.

"I'm listening."

"He took a hammer," his father said, "to the lattice plate. Four blows."

"Four," his mother said.

"On the third blow he got through the casing. On the fourth he bent the bus bar."

"And."

"And the lattice on his floor went out. And the lattice on our floor stuttered. And the lattice on the third floor, where the Hessens are, stayed up because the Hessens' kitchen is on the other riser. And the housing inspector came on Fourth-day and shut the building's main feed for two hours while they cut the bus bar back and patched it."

"Mm," Ash said.

"The man's name is Toller."

"Toller."

"He's been in the building," his father said, "since the first month. He came over from the lower row when they pulled the lower row off the lattice and put the lower row on the new pumps. He has, by the count of the woman across the landing, broken three things in this flat in eight months: the heating plate, the wash basin tap, and the lamp in the back hall. He broke them with his hands."

"Mm."

"On purpose?" Ash said.

His father took a spoon of soup. He held it. He set it back.

"I asked him."

"You asked him."

"On Threeday evening. On the landing. He was in the landing with his hand on the rail and he was, in the hand on the rail, a man who had taken a hammer to a thing in his own kitchen and was, in the after of it, standing on his landing with his hand on the rail."

"What did he say."

His father broke a piece off his bread.

"He said," his father said, "*it didn't take the heat right.*"

"Mm."

"He said it three times. The third time he said it, he said it different. The first time it was about the lattice. The second time it was about the lattice. The third time it was about." His father stopped. He set the bread on the edge of the bowl. "Like a chair."

"A chair."

"Like a chair you've sat in for thirty years and one morning you sit in it and the seat is wrong. The seat hasn't moved. You've moved. But you can't sit in it. Not the way you sat in it on Tenday." His father looked, for the first time at the table, at Ash. "He didn't say *I sat in it wrong.* He said *the seat is wrong.* He took the hammer because the seat was wrong."

"Mm."

"Your mother brought him a loaf on Fourth-day."

"I brought him a loaf," his mother said. "I did not bring him a loaf for the lattice. I brought him a loaf because his wife works the late shift at the laundry on the harbor and his daughter is at her aunt's this week and there was no one in the flat to make a loaf, and a man who has just had two hours of his power taken away and the housing inspector at his door for an afternoon does not need to be asked, in addition to the rest of it, what he wants for supper."

"You brought him a loaf."

"I did."

"He took it."

"He took it. He said thank you. He said it twice. He has not, since Fourth-day, taken a hammer to anything else, and I do not, in any direction, expect that this will hold, but the loaf was the loaf."

A pause.

His father picked up the bread again.

"Like a chair," he said.

His mother did not answer.

She did not answer because the answer was on the table already, in the empty fourth bowl with the spoon laid across it, and they had not, any of them, spoken about the bowl, and they were not, this evening, going to.

Ash ate his soup.

* * *

She came at the bottom of the seventh hour.

He heard her on the stairs before the door opened. He heard her because she was, on the stair, taking it the way his mother took the stair after the fortieth hour of the week, which was to say she was taking the rail. Maren did not take the rail. Maren took stairs the way Maren took everything, which was at the pace she had set for them and not the pace they had set for her.

The door opened.

"Maren," his mother said.

"Mum."

"You're late."

"I am."

"Sit. There's soup."

"In a minute."

She came to the table the way Maren came to a table, which was to say without ceremony, with her coat off in the small motion of a coat coming off a person who knew exactly how the coat hung on the peg, and she came and she did not sit. She stood at the back of the chair at the fourth place. She put both hands on the back of the chair. She held it.

Ash looked at the hands.

The hands were Maren's hands. The hands were the hands he had watched seal a hairline crack in the courtyard wall when she was nineteen and he was twenty-one, with a draw so small the wall had warmed the half-inch of the seam and the morning's frost on the bricks two feet either side had, in the warming, not melted. The hands were not, this evening, the hands he had watched seal the crack.

The hands were holding the chair.

She was using the chair.

She was using it the way a person used a thing without intending the using to read, and the using read.

"You walked," his mother said, from the stove, where she was already on her feet for the soup.

"I walked."

"From the corps."

"From the corps."

"That's an hour."

"It's forty minutes if you don't stop."

"And if you stop."

"It was an hour."

His father did not, at the table, look up.

His mother poured the fourth bowl. The soup steamed into it. She set the bowl at the fourth place. Maren took her hands off the chair and sat. She sat the way a person sat who had been on her feet for too long and was, in the sitting, finally allowing the sitting to be the sitting. She did not, in the sitting, sigh. The not-sighing was the registered thing.

"Tea?" his mother said.

"No."

"Mm."

His mother went back to the stove.

She did not, at the stove, do anything. She stood at the stove with her hand on the dented kettle, which was on the back ring, which was not hot, because the soup was on the front. She stood with her hand on the kettle and her back to the table for the count of three, which was the count his mother stood at the stove with her back to the table when she was, at the table, registering a thing.

"I will, actually," Maren said.

"Mm?"

"The tea. Yes. Please."

"Mm."

His mother set the kettle on the front ring. She moved the soup to the back. She did not, in the moving, draw on the lattice. She used the trivet handle and her good hand and she moved the pot the way she moved the pot on Fifthdays, which was the way she moved everything when there was, at her table, a thing to be registered without naming, and she registered the thing by not, this evening, channeling for it.

The soup steamed. The kettle began the small low sound a kettle began before it sang.

Maren put her spoon in her bowl.

She did not, at the first spoonful, eat. She set the spoon down. She picked the bread up. She broke the bread the way Maren broke bread, which was a clean break with the thumb at the near edge, and the break was the break, and she set the half on the edge of the bowl, and she picked the spoon up again.

She ate.

She ate slowly. She ate the way a person ate when eating was a thing the body had been refusing for the part of the day before the table, and the table was, in the sitting at it, the permission. Ash watched her eat without watching her eat. He watched her by looking at his bowl and seeing, in the corner of his eye, the small steady mechanical lifting of her spoon, six lifts, seven lifts, the eighth lift slower, the spoon in the eighth lift not making the small clean tap on the rim of the bowl that Maren's spoon always made, because the spoon, on the eighth lift, was held a fraction less firmly.

His mother brought the cup.

His mother brought the cup before the kettle had fully sung. She had put the leaves in the cup. She had poured the not-quite-boiled water over them. She had set the saucer under the cup and she had, in the setting, not used a tray, because she did not, on family nights, use a tray, and she set the cup at Maren's right hand the way she had set tea at Maren's right hand for nineteen years.

Maren wrapped both hands around the cup.

Both. She put her spoon down to do it. She wrapped both hands around the cup, not the way a person held a cup that was hot, because the cup was not yet hot, but the way a person held a cup that they wanted, in the next minute, to be hot. She did not raise the cup. She held it on the saucer. Her palms were on the ceramic. The ceramic was warming, in the way ceramic warmed under tea, slowly, from the inside out.

A minute went by.

His mother sat. His mother had not, all evening, sat through her own soup, and she sat now. His father broke another piece off the loaf. The loaf was, by Ash's count, almost gone. His mother had not made a second loaf. His mother had made the loaf for tonight.

"Work," his father said, mildly, the way a man cued a daughter to talk who was, this evening, not going to start.

"Mm," said Maren.

"How is it."

"It's." A pause. "I'm." Another pause. "We're shifting the southern manifold's draw schedule. The corps has me on the redraft. I'm, ah. I'm." She picked up the cup. She did not drink. She put the cup down. "I'll have a better sense by Tenday."

"Mm."

"It's good to be on a redraft. I like the redraft work."

"Mm."

"Ash," she said.

Ash looked up.

"How's the workshop."

"Eight sheets," Ash said. "Wing nut. Flange. Pell says the third joint is hard."

"Mm."

"He's right."

"Pell."

"Pell."

"The canal-quarter fitter."

"Yes."

"Reska likes him."

"She does."

"Mm."

She picked up the cup. She raised it. She drank. The drinking was a sip. The sip was the sip of a person whose throat, this evening, was working on a delay, and she set the cup down without comment, and she put both hands around it again.

His father told the rest of the Toller story.

He told it the way his father told a story when his father had decided the table needed a story to be a table, which was to say he told it without rushing, with a return at the end to the loaf his mother had brought, and a small, dry observation about the housing inspector's mustache that made his mother make the small specific sound she made when his father had landed a line, which was not a laugh and was, from his mother, what a laugh was.

Maren held the cup.

Both hands. The whole of the dinner.

Ash registered each of his mother's small, mechanical not-channelings: the kettle moved by hand, the trivet by handle, the salt passed across the table not floated, the lamp on the high hook lit, before any of them had sat, by the long taper from the box, not by a draw. His mother did not draw at the family table when one of her children was, at the table, in any visible kind of state. She had not drawn at the family table on the night Ash had been turned out of the apprentice list at sixteen. She had not drawn at the family table on the night Maren had taken the corps placement at twenty-one and the corps had assigned her to the lower city, twenty stops from the flat by lattice tram. She did not draw at the family table this evening. Ash registered the third instance the way he had, at sixteen, registered the first.

His father wound down the story.

"Anyway," his father said. "The mustache."

"Yes," said his mother.

"The mustache was the mustache."

"It was."

"Mm."

A pause.

Maren's spoon was in her bowl. The bowl was three-quarters full. She had stopped eating somewhere in the middle of the Toller story and she had not, since stopping, started.

"I'll clear," she said.

"Sit," his mother said. "I'll clear."

"I'll clear with Ash."

His mother's hand stopped on the table. Her hand stopped a half-second longer than the not-stopping would have stopped it. Then her hand picked up the bread board.

"Mm," she said.

* * *

The kitchen in this flat was small.

Smaller than the kitchen in the old flat, where two people could pass at the basin if both turned sideways. In this flat the basin was set into the corner with the cool cupboard at the elbow and the stove on the long wall, and the working space was the long bench under the high window, and two people in the kitchen stood, by the geometry of it, at right angles.

He stood at the bench with the cloth.

She stood at the basin with the bowls.

The basin's tap was the tap. The tap drew on the lattice. A person could turn the tap and the water came, as the water came from any tap in the building, and a person could, if a person had a working pool and the inclination, draw a pool of water into the basin directly, which his mother did not, ever, do, and which Maren had done at this basin every Fifthday for eight months.

She set the first bowl in the basin.

She put her hand on the rim.

She closed her eyes.

He saw it from the corner of his eye, with the cloth in his hand, looking down at the bench. He saw her close her eyes. He saw her draw.

The pool came.

The pool came the way a pool came from Maren's hand, which was small, controlled, exactly the volume of the basin. It came and it sat. It sat in the basin a half-second. Then it stuttered.

The stutter was not a thing he had a name for.

The pool made a small sound, which was not a sound, which was the absence of the sound a pool of water made when it was, in being held, held. The surface of the water shivered. It shivered the way a glass shivered on a table when a heavy cart passed in the lane below, which was the way a thing shivered when something that had been holding it had, for a heartbeat, not held it. Then the holding came back. The pool sat.

He saw her jaw set.

He saw it in the corner of his eye. He did not turn his head.

She washed the first bowl. She washed it slowly. The water ran over the bowl and the soap took and the bowl came clean, and she set it on the rack at her left, and she did not, in the setting, look at him.

She picked up the second bowl.

She put her hand on the rim of the basin. She drew. The pool emptied. She drew the second pool. The second pool came and the second pool stuttered earlier than the first, and the stutter was longer, and the surface of the water was, in the second stutter, not a surface for a count of two. Then it was. She washed.

Her hands were shaking.

It was a small shake. It was the shake of a hand that had spent the day at the very edge of what the hand could spend, and was, at the basin, on the wrong side of the edge. He could see it because he had, for twenty-three years, watched his sister's hands not shake. He could see it the way he could see, in a gear train he had been around for a year, the one tooth that was a hundredth of an inch shy.

He picked up the cloth.

He picked up the first bowl from her rack.

He dried it.

He dried it the way his father dried, which was thumb at the rim, cloth over the back of the hand, two passes inside, one pass out, the bowl on the shelf with the lip turned. He dried the second. The second came over from her rack as she was finishing the third. He dried the third when she set it. He did not, in the drying, look at her.

He did not say anything.

He dried.

Five bowls. The pot. The four spoons. The trivet, which had not needed washing but which she washed anyway because washing it was, in the geometry of the kitchen, the kind of small extra thing a person did when the doing of small extra things was the only available form of not-saying. The kettle she did not wash. The cup she set in the basin last, the cup she had held both-handed all of dinner, and she washed it slowly, and the stutter on the cup's pool was the stutter that did not, this time, come back.

The water dropped.

It dropped a half-inch in the basin. Not all the way. Half. She had been holding a basin's worth of pool and the pool had, mid-wash, slipped a half-inch's worth, and she had caught it, the way a person caught a tray that was sliding, with the heel of the hand and the small reflex of the arm, and the pool had held the rest of the cup.

Her jaw went hard.

She washed the cup.

She set it on the rack.

She let the basin go.

The water in the basin let go the way a held pool let go, which was a small drop in the surface and a small dimming of the cling at the edge, and the water became the water in a basin that was not being held, and it sat the way water in a basin sat, and she pulled the stopper, and it ran.

Ash dried the cup.

He set it on the shelf with the lip turned. He laid the cloth on the bench. He picked it up again. He folded it. He laid it down.

She had her hands flat on the bench.

Both hands. Wrist to fingertip, palms down, on the long bench under the high window, the way a person bracketed themselves with their own hands. She was breathing the small particular breath of a person whose body had just been asked, for the basin, more than the body had wanted to give, and was, on the bench, recovering.

He stood at the cloth.

He did not move.

He had, three years ago, in the old flat, watched his sister come in from the corps at the seventh hour on a Threeday after a sixteen-hour shift on the canal lift's main draw, and he had watched her come in, and she had set her cap on the peg, and she had said, *I'm going to lie down for ten minutes,* and she had lain down, and she had been up at the eighth-and-half. He had not, that evening, registered it the way he registered, this evening, the bench.

He had not, that evening, had the comparison.

He had, this evening, the comparison. He had it in the corner of his eye. He had it in the half-inch the basin had dropped. He had it in the eight lifts of the spoon and the mechanical not-tap on the rim. He had it in the rail on the stair.

She turned her head.

She did not turn her body. She turned her head, with her hands still on the bench, and she looked at him.

"Don't," she said.

He turned the cloth in his hand.

"Don't what?"

"Whatever you're about to say. I know what you're noticing. Don't."

He held the cloth.

The cloth was the cloth. The cloth was not, in his hand, a thing he had been intending to do anything with. He laid it on the bench. He laid it the way his father laid a tool he was, in the laying, deciding not to use. He laid it down.

He did not say the thing he had been about to say.

He had, in the truth of it, not been about to say a thing. He had been standing with the cloth in his hand and he had been not-saying a thing, and the not-saying had been audible to her in the way the not-saying had always been audible to her, since she was four and he was six, since they had been the two children in the house under the listening of two parents, one of whom had built a life on the saying-of-things and one of whom had built a life on the listening, and the not-saying between the two of them had, since then, been a third language they shared with no one.

He nodded.

He nodded the small quarter-inch his father nodded at the bench, which was the nod of a man receiving a thing.

She looked away.

She looked at the bench. She let her hands rest. She let them rest a count of ten. The breathing came down. The shake in the wrist did not, in the count of ten, leave, but it became the smaller shake of a hand that had returned to the band of the day's steady tiredness from the band of the day's edge.

She lifted her hands off the bench.

She picked up the cloth.

She wiped the bench down. The bench did not need wiping. She wiped it. She wiped the two-foot strip under the high window, the bench her hands had been on, and she folded the cloth twice and laid it across the rim of the basin, and she walked out of the kitchen.

Ash stood at the bench.

He laid his palm on the wood.

The wood was warm where her hands had been.

He stood the way he had stood at the bakery wall on Tenday with his hand in the pocket of his coat, and he stood for a count he did not, in the standing, count, and he registered the warmth, and he registered that this evening, in this kitchen, he had not done a thing, and that the not-doing was, in his hand, the harder version of the not-doing he had begun to learn.

He went out to the table.

* * *

She was at the door with her coat.

"You don't have to walk me," she said.

"I'm going."

"You're not going my way."

"I'm going partway your way."

"Ash."

"I'm going partway your way."

A pause.

"Fine," she said.

His mother was at the table, with her hands on the back of her own chair, in the way his mother stood at the table when she was, at the table, not yet deciding whether to sit. His father was in the chair by the window, with the striker in his hand again, with the small file on it, with the lamp at his elbow set, this evening, by the long taper.

"Mum," Maren said.

"Mm."

"Soup was good."

"It was the soup."

"It was good."

His mother nodded.

She did not, in the nodding, put a loaf into Maren's hand. She had, before they had come out of the kitchen, wrapped the heel of the loaf in the brown cloth and set it on the side table by the door. The heel of the loaf was on the side table. Maren did not pick it up. She did not, in the leaving, refuse it. She left it on the table the way a person left a thing whose taking was, this evening, more than the body would carry.

His mother registered the leaving.

She did not say anything.

Ash put his coat on.

"Da."

"Ash."

"I'll be back on Sevenday."

"Mm."

"For the morning."

"Mm."

His father did not look up from the striker.

His father's hand, on the file, was steady. His father's hand, on a file, had been steady at this kind of work for forty years. Ash registered, on the way to the door, that his father had not, all dinner, done the thing his father did when a thing at the table was being not-said and his father wanted to know if Ash had registered it, which was the small grip on the shoulder when Ash passed the chair. His father did not grip his shoulder this evening. His father had registered that this evening was not the evening for the grip on the shoulder. His father was leaving the grip for an evening when the grip would be the smallest part of what was being asked.

The lack of the grip was the registration.

Ash followed Maren out.

* * *

The stairs going down were the stairs going down.

She took them the way she had not taken them coming up, which was at her own pace, with one hand light on the rail because the rail was there and not because she was using it. The hand on the rail was, Ash registered, a courtesy to him. The courtesy was that she had decided, in the kitchen, that he was going to walk her, and that she was not, in the walking, going to make a thing of taking the stair the rail-way in front of him.

The hand was on the rail anyway.

He registered it without registering it.

They came out into the lane.

The lane was the lane at the eighth hour in the eleventh week of the season. The cold was in the cobbles. The cobbles were in the cold the way cobbles were in cold, which was to say the cold sat on them and any heat a foot put down on them was, in the third step, the cold's heat. The lamp at the end of the lane was lit. The lamp on the corner of the lane and the bridge street was lit. Between them there was the dark a man walked through with a person who was, for the walk, not going to talk, and they walked through it.

She walked at her pace.

Her pace was not, this evening, her pace. Her pace was the pace of his mother on the bridge in the eighth month, which was a pace Maren was not, by twenty years, supposed to walk at. He matched it. He matched it the way he had matched it for his mother, on the bridge, in the eighth month. He had, for the matching, the body. He had spent twenty-three years walking at a pace a working pool did not, in the city, set, and the pace at which Maren walked this evening was, in a way he had not seen until this evening, his pace.

He did not say so.

The bridge street came up. The corner where she would turn east for the corps quarter and he would turn west for the workshop. They stopped at the corner. The corner-lamp was at her shoulder. The light on her face was the lamp light. Her face was the face of a woman who had walked twenty minutes in the cold at her mother's pace and was, at the corner, holding it.

"Ash."

"Yes."

"I'll get a tram."

"Mm."

"From the corner of Sixth."

"Mm."

"Don't walk me to the tram."

"I won't."

A pause.

She looked at him.

She looked at him with her hands in her coat pockets and the corner-lamp on the side of her face, and the look was the look his mother gave a thing on the table that needed to be picked up and could not, this evening, be picked up, and the look was, on Maren, a look he had not seen on her before the dinner, and was not, after the corner, going to be a look he would unsee.

"Sevenday," she said.

"Sevenday."

"With Mum."

"With Mum."

"Tell her I'll come."

"Tell her yourself."

A pause.

"I'll come," she said.

She nodded.

She turned. She walked east on the bridge street, at the pace she had walked the lane. He stood at the corner with his hand on the lamp post. He watched her to the second lamp. At the second lamp she did not turn back. She did not turn back because turning back would have been, on her side of the corner, the thing she was not, this evening, going to do, and he stood at the corner with his hand on the post until the second lamp took her out of it.

Then he went west.

* * *

He walked.

He walked the way he had walked back from Building Six with the small twenty-minute warmth of a thing fixed in his hand and the thing in his chest that was not warmth, except this evening the thing in his chest was not the thing in his chest he had walked back from Building Six with, because the thing in his chest this evening was older.

He thought about twenty-three years.

He thought about them in the way a man thought about a thing he had been in the middle of for so long that the middle had been the only place the thing was. He had been twenty-three years without a pool. He had been four when Maren had been born and he had registered, within the week of her birth, that there was a thing in the room with her that the room had not, in the four years he had been in it, contained, and his mother and father had registered the thing in the room and had, in the registering, looked at him, and the looking had been the first moment he had understood that he was, in the room, the absence of the thing.

He had built, since then, a body around the absence.

He had built it in pieces. He had built it in the mechanical intuition that came, by the eighth year, from a child who had to know how a hinge worked because he could not, in the lifting of his hand, soften the hinge. He had built it in the physical strength that came, by the twelfth year, from carrying water from the rain barrel the four flights his mother's hip would not, at the eighth month of any year, do. He had built it in the small private discipline of the thing-said-in-the-mind-and-not-aloud, which was the discipline a child built when the saying-aloud, in the household, would have asked his mother for an answer she did not, on a given evening, have. He had built it in the long walks across the city at the pace of a man who knew, in the not-asking, where every stair in the city was. He had built it in the bench at Haran's, where, by the fourteenth year, he had, in the bench, the part of the body he could not, by the body alone, have built.

He had built a body for the world that had not been built for him.

Maren had not.

Maren had not because Maren had not had to. Maren had been, since the morning of her fourth year, the daughter the family had been allowed to put down. The family had been allowed to put her down because, by the morning of her fourth year, it was clear what she had, and what she had had not required, in the having of it, the kind of arrangement the household had spent four years arranging around him. She had had the pool. The pool had asked nothing of the household it could not, in a normal year, give.

She had built a body around the pool.

She had built it the way a person built a body around a thing the body could rely on. She had built the discipline of the using of it. She had built the small cool economy of the using, which had, by twenty-one, made her the youngest senior in the corps, and which, this evening, was the discipline in the corner of his eye at the basin that had, for the first time in her life, not been enough for a basin.

He thought: *she has none of the body I have.*

He thought: *she has the pool. The pool is the body she built.*

He thought, the way he had thought the sentence about the school in the bedroll above the workshop on the night before Davan came: *I have a body she does not have.*

He thought: *the gift the twenty-three years gave me is the one gift I have that she does not.*

He thought: *the cost of giving it to her is everything she has built her life on.*

He walked.

The cobbles were cold. The lamp on the corner of the workshop street was lit. He went past the bakery. The bakery was dark, the way the bakery was dark at the eighth hour, with the boy gone home and the door bolted and the long bench under the window scrubbed for the morning. He went past the basin. The basin was empty. Vell was off. The slate was not yet chalked for the morning. He stood at the basin for a beat. He laid his hand on the rim. The rim was cold.

He did not draw.

He did not draw because the basin did not need a draw. He did not draw because the not-drawing was, this evening, the kind of not-drawing that was, in the hand, the practice he had been practicing since Tenday at the bakery wall, and the practice was, this evening, the practice he had brought back across the square from the corner of the bridge street.

He thought: *I am not ready.*

He thought: *neither is she.*

He thought: *the conversation is no longer not-said. It is just not-finished.*

He went into the workshop.

* * *

Haran was at the back bench.

Haran was at the back bench with the wire on the former and the lamp at his elbow, and the bread board was clean on the front bench, with the cloth over it, the way Haran left the bread board on a Fifthday when Haran had eaten alone.

"Mm," said Haran.

"Mm."

He took his coat off.

He hung it on the peg. He stood at the bench. He laid his palm on the beech. The beech was cool. The lamp at the back bench put a small yellow on the front edge of the front bench. He stood for a beat and then he turned and he went up the stair to the room above the workshop and he lay on the bedroll without lighting the lamp.

He thought of his mother.

He thought of her in the kitchen of the new flat at the eighth-and-half hour of a Fifthday in the eleventh week of the season, after the table had cleared, and Maren had gone, and he had gone, and his father had taken the striker into the back room to put the leather case away, and the lamp on the high hook had been the only lamp.

He thought of the cracked bowl.

The cracked bowl had come from the old apartment. The cracked bowl had been the bowl his mother had carried out of the old apartment in a basket, with one hand under the basket and the other on the wall, and the cracked bowl had, in the carrying, been the bowl whose crack she had not, in seven years, repaired, because the crack was a crack she had decided, in the seventh year, to keep. The cracked bowl was on the second shelf of the cool cupboard. She had moved it, in the eighth month, three times. He had watched her, in the third month, take it out of the basket and look at it for a count of ten and put it on the second shelf.

He thought of her in the kitchen.

He thought of her standing at the bench under the high window after they had gone. He thought of her with her hand on the bench where Maren's hand had been. He thought of her with the cracked bowl in her hand, taken down from the second shelf for no reason a kitchen would have had at the eighth-and-half hour of a Fifthday, with the small piece of bread heel in it that she had wrapped at the door and that Maren had not taken, set in the cracked bowl now because the brown cloth was on the side table and the heel had to be somewhere. He thought of her hand on the bowl.

He could see it from the doorway he had not, this evening, gone back to.

She would have stood at the bench. She would have stood with the bowl in one hand and the other hand on the bench. She would not, in the standing, have set the bowl down quickly. She would have stood the count she had stood at the stove with her back to the table when Maren had asked, after all, for the tea. She would have stood the count, with the bowl in her hand, looking at the bench under the high window.

She would not have channeled.

She had not channeled at the family table all evening. She would not channel in the kitchen after the table had cleared. She would warm the heel by the stove the way she had warmed bread for thirty years before the lattice, which was on the back ring with the kettle moved aside, two minutes, turned once, and she would eat the heel. She would eat it standing. She would eat it the way she had eaten things standing for forty years, which was the way a working woman ate a thing whose eating was a thing she did between two things, the way she had eaten a heel of bread standing at a different basin in a different kitchen with her hand on the rim while her son had, in the next room, been four years old and not had a pool.

He thought: *she does not know what to do with her hands.*

He thought it without thinking it. He thought it the way a man saw a tooth in a gear train that was a hundredth shy. He thought it from the bedroll, in the dark, with the workshop quiet below him and Haran winding wire at the back bench, and the thought arrived with the small particular weight of a thing that had been arriving since the night of Maren's tea.

He thought: *the work she did with her hands for twenty-three years was the work of holding the family around me.*

He thought: *the work she did this evening was the work of holding herself in the kitchen because the family was, this evening, holding around Maren.*

He thought: *she does not, after eight months, have a thing to put her hands on that does not, in the putting, ask her to perform a not-needing the household no longer needs.*

He thought of the cracked bowl in her hand and he thought: *the bowl is the thing.*

He thought: *the bowl is the thing because the bowl is the only thing in the kitchen that does not work and is, in the not-working, kept.*

He thought: *she keeps it because it is the one thing in the flat that is, like her, still useful in the way it was useful before.*

He thought it from the bedroll.

The dark windows were dark. The reservoir sat. He registered it. *Thirty-seven.*

He turned on the bedroll.

He thought of the rail under Maren's hand on the stair.

He thought of the cup with both hands around it.

He thought of the half-inch the basin had dropped.

He thought of the heel in the cracked bowl on his mother's bench.

He thought of his father's hand, all dinner, not on his shoulder.

He thought: *not tonight.*

He thought: *but soon.*

Below, in the workshop, Haran wound wire. The wire turned on the former with the small clean sound the wire made. The lamp burned. The door, in the way Haran left the door at the eighth hour when there was a thing on his mind, was unbolted.

Ash lay on the bedroll.

He did not sleep for a long time.

When he did, the last thing he carried into the sleep was not Maren at the basin and not his mother at the bench and not his father with the striker by the window. The last thing he carried was the empty fourth bowl with the spoon laid across it, and the loaf, and the way his mother had set both down without saying that the laying was the laying, and the way the table had let her, the way it always did, do the saying with her hands.
