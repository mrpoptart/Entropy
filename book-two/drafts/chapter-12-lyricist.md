# Chapter 12

The broadsheet was on the workshop bench before the kettle was warm.

Ash saw it the moment he came down the stair from the loft, the corner of it weighted under the small brass file that Haran kept for no reason except that his father had kept one. The paper was the cheap paper. The ink was the cheap ink. The headline was set two sizes larger than the headlines yesterday's paper had carried, because yesterday's paper had carried headlines about a broken main and the price of coal and today's paper carried a headline about three Council seats.

Three. Halver's, Lower Reach, Old Quarry.

He read it standing. He read it again sitting. He read it the third time with the kettle hissing behind him and did not get up to lift the kettle off, and Haran came in at the second hiss and lifted it himself without looking at Ash, and poured two cups, and set one at Ash's elbow, and went on to his own bench.

"You've seen it."

"I've seen it."

"Mm."

The ordinance was further down. Below the fold. Two columns, set in the careful type a printer used when he wanted a thing to look like law and not like agitation. *Designated districts. Elder-generation pool above threshold. A portion daily, scheduled, recorded.* The word *compelled* did not appear. The word *required* did. The architecture of the language was the architecture of a thing that had been written by someone who had read the city's charter the way a stonemason read a wall, looking for the courses.

Ash set the broadsheet down.

He picked it up.

He set it down again.

"Davan didn't write that," he said.

"No."

"Someone who reads charters wrote that."

"Someone Davan found. Or someone who found Davan."

"Mm."

Haran was at his lathe. The lathe was not running. Haran's hand was on the headstock the way a hand sat on a horse's neck, not steering, just present, and his eyes were on the broadsheet at Ash's elbow with the flat patient look that was Haran's reading-face.

"Constitutional," Haran said.

"Modest."

"Modest in the way a first cut is modest."

"Yes."

A silence. Ash drank the tea. The tea was strong. Haran had brewed it strong because Haran had read the broadsheet too and had not said so and had brewed it strong instead, which was Haran's vocabulary for *this is going to be a day.*

"They moved fast," Ash said.

"They moved at the speed they had been moving. We were the ones who thought it was slow."

He went down to the district at the second hour.

The walk was the walk. Down through the upper terraces where the lattice lamps still held, across the cut at the old aqueduct where the stone had been patched in the third month after the quake and the patch had darkened a quarter shade and was beginning to look like the stone around it, and onto the long descending lane that became, at the limestone shelf, Stonecurve. He passed two broadsheets on two walls. Both the same. The headline larger at the second wall than at the first because the second wall belonged to a printer who had set his own type, in his own shop, in a size he had decided on himself. That was a thing. Ash marked it.

In the district the morning was the morning. Tesha had Building Six's pump open at the inspection hatch, two younger workers crouched at her shoulders, Pell's voice carrying from the eastern row where Pell was telling someone, with the patience of a man who had said it three times this week, that you set the gasket dry and you set it slow and you let the bolt find its own seat, and you did not, you did not, channel it. Ash watched a moment from the lane.

He did not go in.

Tesha looked up. She saw him. She lifted a wrench in the small wave that was Tesha's wave for *I see you and I'm working,* and went back to the pump.

He turned uphill.

* * *

The Hall of Infrastructure had the smell it always had, paper and wax and the cold-metal undersmell of the brass fittings on the doors, and at the second landing the smell shifted to the smell of Dorenne's corridor, which was the same smell with the wax stronger because the wax on Dorenne's corridor was applied on a different schedule.

Tessaly was at the outer desk.

"He's here," she said, into the inner office, before Ash had reached her.

"Send him."

He went in.

Dorenne was at her standing desk. The standing desk had been hers since the second month of the reconstruction, since her hip had begun to argue with her at the eighth hour, and she stood at it now with both hands set to the slope of it and her weight on the hands and a survey under her left palm that was the southern aqueduct and a schedule under her right that was the elder-channeler register for Halver's. Both were marked. The marks on the schedule were fresh enough that the ink had not fully set; Ash could see the faint shine of it under the lamp.

She did not turn.

"Ashlyn. Sit."

"I'll stand."

"Sit, please."

He sat.

She finished the line she was making. She set the pencil down. The pencil went into the groove cut for it at the head of the desk, and the going-into was the small careful placement of a tool returning to its place, and she turned.

"Thank you for coming."

"You said come."

"I said *we need to talk.* You came. Thank you for coming."

She did not sit. She crossed to the window and adjusted the angle of the blind by a finger's width and the light in the room moved a half-step and the survey under her hand did not need the move, the move had been for her. She came back to the desk.

"You've read the ordinance."

"I've read it."

"All of it, or the broadsheet summary."

"All of it."

"Of course."

A pause. She let the pause sit. The pause was a working pause; she was, Ash saw, choosing the order. She had three things she meant to say and she was deciding which went first.

"The framework that maintains the southern aqueduct," she said, "depends on twelve elder-generation contributions, scheduled to the quarter-hour, six days in seven. The hospital heating in Lower Reach depends on nine. The structural reinforcement on the eastern terraces depends on six. These are not estimates. These are the schedules I and my staff coordinate every week. The ordinance, as written, removes a portion of those contributions and reallocates them to communal projects in the lower districts. The portion is small. The framework is not built to absorb a small loss in twelve different places at once."

"You'll rebuild the framework."

"I'll rebuild the framework. While the framework is rebuilding, the aqueduct will run at reduced capacity, the hospital will heat by margin, and the eastern terraces will be carrying their own load without the reinforcement schedule for, at minimum, six weeks. At maximum, longer. I do not know how much longer because I do not know what the second ordinance will be."

"There's a second ordinance."

"There will be a second ordinance. There is always a second ordinance."

She came to the desk. She did not lean on it. She rested the fingertips of her right hand on the edge of the survey and held them there and the holding was the holding of a person who had decided not to pick up the pencil yet.

"Ashlyn."

"Dorenne."

"I'd appreciate it if you would consider a public statement."

There it was.

The temperature of the room had not moved. She had said the line at the temperature she said all of her lines, the precise warmth that acknowledged a relationship without inviting expansion, and the line had landed in the room and sat there.

"A statement."

"Against the ordinance. Against the framework's compulsion of magical labor by law. The Wellspring, speaking to the city. Not as my aide. Not as anyone's instrument. As himself."

"As myself."

"As the man who lived for twenty-three years without what these people are being told to demand. The man who knows what it is to be on the other side of the line. If you say that a system that compels expenditure undermines the conditions for the transition you are trying to build, the Council will hear it. The papers will print it. The Levelers will have to answer it, and they will answer it badly, because there is no good answer to *the Wellspring lived your life and disagrees with you.*"

She had rehearsed it.

He could hear that she had rehearsed it. The cadence was the cadence of a sentence she had walked around a room four times before saying out loud, and the placement of *the Wellspring lived your life* was the placement of a sentence she had moved earlier and earlier in the paragraph until it landed where it landed.

She was not wrong about the placement.

"You want me to oppose them."

"I want you to defend the framework that keeps the city standing while you build the framework that will eventually replace it. I want you to give us six weeks. Eight."

"And in eight weeks?"

"In eight weeks I will have absorbed the ordinance, restructured the schedule, and the Levelers will have moved to the next ordinance, which I will have prepared for, because I am not in the business of being surprised twice."

"Mm."

He did not look at her. He looked at the survey on the desk. The southern aqueduct. The marks were the marks of a person who knew the aqueduct course by heart and was checking it against the elder-register because the elder-register had, this week, become a different document. He saw her hand move toward the pencil and not lift it.

"You're not wrong," he said.

"I know."

"The schedule does need eight weeks."

"I know."

"The hospital does need the margin."

"I know what I know, Ashlyn. I am not asking you to tell me I am right. I am asking you to say it where it can be heard."

"And if I don't."

She did not answer at once.

She moved. She crossed to the window again, the same window, the same blind, and this time she did not adjust it. She stood with her back to the room and her hands clasped behind her at the small of her back, and her voice, when it came, came at the same temperature it always came at, which was, for her, an act of will at this hour of this morning.

"Then you're choosing to let the transition framework collapse for the sake of your own comfort with the narrative."

It landed.

The line landed and sat.

He did not answer immediately because the line was not, on its face, refutable. The transition framework was the framework she meant. Her framework. The eight-week framework, the elder-register, the southern aqueduct at its quartered-hours, the hospital that heated by margin. She had used the word *transition* on purpose. She had used it because *transition* was his word, and she was returning it to him with her freight loaded onto it.

He felt the freight settle.

*Comfort with the narrative.* That was the cut. Not *comfort.* Not *narrative.* The two words together. The implication that what he was protecting was a story he liked telling about himself, and that the story was costing the city eight weeks of the hospital's margin.

"I know what you're doing with that sentence," he said.

"I know you know."

"It's a good sentence."

"It is the sentence."

He stood.

He stood not because he was leaving but because he could not say what he had to say sitting. He went to the second window, the one that did not have the blind, and put a hand on the sill, and looked down at the yard where two clerks were carrying a survey roll between them with the careful walk of clerks who had been told the roll mattered.

"I can't stand up," he said, "and tell people who have lived my life that they should be patient."

"That isn't what I'm asking."

"It's what they will hear."

"Then say it differently."

"There is no way to say it differently. The most powerful person alive defending the current allocation is the most powerful person alive defending the current allocation. The framing chooses itself. *I lived without; trust the system that left me without; wait* is the only sentence that comes out of my mouth in that statement, no matter what words I use."

"You underestimate your articulacy."

"You overestimate the audience's patience for nuance from the man with the reservoir."

She turned.

She did not turn fully. She turned a quarter, enough that he saw her in profile against the window, and the profile had the careful set of a person who was not allowing her face to do what her face would have done if she had let it.

"Eight weeks, Ashlyn."

"I know."

"The hospital."

"I know."

"Children at the eastern terraces who are sleeping under reinforcement that you and I both know is the reason the buildings are still standing."

"I know, Dorenne."

"Then."

"I'm not going to do it."

The word *then* sat where she had left it. She did not finish the sentence she had started with it. The unfinished was the tell, and he marked the tell, and he did not point at it, because pointing at it was not what this was.

"I see," she said.

"You don't agree."

"I do not agree. I also see."

She came back to the standing desk. She lifted the pencil. She held the pencil. She did not write with it. She held it the way a person held a tool when the holding was the only motion the body would permit, and her hand, in the holding, was steady.

"You are choosing," she said, "to let the transition framework collapse for the sake of your own comfort with the narrative."

She said it again.

The second saying was different from the first. The first had been the prepared sentence; the second was the unprepared one. She said it the second time at the same temperature and with the same syntax and the difference was not in the words. The difference was that the first time she had been delivering the line and the second time she had been telling him what she believed.

"I hear you," he said.

"I know you do."

"I still can't."

"I know."

A pause.

"I'd appreciate it," she said, "if you would think on it overnight."

"I'll think."

"That isn't yes."

"It isn't yes."

"Of course."

She set the pencil in its groove.

He went to the door. At the door he stopped, because there was a thing he could feel he ought to say, and he could not find the thing. *Thank you* was wrong. *I'm sorry* was wrong. *You're not wrong about the framework* was true and useless. He stood with his hand on the door and Dorenne, behind him, did not fill the silence, because Dorenne had the patience of a person who had been outwaited by walls.

"The aqueduct," he said.

"What about it."

"If I came down and looked at the schedule with you and Tessaly. After. Once you knew the shape."

"You can't reduce the load with hands."

"I can think with you about it. That's what I have."

She did not answer at once.

"I'd appreciate that," she said.

He went out.

* * *

The walk back was a different walk.

He took it long. He went down through the upper terraces and across the cut and down the long lane and at the third broadsheet wall he stopped, because the third broadsheet wall, since this morning, had a list.

Names.

Twelve of them.

The list had been pasted under the ordinance broadsheet by a different hand, on different paper, and the type was a smaller type, and the column was titled in a hand-set wood letter that read *DESIGNATED.* He read the list. Eight of the names he did not know. Two he had heard in passing. Two he knew. *Hennick Vorel, of the upper terraces. Marra Telne, of the Old Quarry.* Marra Telne had channeled the structural courses of the eastern terrace reinforcement schedule. Ash had stood at her elbow at the second month after the quake and watched her seat the iron, and she had told him, without looking up, that she was sixty-one and that her hip was worse than her pool and that she would do what the Hall asked because her sister lived in the building.

A woman was reading the list when he stopped.

She did not see him. She was reading with her finger, the finger moving from name to name in the slow particular movement of a person who was not a fluent reader and was not embarrassed about it, and she said, half aloud, when her finger reached the eighth name, "Hoarder."

She said it like a word she had practiced.

She did not say it angrily. She said it the way a person tested a coin against a stone. The word came out and she looked at it the way she might have looked at the coin, weighed it, set it back in the pocket. She moved her finger to the ninth name.

He went on.

At the lane corner there was a fishmonger's stall. The fishmonger had a stack of broadsheets under a stone at the end of the counter, the way he kept his receipts under a stone, and a woman ahead of Ash in the small line at the stall was buying a length of smoked eel and was talking, while the fishmonger wrapped the eel in oiled paper, about the woman in the next building over.

"She's elder."

"She's elder."

"She's not on the list."

"She's not."

"She should be."

"Mm."

"Won't channel into the cisterns. Won't channel for the lamps. Sits up in that flat with her two cups of tea and her cat and won't lift a finger and the rest of us hauling buckets up four flights."

"Mm."

"Hoarder."

The fishmonger said nothing.

He set the eel on the counter. He took the woman's coin. He gave her change. He set his hand on the broadsheet under the stone and adjusted it so that the headline was square to the edge of the counter, which was a small motion, but Ash saw it, and the woman saw it, and the woman picked up her eel and went.

The fishmonger looked at Ash.

"What can I do for you."

"Two of the small ones."

"Mm."

He bought the fish he did not need and walked on. The paper of the broadsheet stayed at the edge of the counter, square. The word stayed in the air at the corner of the stall, and at the corner of the next stall, and at the corner of the lane where two boys were going past with a bucket between them, and one of them was using it, the word, on the other, in the way boys used words they had heard their mothers use that morning.

*Hoarder.*

The boy laughed. The other boy laughed. They went on with the bucket.

He went on with the fish.

At the entrance to Stonecurve there was a third broadsheet. This one had been pasted three times since the morning, three layers of the same sheet on the same wall, which was the printer's way of replacing a sheet that had been torn down. The top sheet was uncreased. Ash stood at it a moment, his hand on the paper bag of fish, and read the list a second time. The same twelve names. The same letterhead. *DESIGNATED.*

He did not tear it down.

He did not know whether the not-tearing was right.

He went into the district.

* * *

Tesha was at Building Six. The pump was running. The pump was running at the rate the pump ran at when Tesha had set it, which was the eighty-percent rate, and the eighty-percent rate had become, in the four weeks since the redesign, the rate, because nobody in the district was reaching for the hundred and Ash had stopped reaching for it and the eighty was, on its own paper, what the building used.

She straightened when she saw him.

"You bought fish."

"I bought fish."

"You hate fish."

"I bought it anyway."

She wiped her hands on the rag at her belt. The rag was the second rag, the dry one; she kept the first rag at her right hip and the second at her left and the second was for hands she meant to bring to her face. She did not bring her hands to her face.

"You came down through the upper market."

"Yes."

"You saw the lists."

"Yes."

"Mm."

She did not say more. She put the rag back at her belt and turned to the pump and tightened a bolt that did not need tightening, and the not-needing was a thing he saw, and he did not name it.

"Tesha."

"Mm."

"Tonight, after."

"Mm."

"I want to talk a minute."

"Mm. Yes."

"At the end of the shift."

"Yes."

She did not turn from the pump. He left the fish with her, because the fish had been an excuse, and because Pell in the eastern row had three children, and he went on up the lane to the Cooperage.

* * *

The Cooperage yard was full.

It had not been full, the last time Ash had seen it. It had been a yard with a table and a crowd ranged on a yard, and the table had been improvised, and the crowd had stood at improvised distances. The yard now had a platform. The platform was carpentered, three steps up, the boards not yet darkened, the saw-marks at the cut-ends of the stringers still pale. The yard had been swept. The yard had a man at the gate who knew Ash by face and who lifted his chin, not in greeting, in acknowledgment, and stepped aside.

Davan was at the platform.

He was not on it. He was at the foot of it, talking to two organizers Ash did not know and one he did. Korel, the older one, the canal-quarter coat. Korel saw Ash first. Korel did the small thing with his eyebrows that was Korel's *here,* and Davan turned.

"Ash."

"Davan."

"You came."

"I came."

"I'd hoped you would."

He had not said *I hoped.* He had said *I'd hoped.* The contraction was the small private register of a man who, three weeks ago, had spoken to Ash without contractions in public and with them at a workbench, and was, today, in a yard that had become a yard with a platform, still using the workbench register. Ash marked it. He did not know yet what to do with it.

"Walk with me," Davan said.

They walked.

They went out of the yard by the side gate, down the lane that ran along the disused canal, and the towpath was the towpath, and the canal was dry, and a row of weeds had come up at the wall side of the towpath in the four weeks since Ash had last walked it and had been allowed to come up, because the people who walked the towpath now were not the people who pulled weeds.

"Three seats," Davan said.

"Three."

"I didn't expect three. I'd expected one. I'd hoped two. The third was Old Quarry and I'd written it off because the slate was the slate."

"The slate changed."

"The slate didn't change. The voters did. I learned a thing in the last three weeks, Ash. I learned that the voters were ahead of us. We were running behind a conviction the lower districts had already arrived at. We thought we were leading. We were chasing."

"Mm."

"Do you know what that feels like."

"I can imagine."

"It feels like running the wrong race."

"Mm."

They walked. Davan walked with his hands behind his back, a posture Ash had not seen on him before, a posture that had appeared somewhere in the last month, somewhere in the days between the workbench and the platform. The hands were clasped lightly. The clasp was a clasp a man learned by watching other men clasp their hands. Ash had seen Dorenne clasp her hands that way at the window an hour earlier. He did not say so.

"The ordinance," Ash said.

"The ordinance."

"It was written by someone who reads charters."

"Yes."

"Not you."

"No. A woman named Thessa Korven. She read law at the Hall before her pool came in light, then read it again in her own kitchen. She is, by any measure I care about, the most precise mind in the lower city."

"You found her."

"She found us. I will not pretend the finding was mine. She came to a meeting in the second month. She listened. She did not speak. She came back the next week and she had, in a folio, the ordinance."

"Modest."

"Modest is the word she used. *Modest is what gets through. Then you know what doesn't.*"

"Mm."

The towpath dipped. They went down into the dip and up the other side. At the top of the rise the canal widened out into the basin where the second cistern's exchange had failed twelve years ago, and the basin was the basin, dry, the bed of it a pale stained dust, and Davan stopped at the rise and looked down at it and Ash stopped beside him.

"I need to tell you something," Davan said.

"Tell me."

"Three of the men in the yard back there, behind the platform, are men I would not have allowed in a meeting six weeks ago. They are useful now. I have allowed them. I tell you because you will see them and you will recognize them, the type if not the face, and I would rather you heard from me that I know what I am doing."

"What are you doing."

"I am letting in men who will say a harder version of the thing because the soft version stopped working a year before I started saying it. I am letting in men who will print names because the printing is what the elder-generation will respond to, and I have decided, in the last three weeks, that I cannot afford the luxury of refusing to use what works."

He said it evenly.

"Davan."

"Ash."

"The names are on walls."

"I know."

"I read a list this morning. Twelve names. Marra Telne is on the list."

"I know."

"Marra is sixty-one. She has a hip. She channeled the structural courses on the eastern terrace and she did it because her sister lives in the building, and she did it for nothing."

"I know who she is."

"She's not what your printers say she is."

"She is exactly what my printers say she is. She is an elder-generation channeler with a strong pool who has been contributing to the city's reconstruction at a rate well below her capacity, and the list is not a moral judgment of Marra Telne. It is a list of names. It is a list of names because we have spent four years with no list, and during those four years the rate of contribution did not change, and in the three weeks since the lists began, the rate of contribution has changed."

"You're using the word *contribution.*"

"I am."

"There's a different word in the markets."

"I know."

"It's *hoarder.*"

"I know what's in the markets, Ash. I was in the markets last week and the week before and the week before that. I have been in markets for ten years."

"A boy used the word."

"A boy in a market used the word. Yes."

"You taught him the word."

"I did not teach him the word. The word existed. I gave him a list to read it onto."

The wind came up the basin.

It came up the basin the way wind came up dry basins, in a long indifferent push, and the dust at the bed of the basin moved and settled, and the weeds at the wall of the towpath bent and did not break, and Davan stood with his hands behind his back and the wind in his coat and looked down at the dust.

"Davan."

"What would you have us do? Ask nicely?"

He said it without turning.

"We tried asking. We tried organizing. We tried appealing to the Council. I sat in the gallery at nineteen and watched men twice my age tell me that the framework was being *reviewed,* that the *capacities* were being *audited,* that the *next session* would consider the *interim report.* I sat in the gallery at twenty-one and watched the next session table the report. I sat in the gallery at twenty-three and watched the report be referred to a committee that had not met in two years. Nothing changed until we made the powerful uncomfortable. Now things are changing. Are you asking me to stop because the methods are wrong, or because the methods are working?"

Ash did not answer.

He did not answer because the question was the question, and the question had been built carefully, and built with the same care that had built the ordinance, and he could feel the joinery of it in his mouth before he had a word to set against it.

He could see the boy with the bucket.

He could see the woman at the wall with her finger on the eighth name.

He could see Marra Telne at the second month, seating the iron with the careful diagonal she seated iron with, and not looking up, and saying her sister lived in the building.

He could see Tesha, at the pump, tightening a bolt that did not need tightening.

He could see the eighty-percent.

"The methods are working," he said.

"Yes."

"The methods are also working on Marra Telne, who does not deserve to be on a wall."

"Marra Telne can come off the wall. The mechanism for coming off the wall is contributing at her capacity. The mechanism is published. The mechanism is fair."

"The mechanism is a wall with names on it."

"Yes."

"That's coercion."

"That is pressure. The line between pressure and coercion is a line we are walking, Ash, deliberately, and we are walking it because the only place the line is not walked is the place where nothing happens. I would prefer not to be walking it. I am walking it because the alternative is to wait, and I have been told to wait my entire life by men who had what I did not, and I am not going to tell my sister to wait so that you and Director Kharren can preserve the comfort of a framework neither of you had to live under."

He turned then.

He turned and his face was the face of a man who had not slept enough in the four weeks since the platform had been built, and whose hands behind his back had been behind his back because the hands, in front of him, would have been doing something he did not want them to do, and Ash saw, in the turning, the man who had sat at his workbench eight weeks ago and asked how the pump mechanism worked.

The man was still there.

The man was also no longer the only thing in Davan's face.

"I'm not asking you to stop," Ash said. "I came to tell you what I'm seeing."

"You came to tell me a boy used a word."

"Yes."

"I have heard the word used by a boy. I have heard the word used by men. I have had the word used at me, when I was the boy. It is not new."

"It's new to be on a printed list with an address."

"The printing is new. The word is not. The printing is what we have. The Council had laws and committees. We have a press."

"Davan."

"Ash."

"I came to tell you because if I don't tell you, then in six months when this is somewhere it shouldn't be, I won't have told you, and I'll wonder."

He saw Davan absorb that.

He saw it in the quarter-second between the line and the response, in the small unclasping and reclasping of the hands at the small of the back, in the half-step the boots took on the towpath, the readjusting of a stance that had been the platform's stance and was, for that quarter-second, not.

"All right," Davan said.

"All right."

"I have heard you."

"That's all I came for."

"It isn't all you came for."

"No."

"You came also to ask me to do something I am not going to do."

"Yes."

"And you knew when you came that I was not going to do it."

"Yes."

"Then why come."

"Because I would rather the not-doing be a thing that was asked and refused than a thing that was assumed."

A long silence.

"That," Davan said, "is the most Ash sentence I have ever heard you say."

"Mm."

"Walk back with me."

They walked back. They did not talk. At the side gate Korel was waiting, because Korel was the man who waited at the side gate, and Korel did the eyebrow and did not do the eyebrow, and Ash went past him and out into the lane and did not look back at the platform.

He felt the platform at his back the way a person felt a wall.

* * *

The day was fading when he came down into Stonecurve. The lamps were not lit; the lamplighter would come at the seventh and trim the wicks the way the wicks were trimmed in this district, which was the way Ash had learned, in the third month, to trim a wick, because nobody in this district had been trained to trim wicks the way the wicks needed to be trimmed when the lattices stopped holding.

Tesha was at Building Four.

"Tesha."

"Good."

"I do."

She walked beside him. "All right."

"Why can't we do both?"

It landed.

It landed the way the first sentence had landed in Dorenne's office, and it landed at the same temperature, because Tesha was not Davan and was not Dorenne and was the woman she was, and she had not rehearsed the sentence, and the sentence had come out of her without rehearsal, and was, for that, the harder of the three sentences he had heard that day.

"I."

"Why can't we do both?"

He did not answer her at once.

He stood at the corner and the lamplighter was coming up the lane behind him, the small hooded lamp of the lamplighter swinging, the wicks waiting in their cups, and Tesha stood with her hand on the post and waited the way she waited when she had handed Mira a tool and was not going to fill the silence the worker needed.

"I don't have a good answer," he said.

"Mm."

"I don't have a good answer because the answer I have sounds like every answer powerful people have ever given to people in rooms like this one."

"Try anyway."

"Building takes time. Redistribution takes from the building. If we do both, we do the redistribution at the cost of the building, and the building is what gets you and Mira and your mother through the next twenty years. That's the answer."

"That's a real answer."

"It's a real answer that doesn't help your mother in March."

"No."

"And I don't have an answer for March."

"No."

"And telling you to choose the building over March is the thing I cannot, in my mouth, say to you."

"Then don't."

"I won't."

"Then I'll go to the meetings. And I'll teach Mira the seam. And you'll let me, because the picking isn't real. The picking is in your face and not in the work."

"Mm."

"Eat the fish, Ash."

She let go of the post.

She went into the lane. He stood at the post.

He stood at it the count of ten. The flame held. He could feel the reservoir behind his ribs, the held breath of it, the thirty-seven percent that was not his and was, in the held shape of it, the answer he had not given Dorenne and the answer he had not given Davan and the answer he had not given Tesha, and the answer was that he did not know.

He did not know how to do both.

He did not know how not to do both.

He walked back up to the workshop.

The fish was on the bench.

Haran was at the lathe. The lathe was running. Haran did not turn.

"Eat," Haran said.

Ash sat. He unwrapped the oiled paper. The fish was the fish. He ate it cold, with his hands, in the small careful way a man ate a thing he had bought by accident, and the lathe behind him ran, and the broadsheet was still on the bench under the brass file, and the headline still said three.

He thought about Marra Telne.

He thought about the boy with the bucket.

He thought about Tesha's hand on the post.

He thought about Dorenne standing at the window with her hands clasped at the small of her back, the same clasp Davan had used on the towpath, the two of them standing at opposite ends of a city using the same posture without knowing it.

He thought about his mother holding the cracked bowl.

The cracked bowl had nothing to do with the day.

The cracked bowl had everything to do with the day.

"Haran."

"Mm."

"I don't know what to do."

The lathe slowed. It did not stop. Haran let it slow at its own pace, the way Haran let things slow, and at the bottom of the slow he set his hand on the headstock and looked, for the first time since Ash had come in, at Ash.

"Mm."

"They're all right."

"Mm."

"They can't all three be right."

"They can."

"Then what do I do."

"You eat the fish. You sleep. In the morning you come back and we work on the chimney joint at Block Three because the chimney joint at Block Three is something you can do tomorrow and the rest is something you can't."

"That's not an answer."

"No."

He did not say more. He turned the lathe back on. The lathe came up to its speed and the chip lifted off the work and curled and dropped, and Ash sat on the stool at the bench and finished the fish and watched the curl and did not, for the count of a long minute, think anything.

The reservoir held.

The headline held.

The list on the wall held.

Tesha's question held.

He went up the stair to the loft at the ninth hour, and he did not sleep for an hour, and then he slept.
