# Chapter 16

The letter came up the lane in a boy's hand at the second hour, and Ash saw it before Haran did.

He was at the front window of the workshop with a cup of tea gone tepid in his palm and the shutter half-open against a wind that had been working at the eaves since before light, and he saw the boy because the boy was running. Boys ran in that lane for a half dozen reasons and most of them were not letters. This one had a letter. He had it folded once in his right hand and pressed flat against his thigh as he ran, and the running was the running of a boy who had been told the letter was wanted and not the running of a boy who had been told the letter was urgent, which was a difference Ash had learned to read in the months he had been watching boys come up that lane to this door.

He set the cup down.

He went to the door before the knock. He opened it on the boy's raised hand and the boy lowered the hand and held out the letter without a word, the way the boys in Haran's network held out letters, which was the way of children who had been told that the work was carrying and not speaking, and Ash took the letter and gave the boy a copper from the dish by the doorframe and the boy went back down the lane.

The letter was for Haran.

He carried it in. He did not open it. He laid it on the bench at Haran's elbow the way a man laid a thing on a bench at another man's elbow, square to the grain, the seal up, and he went back to his stool and picked up the cold tea and did not drink it.

Haran finished the join he was working.

He set the small pliers in the roll. He looked at the letter without picking it up. He looked at the seal a count, and the count was a count Ash did not recognize, and Haran's face did a small thing it did not often do, which was to settle, the way a face settled when the man behind it had been waiting for a thing without naming the waiting and had now received the thing. He picked the letter up. He broke the seal with his thumb. He read.

He read it twice.

He read it the second time slower than the first. When he had finished the second reading he set the letter on the bench, not folded, face down, in the small empty place between the wire roll and the lip of the vise, and he put his hand flat on it, and he looked at the wall above the bench, and he did not, for the count of perhaps fifteen, do anything else.

"What," Ash said.

"Mm."

"Haran."

"Ardenmere."

He said the name and did not say more. He said it the way a man said a name he had been carrying as a name and was now saying as a fact, and the saying lay on the bench beside the letter, and Haran put his hand on the letter again, and his hand was the hand of a man who was deciding whether to hand a thing across a bench, and after a count he handed it.

Ash took it.

The hand that wrote it was a hand he did not know. The letters were small and the lines were close and the writer had run out of paper at the bottom and turned the sheet ninety degrees and run a column of words up the margin in a hand that had been tired by then and had not been tired at the start. Ash read.

*Haran. The pools went under in the last week. Not weak. Under. Maeve at the south spring tried to draw on the second day and her pool gave her nothing, not the thinning we have been used to, but nothing, the way a dry well gives a bucket nothing. By the third day the rest of us had the same. The lattice in the meeting hall has been cold since the fourth. The grain dryer at Vell's barn ran for an hour after the start because Tarrin had a held charge and gave it, and after that hour it stopped. The aqueduct lift at the upper pond stopped on the fifth. We have been carrying water. We are carrying water now. I am writing this by lamp because the lamp is the lamp.*

*The fields are not the fields. The wheat has come up but the irrigation runs we built when you were here last have not run. The clay sluices crack at the seams where the channelers used to seal them and the seams have been cracking for two seasons and we did not, then, understand the cracking as the cracking it was. We understand it now. The seams are open and the water goes where the water goes and not into the rows.*

*We sent to the hill country. Bren writes back that they have a hand-built pump and a wood sluice and they will send a man to show us if we send a man to learn. I do not know who to send. The young pools are weakest. They do not know the work. The old pools are tired and there are not many of them. We have been a community where the work was the channel and the channel was the work and now the channel is not, and we are a community where the work has not yet been done.*

*The grain that is in the silo will go four months at the ration. The grain that is in the ground will not be the grain it should be. We will not, this year, harvest what we eat.*

*Tell me what you would tell me. Tell me what others have done. The boy carries this. Pay him. He has come a long way.*

The signature was a name Ash did not know and a place Ash had heard Haran speak of three or four times in evenings and never in any way that had made the place a place in his head until now.

He read it again.

He read it the way a man read a thing the second time when the first reading had not let the words be words, when the words had stayed words on a page and not become the country they described, and on the second reading the country came up off the page and was a country, and the country was a country going under, and Ash set the letter on the bench and stood up.

"I'll go," he said.

"Mm."

"Today. The boy is, the boy is still close, I can. I can go today, Haran."

"Mm."

"The reservoir is at, "

"I know what the reservoir is at."

Haran said it the way Haran said a thing he was not going to argue. He said it and then he did not say more for a count, and the count was not a count of him gathering a sentence; it was a count of him deciding whether to gather one. He gathered one.

"What would you do when you got there?"

Ash stopped.

"Pour the reservoir into their fields until you left, and then what?"

He said it without lifting his voice. He said it the way he had said most of the hardest things he had ever said to Ash, which was at the level he said *forge* and *tea* and *try again*, in the cadence of a man who had not invented the question and was only the man at this bench who had to ask it.

Ash did not answer.

He sat back down on the stool. He did not, in the sitting, decide anything. He sat with his hands on his knees the way a man sat when his hands had been about to do a thing and had been told not to and did not yet have a next thing, and the letter was on the bench in front of him, and outside the wind was still working at the eaves.

"They don't have a year," he said.

"No."

"They don't have a season."

"They don't have one season at the rate the letter says. They have less than one."

"And I have, "

"You have what's in the reservoir. And you have what's in your hands. You have what you know to do with the both of those. And you have the road from this door to that door, which is six days fast and ten days easy."

"Six days."

"Six. The boy came in nine. The boy walked. You'd ride."

"And then."

Haran did not answer at once.

He picked the letter up. He folded it on the original creases. He set it in the small wood box at the top of the bench where he kept the letters of the network, and the box was nearly full, and Ash had not, until this morning, noticed that the box was nearly full.

"Then you stand in their meeting hall," Haran said, "and you channel into the lattice and the lattice warms, and you channel into the lift at the upper pond and the lift lifts, and you channel into the seams of the clay sluices and the seams hold for as long as your hand is on them, and you ride home, and the lattice goes cold and the lift stops and the seams open. And the harvest is the harvest it was always going to be, except that you have spent a piece of yourself you don't get back, and they have spent a week believing the answer was you, and at the end of the week the answer is gone."

"Yes."

"That's what would happen."

"Yes."

"You know it's what would happen."

"Yes."

Haran put the lid on the box.

He did it gently, the way he did things at this bench when the things had a weight in them, and the lid settled, and the box was the box, and the letter was inside the box, and the box was on the shelf where the box had always been.

"Sit," Haran said.

"I'm sitting."

"Mm."

He went back to his wire.

* * *

The next letter came on the fourth day.

It was not from Ardenmere. It was from a place called Birchhollow, which was higher in the hill country and west of the road that ran north out of Solathis, and the writer was a woman named Esa who had been at one of Haran's three-day workshops in a season Ash had not been alive in. Haran read it at the bench in the afternoon and did not, at first, hand it across.

He laid it on the bench.

He went to the small wood box and took down a folded sheet and unfolded it, and the sheet was a list. The list was in Haran's hand, and the list had names down the left margin and short notations against each name, and Haran ran his thumb down the column and found the name *Esa* and put a small charcoal mark beside it, and then he wrote a second line under the original entry, and the second line said something Ash could not read from where he sat, and Haran folded the list and put it back in the box.

Then he handed Ash the letter.

*Haran. Word from the lower passes that Ardenmere is under. We had heard rumors a fortnight back. Now the rumors are letters. I am writing because you asked, two summers ago when you came through, that any of us who had built a thing should write you when the thing was tested by a year of use, and the thing has been tested by two now, and I should have written sooner.*

*The crank line at the long field works. We built it the way you sketched it, with the timber crook and the leather collar at the joint and the lift pulley above the elbow of the run, and the boys turn it in shifts of half an hour, and the water goes uphill the way water does not naturally go and arrives at the high field in the volume the long field needs. We have not channeled at the long field for fourteen months. Two of the old channelers have died in that time, and one has gone south to her daughter, and the fourth is still here and has not been called to the field since the spring before last. The field is the field. The wheat is the wheat. The boys complain about the cranking and the boys are also proud of the cranking, and the pride is the pride of boys who have been given a thing they can do that the village needs.*

*We are not Ardenmere. I know this. We started earlier and we started smaller and we had you here for the three days, and Ardenmere did not have you, and Ardenmere was richer and was further from believing it would have to. I write you the working because you asked. If the working is of use to anyone, send it on.*

*The hand-pump at the cooper's well is on its second leather. The first leather lasted nine months. We have a man who can cut leather to the pattern from the offcuts of saddle work and he will train another. The training takes a winter.*

Ash set the letter down.

"Esa," Haran said, from the other bench. "She's the one with the daughter who keeps the bees."

"Yes."

"She would not write a letter she did not mean to send."

"No."

"Mm."

He went back to his work.

The third letter came on the sixth day, and the fourth on the seventh, and by the end of the second week the wood box was full and Haran had moved a second box up onto the shelf beside it, and the second box was beginning to fill.

Ash read them.

He read them at the bench in the evenings after the workshop closed, by lamp, with the cold tea at his elbow and the wind doing what the wind did at this season under the eaves. He read them in the order Haran handed them across, which was not the order they had arrived in but the order Haran had decided he should read them in, and Ash did not, after the first three, ask why the order was the order. The order was the order Haran chose.

A letter from a coastal village called Brackmouth where the fishing fleet had been rebuilt with sail.

*The sails are rough. We cut them from canvas a tinker brought up from the south for half what you said they would cost. The first cut was wrong and we lost a month on it. The second cut was right. The boats go out on the sails now and come back on the sails and on a tide we have learned to read again, the way our great-grandfathers read it, except that we are reading it in a worse year, and the catches are the catches. We have a man who can repair a tear at sea. We are training two more.*

A letter from a highland community whose name Ash did not catch the first time and then caught.

*We pulled the channeled heating out of the long-house in the autumn before last and laid in compressed straw between the inner and outer walls in courses with a battening of hide, the way you said the old herders had done it before the channelers came, and the long-house held its heat through the cold months on a fire of one log an hour and a fire of one log an hour we can keep. The sleeping cots stayed warm. The children stayed warm. The old people stayed warm. We have insulated the meeting hall this autumn and the school in the autumn before next.*

A letter from a place Ash had not heard of, in a hand he did not know, on paper that was thinner than the paper of the others.

*We did the gravity feed off the upper spring in the year you came. The pipe is cedar that we tarred ourselves and the tar held two seasons before the lower run wanted patching and we patched the lower run. The water comes. The water has come every day for three years. We do not understand, now, that we ever did it the other way. The young ones do not remember the other way. They turn the spigot at the head of the row and the water comes and they do not, in the turning, think about a channeler, because there is no channeler, and there has not been one in their living. I write because you asked.*

Ash read these.

He read them the way a man read a thing he had not expected to be reading, which was without the lens he had brought to the bench and with the lens the letters made him build as he read, and the lens was a lens that focused on a thing he had not, in the months of Stonecurve, looked for in this direction. He had been looking down the road from his door. He had been looking at the city he was in. He had been looking at the district below him and the family above him and the lane between them. He had not been looking at the country.

The country had been working.

The country had been working for two years and three years and four years in some places, in pockets and in single villages and along the upper passes and at the edges of the coastal road, in small slow accumulations of canvas and leather and timber and tarred cedar and compressed straw and crank handles that turned when the boys turned them, and the working had not been a thing anyone had announced because announcement was not the kind of work it was.

It was the kind of work a person did because the channel was no longer there.

* * *

On the ninth night Ash sat at the bench with seven letters open across the wood and he did not, for a count he did not count, move.

He had set the letters out the way a man set out the pages of a thing he was trying to read all at once. Esa's at the left elbow. The sail letter beside it. The highland letter under those. The cedar pipe letter at the top, because the cedar pipe letter was the oldest, and underneath he had put the three letters from Ardenmere, which had come in three separate weeks and which he had not, until this evening, laid down in a row.

The Ardenmere letters were the ones he kept reading.

The first letter he had read on the morning the boy ran up the lane. The second had come a week later, in a different hand than the first, in a hand that had been steadier and had used a different paper, and the second writer had set out, in lines, what had been tried.

*We sent to Esa at Birchhollow. The man we sent is Tarrin's youngest, who is fifteen, and we sent him because the young pools are nothing now and a young body is a young body and he could walk. He has gone. He will be back in a fortnight. Esa has written to say she will receive him and show him the crank line and send him back with a sketch and with what cuttings of leather she can spare. We do not, in the meantime, have a crank line. We have buckets. We have the carry from the lower pond up to the field, which is a half mile rising, and we have set the carry on shifts, and the shifts are, for the moment, holding.*

*The grain dryer is cold and the grain is in the silo and the silo is dry and the grain will keep a season at most before damp. We have moved what we can to the upper barn. We have turned the silo by hand twice in the last week, the way it used to be turned before any of us was born, and the turning is hard, and the men who turn it are tired, and the men who turn it have stopped being tired because they do not, any more, have the choice to be tired.*

*We did not, two summers ago, do what Birchhollow did. We did not do it because we could not, then, see the use of doing it. The lattice was warm. The lift lifted. The dryer dried. We thought, when you came, that you were a man who had built a thing for a place that needed it, and we were not yet a place that needed it. We were wrong about that. We were wrong by two years. We have spent the two years on the channel, and the channel is gone, and the two years are gone with it, and we have not built what we should have built when there was still a man and a horse to teach us how. I am writing to tell you this because you should know. We were wrong. We have been wrong. We are wrong now, this morning, in a way we cannot un-wrong by the time the harvest comes.*

The third letter was shorter.

*The boy has come back. He has the sketch. We are building the crook this week from the timber at the upper edge of the wood. The leather is what Esa could spare and is not enough for the second collar, and we will tan our own from a steer we cannot afford to slaughter, and we will slaughter it, because the alternative is the alternative. The first crank line will run by the new moon if the weather. The second will run, perhaps, before the rains.*

*Tell Maeve in the south district that the grain ration is at three-quarters and we are moving to two-thirds at the end of the month. Tell her also that we are alive. The number who are alive at the end of the season will depend on the cranking and the carrying and the slaughter and on whether the weather gives us the rains the wheat needs after the rows are dry enough to be open to receive them, and these are the things we have been used to depending on, and we are remembering how.*

*We are remembering, Haran. That is the thing I would say to you if I could say it. We are remembering. The remembering is not fast and it is not free, and we are paying for it now in a year we cannot afford to pay anything in, and we are paying. Esa was right to send the boy. We were right to send for him. We will, if the weather, eat next winter. We will not eat as much as we ate the winter before last. We will eat.*

Ash sat with the three letters under his hands.

He laid his palm on the third one. He laid his palm flat and the paper was the paper, thin and warm under the lamp, and he closed his eyes a count.

The thing he felt was not what he had expected to feel.

He had expected, when the box began to fill, a thing that would come up under his ribs the way relief had come up at the forge in the morning he had watched Haran light the kindling. He had expected, in the reading, a vindication. He had been building a thing in Stonecurve for a year. The letters were of communities that had built the thing earlier, in smaller, in country. He had expected to recognize the work.

He recognized the work.

He also recognized something he had not, in any month of the year, let himself look at.

The work in the letters had not waited for him. The work in the letters had not waited for anyone. It had been done by people whose names he did not know, in places he had never been to, with materials that were the materials they had, in winters he had spent as a boy at his father's table eating bread that had come up to the city from a country that was, even then, beginning to do this work. They had been doing it while he was being weighed for his pool. They had been doing it while he had been told he was ordinary. They had been doing it through the years he had carried the weight of being a man with no channel in a city of channels, and they had done it without him, and without his city, and without any of the institutions his city had built, and they had done it because the channel was not coming back.

They had been ahead of him.

They had been ahead of him for as long as he had been alive, and longer.

He sat with that.

He sat with it the way he had sat with the second line on the bench paper at the forge, in the cool of the shop with the lamp at a low burn, and the sitting was not the sitting it had been then, because the line on the bench paper had been a line he had drawn for himself and the country in the letters was not a thing he had drawn at all. It had drawn itself. It had drawn itself in canvas and tar and timber and leather, in three-day workshops and in fortnight walks and in steers that could not be slaughtered being slaughtered, and he had not, until this evening, known the drawing was on the page.

His chest did the thing it did when a thing he had thought was the case turned out not to be the case.

It did not open this time.

It closed.

It closed the small careful way a chest closed when a man had been holding a thing as a thing he had earned and was, in a count, learning that he had not earned it, that the thing had been there before him, that the earning had been other people's, in years he had not been counting, and he sat with the closing, and the lamp at his elbow burned at the low burn, and the seven letters lay open across the wood.

* * *

Ryn came up the lane on the Tuesday.

She came at the third hour the way she came at the third hour, with her coat over her arm because the wind had eased by midmorning, and she let herself in the side door without knocking and crossed the workshop to the bench and looked at the letters.

She did not pick one up.

She read the way Ryn read, which was at distance, with her hands at her sides and her head a finger tilted to the side and her eyes moving across the lines without commitment. She read the top one. She read the one beside it. She read the third and fourth and stopped at the fifth, which was the cedar pipe letter, and she read that one a count longer than the others.

"Haran's people," she said.

"Yes."

"Ardenmere's the dark one."

"Yes."

"And these are the ones."

"The ones who built before."

"Mm."

She pulled the stool out from under the corner of the bench and sat. She did not take her coat off. She set it across her knees. She looked at him.

She looked at him the way she looked at him, which was without performing the looking, with the practical patience of a woman who had been looking at him since they were children and had never stopped being able to see him under whatever the year had laid on him, and she let the looking sit a count.

"You were going to go," she said.

"To Ardenmere. Yes."

"Haran stopped you."

"Haran asked me a question."

"Mm."

"He asked what I would do when I got there."

"And."

"I would, "

He stopped.

"Ash."

"I would pour the reservoir into their fields. Until I left. And then I would leave. And then the reservoir would be smaller and the fields would be the fields they were before I came, and the lattice would be cold, and the lift would not lift."

"Yes."

"Yes."

She nodded.

She did not say *I'm sorry* and she did not say *that's hard* and she did not say any of the small soft things people said in this country when a man had named a thing, because Ryn did not say small soft things. She nodded. The nodding was the acknowledgement and was also, in the tilt of it, the nod of a woman who was about to say a next thing, and Ash waited.

"So the question isn't whether your approach works," she said.

"No."

"It's whether you can get out of the way fast enough for other people to do it themselves."

She said it the way she said a thing she had been turning in her hand on the walk over, the way she said the things she had been thinking through before she got to the door, in the cadence of a person finishing an argument she had started with herself in another room, and the saying was not pointed. The saying was put down.

It was put down on the bench beside the seven letters.

It lay there.

Ash looked at it.

He looked at the letters under it. He looked at Ryn at the corner of the bench with her coat across her knees and the wind no longer at the eaves. He looked at the lamp. He did not, for a count, speak, because his speaking had nowhere to go that was not into the thing she had set down, and he was not yet ready to step into the thing she had set down. He sat on his side of the bench with the thing between them, and Ryn, who had said it, did not press.

"I built it," he said. "I built it well."

"You did."

"I built it with the people."

"Some of it."

"With Tesha. With Dael. With Corren. With, "

"Some of it."

"Some of it."

"Yes."

"And the rest I built for them."

"Yes."

"And Esa, "

He stopped again, because he did not know how to finish the sentence. He knew the shape of the finish. The shape was that Esa had not built for. Esa had built with a man who came for three days and showed and went, and the people of Birchhollow had built the rest, and the building had been the building, and the building was on the long field now in canvas and timber, and the long field was the long field.

"Esa built it herself," Ryn said, gently.

"Yes."

"With his sketch."

"Yes."

"And he went home."

"Yes."

"And the boy from Ardenmere walked nine days to get the sketch from her, and she didn't go to Ardenmere either, did she?"

"No."

"Mm."

She took her coat off her knees. She folded it over the corner of the bench. She looked at the letters again, and at him again, and her face did a small thing that was nearly a smile and was nearer to the recognition that came before a smile, and she did not, in the doing, push.

"I'll make tea," she said.

"I have, "

"It's cold. I'll make new."

"All right."

She made tea.

She made it at the stove in the corner the way she made it, with the water from the basin and the kettle on the cold ring and a small careful channel from her own pool, the working channel of a woman who had a working pool and used it for the small daily work that was the work, and the kettle warmed at her hand, and Ash watched the warming, and the air around her hand bloomed the small visible bloom that the air bloomed around any ordinary channeler, and Ash watched the bloom and did not, in the watching, judge it.

She poured two cups.

She brought them across. She set his at his elbow without a word. She sat back on the stool with hers in both hands and did not, after she had sat, say more.

They drank.

The tea was hot. The cup was warm. The wind did not come up at the eaves. The lamp burned at its low burn. Ryn looked at the letters and Ash looked at the letters and neither of them, for a count, looked at each other, and the count was a count of two friends sitting with a thing that did not need to be looked across.

After a while she stood.

"Tuesday," she said.

"Tuesday."

"I'll come next week."

"All right."

"Don't do anything stupid."

"No."

"Mm."

She went out the side door.

* * *

The last letter came on the afternoon of the eleventh day.

It was a different kind of letter. It was on lined paper, half a sheet, and the hand was a hand Ash knew, and the seal was not a seal but a small careful crease where the writer had folded the half sheet in thirds and pressed the fold flat with her thumb the way she pressed a fold flat with her thumb. It was not addressed to Haran. It was addressed to Ash, in the upright square hand of a woman who had written his name on chart margins and deployment rolls and ledger lines for a year and a half and had not, in any of the writing, written it any other way.

Maren had written it.

The boy who brought it was the same boy who had brought the first one. He came up the lane at the slow trot of a boy who was tired of running, and he came to the door, and Ash came to the door, and the boy did not, this time, hand the letter across without speaking. He had a thing he had been told to say.

"From the Hall," he said.

"Yes."

"She said. She said particularly to you. Not to him."

He flicked his eye to the bench where Haran was at his work.

"All right."

"She said also. To read it where you read it. Not at the door."

"All right."

The boy held the letter out. Ash took it. The boy stood with his weight on one foot a count, because there was a copper coming, and Ash gave him the copper, and the boy went down the lane.

Ash carried the letter to his stool.

He did not, for a count, open it.

He sat with it in his hand. He looked at the letters spread across the bench from the days before. He looked at the wood box on the shelf with its lid set. He looked at the far wall of the workshop where the light from the high window was beginning to come down at the angle of late afternoon, and he turned the half sheet in his hand once, and the paper was lighter than the paper of the network letters, the better paper of the Hall, smooth under the thumb, expensive.

He opened it.

The hand inside was the same upright square hand, but it had been written quickly. The lines did not come down evenly. The first line sat high on the half sheet and the last line sat near the middle, and the writer had not, this time, taken the second half of the page for a margin column. She had said what she had to say in seven lines and stopped.

*Ash. Ardenmere is at the end of the second week now. The reports come in to my office on the regular schedule because the regular schedule is the regular schedule, and I have begun to read them on the day they arrive instead of with the weekly batch. I am writing to tell you the figures because you should have the figures.*

*Pool readings across all measured channelers in the settlement: zero. Not low. Zero. This is the first community on the registry to read at zero across all reporting points. Population at the last census: one thousand four hundred and eleven. Grain reserve at the start of the month: four months at standard ration, three at the reduced ration the council there has imposed. Aqueduct lift: stopped on the fifth day. Channeled lattice: cold from the fourth. The reports describe a return to pre-channel agricultural method.*

*I do not, in writing this, ask you to do anything. I have been at this work long enough to know what I would ask of you and what would be wasted. I am writing because I have seen the figures and I think you should see them, and because Haran's people send him their letters and it occurs to me that no one sends you the figures from the Hall, and the figures are what I have to send.*

*This is what it looks like.*

*— D.*

Ash read it.

He read it twice. He read it the second time the way he had read the first letter from the lane, which was the way of a man whose eyes had gone over the words and not let the words be what they were. The words were what they were.
