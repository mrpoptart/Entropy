# Chapter 18

The path up to the south block ran along the cut where the old culvert had been opened and not yet closed, and Ash took it with his hands in his pockets and a folded slip of paper at the top of the inside one, and he did not, this morning, have a plan.

He had a question.

The question was a small one. It was the kind of question a man asked when he had lived in a thing for six months and only now thought to look at it from the side, and the looking from the side made the thing strange. The question was: where did the water actually go.

He had walked this district a hundred times. He had walked it with surveys and with elevation lists and with the small tight sketches he kept in the leather book at his hip, and he had walked it the way a man walked a thing he owned, which was the walk of a man who already knew. This morning he was walking it the other way. Slow. Slow in the legs, slow in the eye. Slow in the small instinct at the back of his throat that wanted to lift his hand and fix what he passed.

He passed the heating row.

He passed the second standpipe.

He came up the rise to Building Six, and the door was open the way doors were open when a household had decided that opening was less work than closing, and a woman he knew by face but had never spoken to was at the threshold with two pails on a yoke and a child at her hip and an expression that said she had been doing this carry for a long time and was not, today, in the mood to be polite about it.

He stopped at the bottom of the step.

"Morning."

She looked at him. She measured the morning. She set one pail down with a wet thump and shifted the child to the other side and said, "Morning."

"Could I ask you something."

"You can ask."

He nodded at the pails. "How many trips."

"This time of day."

"This time of day."

"Six."

"Six trips."

"From the pump." She lifted her chin at the pump house at the bottom of the slope, which sat where the survey had said the pump should sit, which was at the low corner of the lot, which was the place a pump went if you were thinking about pressure and head and the line into the main, and which was, Ash now saw, the place a pump went if you were not thinking about a woman with two pails and a child and a yoke.

"Six trips up."

"Six trips up." She did not say *and back down*. The *and back down* was in the angle of her shoulders, and in the way the yoke had worn the shoulders to a shape that was not a shape a young woman's shoulders had to be.

"Thank you," Ash said.

She watched him a moment, to see if the thanking was a thing or was the front of a thing. He did not put a *but* after the thanking. He did not lift his hand to the pump. He stood at the bottom of her step with his hands in his pockets and waited until she had picked the second pail back up and gone in, and then he stood a count longer, and then he walked on.

He wrote nothing in the book.

He had decided, on the way up the curve this morning, that the book would stay in his pocket today, and that he would, today, listen the way a man listened when he had nothing to write with. Writing changed the listening. He had not known that until last week. He knew it now.

\* \* \*

Tesha was at the heating element when he came down the row, which was where Tesha was most mornings now, because the heating element was the thing she had decided was hers.

She had not decided it out loud. She had decided it the way she decided things, which was by being there one morning and then the next and then the morning after that, until the absence of her at the element would have been more strange than the presence, and the crew had, without anyone proposing it, started bringing the small problems to her instead of to the manual on the hook at the wall.

She had a cloth in one hand and a wrench in the other and was not using either. She was looking at the lower seam where the casing met the floor plate and was, Ash thought, listening to it.

He came up beside her and did not speak.

She let him stand.

After a while she said, "It's ticking."

"Where."

"There." She did not point with the wrench. She pointed with her chin. The chin pointed at the lower seam. "When the burner cuts in. Once. Then nothing. Then once."

He listened. The burner was off. The element was cold. The ticking was not happening because the burner was not happening.

"How long."

"Three days. Maybe four."

"And it still runs."

"It still runs."

He crouched. He put a hand on the casing the way a man put a hand on a horse that did not yet know him, which was light and palm-flat and unhurried, and he did not, when he put the hand there, reach for the channel. The reach was a separate motion. The reach was a motion he had made a thousand times in this district and was not going to make this morning. He put the hand on the casing and he listened with the hand, and the casing was cold, and the cold told him nothing, and he took the hand off.

"Could be the bracket," he said.

"Could be the bracket."

"Could be the floor plate moving."

"Could be the floor plate."

"When did the floor last get wet."

She looked at him. She looked at him in a way she had not looked at him before, which was the look of a person who had asked a question into a room for a long time and had, at last, heard the room ask one back.

"Two weeks ago," she said. "When the standpipe spat."

"How wet."

"Wet enough."

"And it dried."

"It dried."

"But the plate moved."

"The plate moved."

He stood up. His knees cracked. He did not, this morning, mind the cracking. He looked along the row at the four other elements that ran off the same floor plate and at the place where the floor plate met the wall and at the small dark line at the wall that was either a shadow or a separation, and he said, "Can you walk me along it."

She walked him along it.

She walked him along it slowly, and she stopped at the places where she had noticed things and did not stop at the places where she had not, and the places where she stopped were not the places he would have stopped if he had been walking it alone, and that was the thing. That was the whole thing. She had been living in this row for four months. He had been visiting it.

At the end of the row she said, "The pump's wrong."

He looked at her.

"In Six," she said. "The pump."

"I know."

"You know."

"I know now."

She made a sound that was not quite a laugh. It was the sound a person made when a long-held grievance had, without warning, been stepped over instead of argued with. "All right."

"What would you do."

She looked at him. "You're asking me."

"I'm asking you."

She thought about it. She thought about it the way she did everything, which was without hurry and without performance, and the thinking moved across her face the way weather moved across a field. "Move it up."

"Up the slope."

"Up the slope. To the bend by the wall. The line still runs to the main from there. It just runs the other way."

"Pressure'll be off."

"Pressure'll be off. We'll size the line."

"You'll size the line."

"I'll size the line."

He said, "All right."

The *all right* was a small thing. It came out the way *all right* came out of a man's mouth when *all right* was the only honest answer, and Tesha looked at him a moment to see if the *all right* was a thing or the front of a thing, and she found, this morning, that it was a thing, and she nodded once and turned back to the seam.

"I'll tell Pell," she said.

"Tell Pell."

"He won't like it."

"He won't."

"He'll do it anyway."

"He will."

She did not, when she said this, smile. She did not need to. The not-smiling was its own register. Ash stood at her shoulder a count longer and then walked on down the row, and the small clean place in his chest where the pride lived was, this morning, sore in a way that was not bad. The pride had been asked to step down off a thing it had been standing on. The pride had stepped down. The stepping down was the sore.

He walked.

\* \* \*

Mira found him at the cistern at midmorning.

She had the long ledger under her arm and the pencil through her hair and the look of a woman who had been doing arithmetic for an hour and had come up the slope to do less of it, and she fell in beside him without asking and said, "Pell wants to know if you're moving the pump."

"Tesha's moving the pump."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's what I'm answering."

Mira walked another three paces and then said, "Good."

That was all she said. They came to the cistern, and the cistern was full, and the float was at the line where the float was supposed to be, and the small inlet valve was, Ash saw, fitted with a brass collar he had not put there. He stopped.

"When did this go on."

"Last week."

"Who."

"Pell's brother."

"Who's Pell's brother."

Mira looked at him. "Karrik."

"Karrik."

"He's been at the workshops. The valve ones. Tesha's. He saw the inlet was chattering and he made a collar."

"Out of what."

"Off-cut from the south rail."

He bent down. The collar was rough. The collar was rough the way a thing was rough that had been made by a person who had not made many of that thing yet, and the seating was off by maybe an eighth, and the seating was off in the direction that did not matter, which was the direction toward the wall, and the chattering was, at this moment, not happening.

"It's holding," Ash said.

"It's holding."

"I'd have done the seat tighter."

"I know you would."

He stood up. He brushed the dust off his palm onto his trouser. He did not, this morning, take the brass collar off the inlet and put a better one on. The instinct came. The instinct came the way the instinct came, which was the way a hand reached for a tool when the tool was on the bench, and he watched the instinct come and he did not act on it, and the not-acting was a small clean quiet thing that sat in his chest beside the sore where the pride had stepped down.

"Tell Karrik," he said.

"Tell him what."

"Tell him it's holding."

Mira looked at him.

"Tell him I said so."

"You can tell him."

"I can tell him."

"He'd rather hear it from you."

"Then I'll tell him."

She nodded. She wrote something in the ledger. She did not show him what she had written. The not-showing was, he thought, a thing too. There were a lot of things, this morning, that were things.

\* \* \*

The catalytic was for the foundation course at the back of the new build, which was the row Tesha had argued for and the row Pell had cut the line for and the row the old men with the barrows had laid the base of last week, and the base needed setting, and the setting was the thing Ash had come up the curve to do.

He did not, this morning, do it the way he had done it in the spring.

In the spring he would have stood at the head of the course and pressed the palm of his hand against the mortar line and pushed the wide loose press into the work, and the work would have set, and the setting would have been a setting and a half, because the press would have gone into the mortar and into the stone and into the air and into the dust and into the wall behind the wall, and the setting in the mortar would have been, of all the settings, the smallest part.

This morning he laid two strips of canvas along the line, and he laid a third over the strips, and he put his palm on the canvas at the first joint, and he breathed.

He narrowed.

He narrowed the way Haran had shown him at the rod, which was not a clenching but a quieting, and he felt, under the quieting, the thin line of the thing itself, and the thin line was, this morning, there.

It was there for a count.

He held it.

The mortar warmed under his palm, and the warming was not the fog, and was not the nothing, and was the third thing, and he held the third thing for a count and a count and a count, and he moved his palm down the canvas the width of his palm, and he held it again, and the joint set under him in the way a joint set when the heat went where it was meant to go and did not go where it was not, and the canvas above his hand was barely warm, and the stone behind the joint was barely warm, and the wall behind the stone was cool to the touch when he laid his other hand on it, and the difference was the difference between this morning and the spring.

He moved his palm down again.

The line was thin. The line wanted to break the way a line wanted to break when a man had only been holding the line for nine days, and he had to find it again twice along the course, and the finding-again was the work, and the work was slow, and the slow was the point.

He came to the end of the course.

He took his palm off the canvas.

He stood up.

He looked at his hand, which was warm, and at the canvas, which was warm where the joint had set and not warm where the joint had not been worked, and at the line of the joint, which was, when he ran his thumb along it, even.

The reservoir at the back of him was a quantity. It was a quantity that had moved. It had moved by less than it would have moved in the spring for the same length of joint. It had not moved by nothing. The math was the math.

He looked along the course.

Pell was at the far end of the course.

Pell had been there the whole time.

Ash had not seen him come up.

"That was different," Pell said.

"It was."

"That all you're doing today."

"That's all I'm doing today."

Pell looked at him a moment. Pell was not a man who said things he did not mean and was not a man who said many things at all, and the looking was the looking, and after a long count Pell said, "Less waste."

"Less waste."

"More precision."

"More precision."

Pell nodded once. He did not ask how. He did not ask where Ash had learned it. He turned and walked back down the course to where the men with the barrows were waiting on him to call the next lift, and he called the next lift, and the men lifted, and the work went on the way the work went on.

Ash stood at the end of the course a count longer, and then he walked.

\* \* \*

The communal element was a thing they had been arguing about for a month.

The argument had been, at the start, a builder's argument and a Leveler's argument, and the builder had said *put a heating lattice in the floor of the common room and run it off a catalytic charge twice a year*, and the Leveler, who was not a Leveler now but had been one in the spring, had said *no, we'll feed it ourselves, we'll all feed it, we'll keep it on us*, and the argument had gone the way arguments went when both arguers were right about the half they were arguing about and wrong about the half they were not.

Tesha had broken it.

Tesha had broken it without speaking. She had drawn, on the back of a freight slip, a thing that was not the builder's thing and was not the Leveler's thing, and the thing was a lattice in the floor with a small inlet at the corner and a small inlet at the other corner and a third inlet that was not an inlet but a mouth, and she had pushed the slip across the table to Ash, and Ash had looked at it, and he had said, "Both."

She had said, "Both."

The builder had said, "How."

The Leveler had said, "How."

She had said, "He sets it. We feed it. When it goes weak, he sets it again. When it goes strong, we feed it less."

The builder had said, "That's not a system."

She had said, "It is now."

So this was the day for the setting.

The common room was full when Ash came in. It was not full because anyone had been called. It was full because Mira had said at the standpipe at breakfast that the setting was today, and the standpipe was, in this district, a better paper than any paper, and the room had the shape it had when a room had filled itself, which was the shape of people standing where they fit and not where they had been put.

Karrik was at the back. Ash found his face and nodded at it. Karrik nodded back. The nodding was a small clean thing.

The lattice was in the floor. The lattice had been laid by Pell's crew in the morning and was a grid of thin iron bedded in the screed and skinned with the cheap plaster the new builds used, and the surface of the floor was cool under Ash's boot when he stepped onto it, and the cool was the cool of a thing that was waiting to be told what it was for.

He knelt at the corner inlet.

He laid his palm on the inlet plate.

He breathed.

The room was quiet behind him. The room had the kind of quiet a room had when it was watching a thing it had not seen before, and Ash did not, this morning, do anything to fill the quiet. He let the quiet be the quiet.

He narrowed.

The line came at the second try. The line came the way it came at the bench, which was for a count, and then for a count more, and he held it, and he moved the line into the inlet plate, and the inlet plate took it, and the lattice took it from the inlet plate, and he felt, under his palm, the small clean direction of the heat moving where the iron told it to go, and he held the line, and he moved it down the lattice the way he had moved it down the canvas in the morning, and the lattice warmed in the way the lattice was meant to warm, which was at the iron and not at the screed and not at the air, and the warming traveled the way iron carried warming, and the floor under his knee began to be a floor that was not cold.

He held it a long count.

He let it go.

He took his palm off the plate.

He sat back on his heels.

The floor was warm. The floor was warm the way a floor was warm when a thing had set into the iron the iron was, and not the way a floor was warm when a man had pressed a press into a room and the room had taken what it took. The lattice would hold the warm for a length of time that was not forever. It would weaken. It would need feeding.

He stood.

He looked at the room.

"It'll fade," he said. "By the weather turn it'll be cool again."

A man at the back said, "And then."

"And then you feed it."

"With what."

"With what you have."

The man said, "I have a thumb."

There was a small laugh in the room. The laugh was the laugh of a room that wanted, this morning, to laugh.

"Then you feed it with a thumb," Ash said. "And the woman beside you feeds it with what she has. And the man beside her. The lattice doesn't care how much. The lattice cares that it's fed."

"And when it goes cold."

"It won't go cold all at once. It'll go cool."

"And when it goes cool."

"I come back. I set it again."

"And then we feed it again."

"You feed it again."

The man with the thumb thought about this. He thought about it the way a man thought about a thing that was not the thing he had been told a thing was, and after a moment he nodded, and the nodding moved through the room the way a small thing moved through a room when the room had decided to take the small thing in.

Tesha was at the second inlet.

She had her palm on the second plate.

She had not asked. She did not need to ask. Her pool was a small one, and Ash knew it was a small one because he had asked her at the bench last week, and she had said the number without flinching, and the number was the number of a person who had grown up at the back of the curve. She put her palm on the plate and she fed what she had into the lattice, and Ash watched her, and the lattice took what she gave it, and what she gave it was small, and what she gave it was not nothing.

The man with the thumb stepped up to the third inlet.

He laid his thumb on the plate. He laughed at himself laying the thumb on the plate. Then he stopped laughing, because the lattice took what the thumb had to give, and the thumb had a little to give, and the giving was not a joke when the giving was happening.

The room watched.

The room did not speak.

After a while Tesha took her palm off the plate, and the man took his thumb off, and the lattice was fed, and the floor was warm, and the warm was a warm that had come from three places and was, in the floor, one warm.

That was the answer.

Ash did not say so. The not-saying was the saying.

\* \* \*

He walked the district at evening.

The light was the long light. The long light came across the south block at the angle the south block was built for, which was the angle the survey had been drawn around, and the heating row was on, and the air at the row was the air of a row that was warm without being hot, and the pump was, at the bottom of the slope, not where it had been this morning. Pell's crew had moved it before the light went. They had moved it the way Pell's crew moved a thing, which was without ceremony and with one wrong cut and a swearing-at and the right cut after, and the line into the main was where Tesha had said it would be, and the standpipe at the bend was running, and a boy was filling a pail at the bend with the pail flat on the stone the way a pail filled when the water came at it from above instead of from below.

Ash watched the boy fill the pail.

He watched the boy carry the pail down the slope to the door of Building Six, which was downhill from the new pump, which was the way the woman with the yoke had wanted it to be six months ago and had not asked, because she had not been asked.

He stood at the corner of the lane.

The reservoir at the back of him was the reservoir it was, and it had moved this morning by the measure it had moved, and the measure was less than the spring measure for the same work. He did not, standing at the corner, do the math out loud in his head. He did the math out loud in his head every other moment of his life. He was not going to do it now. The not-doing-it was, this evening, the rest he was giving himself.

He should have seen it months ago.

He had not.

He saw it now.

He walked on.

\* \* \*

Tesha's workshop was in the storage room at the side of the cistern shed, which was a room she had not asked for and had taken, and the door was open and the lamp was on, and Ash came along the cinder path and stopped at the doorway and did not go in.

She had two people at the bench.

One was a woman of about her age in a cleaner's apron, and one was a boy of fifteen or sixteen with the careful hands of a person who had never been taught carefulness and had figured it out anyway. They had a pressure valve between them on the bench. The valve was off a heating element. The valve was the kind of valve Ash had hand-fit in the spring, when he had hand-fit everything, because in the spring everything had to be hand-fit.

Tesha had the valve in two pieces.

She was showing them the seat.

She was showing them the seat the way a person showed a thing when the person had figured out the thing themselves and had not been taught it, which was with a vocabulary that was not the workshop vocabulary and a sequence that was not the workshop sequence and a small confidence that came from having been wrong about it twice and right about it the third time.

"There," she said. "See. That's where it bites."

"That's where it bites," the woman said.

"And if you torque it in dry."

"If you torque it in dry."

"It bites the wrong way."

"The wrong way."

"And then it weeps in a month."

"In a month."

The boy was not speaking. The boy was watching Tesha's hands. The boy's own hands were laid on the bench in front of him in the rest position of a person who knew, at fifteen, that the way to learn a thing was to keep your hands still while another person's hands did it, and to put your own hands on the thing when the other person stopped.

Tesha did not see Ash at the door.

Ash did not step in.

The instinct came to step in. The instinct came the way the instinct came, which was the instinct of a man who had been the person at the bench teaching the seat for six months, and the instinct said *she's missing the part about the dry torque*, and the instinct said *the seat is bites two ways depending on the bevel*, and the instinct said *she's going to miss the bevel*.

She did not miss the bevel.

She got to the bevel a count after Ash thought she should have gotten to it, and she got to it in her own words and not in his, and the woman in the apron understood it in Tesha's words in a way that the woman would not have understood it in his, because the woman in the apron and Tesha had grown up on the same block and used the same words for the same things and Ash, for all that he had lived in this district for six months, had not.

He stepped back from the doorway.

He stood a moment in the cinder path with his hands in his pockets and the long light at his back and the lamp from the doorway on the cinders at his feet.

He walked.

\* \* \*

The crier was at the bottom of the curve.

He was not the regular crier. The regular crier was up at the seventh, and this one was a younger man with a voice not yet trained to the carry, and he was reading the evening list off the slate at the post, and the list was a list of districts, and the districts on the list were the districts where the heating had failed.

Ash stopped at the post.

He did not know he was going to stop. He stopped.

The young crier read the names. He read them in the order they had been chalked, which was the order the messengers had come in from the wards, and the order was not the order of the map. The map was in Ash's head. The map put the names where the names sat, which was at the north edge of the third, and at the upper terraces of the fourth, and at the long block at the south end of the fifth, and at two of the streets in the sixth, and the chalking went on, and the chalking was not stopping at any number Ash would have called a manageable number.

He listened to the end.

He did not write anything down.

He walked.

\* \* \*

The shop was warm when he came in.

Haran was at the bench at the back with his glasses on the end of his nose and a cup in one hand, and the kettle was on the iron plate, and the lamp was up, and Haran did not look up when Ash came in. Haran rarely looked up.

"Letter for you," Haran said. "By the door."

The letter was on the shelf at the door, propped against the candle box. The candle box was where Haran put a thing he wanted Ash to see and did not want to walk across the shop to hand him.

Ash took the letter down.

It was an envelope, plain, the kind Maren bought by the dozen at the stationer on the lower terrace. The stationer's mark was at the corner. The address was in Maren's hand, which was the hand he had known his whole life, and the hand was steady. The hand was steady on the *A* and on the *s* and on the *h*, and on the lane and on the number, and at the corner where she had written *by hand* in the underline she put under *by hand* when she meant a thing was not to go through the post, the underline was straight.

He took the letter to the bench under the lamp.

He sat.

He opened it.

The paper was a single half-sheet. The hand on the half-sheet was the hand that had been on the envelope, which was the hand he had known his whole life, and the steadiness of the hand was the steadiness it was.

The note said:

*Ash,*

*I have taken a leave from work. I will be at the apartment for a while. I am all right. Do not come unless you want to.*

*Maren*

He read it.

He read it again.

He folded it on the existing fold. He did not refold it tighter. He set it on the bench under his palm with his palm flat over it, and he sat at the bench with his palm flat over the half-sheet for a count and a count and a count, and the shop was the shop, and Haran at the back of the shop was at the bench, and the kettle was on the plate, and the warm was the warm.

After a while Haran said, "Bad."

"I don't know."

"All right."

That was all Haran said. Haran did not turn from his bench. Haran did not ask what the note said. Haran sat at his bench with his glasses on the end of his nose and let the asking be a thing the room did not do.

Ash put the half-sheet in his inside pocket, the outside pocket, the outside one this time, and he buttoned the flap.

He sat a count longer.

He stood.

He went to the kettle.

He poured a cup and brought it back to his stool and sat with it in both hands, and he did not, this evening, drink it for a long time.

\* \* \*

He was outside the shop when she came up the lane.

He had stepped out for the air, which was the reason he gave himself, and the air at the lane was the cold of an evening that had committed to being cold, and the lamps along the curve were the lamps they were, and he was at the post by the door with his shoulder to it and his hands in his pockets when she came up the cinders.

He did not see her until she was at the lamp.

She came in at the edge of the lamp's circle and stopped, the way a person stopped who had not decided yet whether to come the rest of the way, and Ash saw the coat first, which was the long grey coat she wore in the city, and the coat was the coat. The face came after the coat. The face was the face.

"Director."

She did not correct him with the first name.

That was the first thing.

"Walk with me," she said.

He walked with her.

They did not go up the lane and they did not go down it. They went along the side of the shop into the alley behind it, which was the alley that ran to the back of the cooper's yard, and the alley was empty, and the alley was lit only by the spill from the high window of the shop, and they stopped at the place where the wall of the shop came to the wall of the cooper's, and the corner held them, and she did not, when they stopped, look at him.

She looked at the wall.

She looked at the wall a long count.

When she spoke, the voice was not the voice she used in the office.

"I spent thirty years building systems that are going to die with the magic," she said. "You spent six months building systems that might not."

He did not answer.

He waited.

After a count she said, "I hate that."

"I know."

She did look at him then. She looked at him in the lamp-spill the way she had not looked at him in any room he had ever been in with her, which was the way a person looked at another person when there was nothing in the room between them but the thing they were standing on.

"I don't know how to do what you're doing," she said. "I don't know how to build things I can't control."

He did not answer that either.

He did not, in the not-answering, look away from her. He held her face the way she was holding his, which was without performance, and the holding was the thing.

She nodded once.

She did not nod at him. She nodded at the wall, or at the air, or at whatever it was she had come up the lane to say to and had said to.

She turned.

She walked.

She walked back along the alley the way they had come, and she went out at the lane the way a person went out who had not been going to stay, and Ash heard her boots on the cinders for a count and then for a count more and then not at all, and he stood at the corner of the wall in the lamp-spill, and the cold came at the back of his neck the way the cold came when a man had been standing in one place for longer than he had thought he had, and he did not, for a long count, move.

When he moved, he did not move for the door.

He moved for the lane.

He stood at the lane and he looked up the curve toward the lamps at the seventh, and he looked down the curve toward the lamps at the lower terrace, and he put his hand to the outside pocket and felt the shape of the half-sheet through the cloth, and he turned, and he went back into the shop.

Haran did not look up.

The kettle was still on the plate.

The cup was still on the bench where he had left it.

He sat back down on the stool and he picked up the cup, and the cup was cool now, and he drank it cool, and the shop was the shop, and the warm was the warm, and the thing in his chest that had been sore where the pride had stepped down off it in the morning was a different sore now, a sore that had room in it for the pump being where it should have been six months ago and for the woman with the yoke and for Tesha at the bench teaching the seat and for the man with the thumb at the lattice and for Maren's hand on the half-sheet steady and for Dorenne at the wall not looking at him, and all of them sat in the sore at once, and the sore was not bad.

He set the cup down.

The grey at the high window was not grey now. The high window was black. The morning would come at it the way the morning came at it. He would, in the morning, go up the curve again. He would walk the row. He would go to the apartment, or he would not. He would write to Joren. He would not write to Joren.

He did not, this evening, decide.

He sat at the bench with his hand flat on the bench and the lamp at his elbow, and he listened to the shop the way he had listened to the woman at the step, and to Tesha at the seam, and to the room at the lattice, and to Dorenne at the wall, which was the way of listening he had been learning, which was the way of listening that did not write anything down.

The shop ticked.

Haran turned a page.

The kettle, on the plate, made the small sound a kettle made when it was not being asked to do anything and was, all the same, warm.

Ash sat with it.
