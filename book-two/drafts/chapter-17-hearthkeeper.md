# Chapter 17

The shop was cold the way the shop was cold before anyone came down the curve in the morning, which was the cold of stone that had been stone all night and of iron that had given its working warmth back to the stone, and Ash had come down the stair in the dark with a candle in a tin holder and had set the candle at the corner of the long bench and had not, for a count, done anything else.

He stood at the bench in the candle's small ring of light.

The letters were where the letters had been when he had gone up the stair the night before. Three uneven rows. Tomas at the head of the leftmost. Iren in the middle. Beska's three lines at the bottom of the right row, the small even hand that had said *we are well*, and *the wheel held the spring planting*, and nothing else. The cloth from Ryn's bundle was folded at the corner. The going-out place at the bench's edge held nothing now. The boy had come yesterday at the second hour after noon, and the letter to Joren had gone with him down the curve, and the going-out place was the going-out place that was empty between letters.

Ash took off his coat.

He did not hang it on the peg by the door. He laid it on the stool at the corner of the bench, with the inside pocket against the seat and the outside pocket up, and the bench paper was where the bench paper was, and Maren's note was where Maren's note was, and the two pockets were on opposite sides of the coat the way they had been for five days. He laid the coat down carefully, the way a man laid a thing down he was going to want where he had laid it.

He went to the stove.

He did not light the forge. He had decided, in the dark of the stair coming down, that he would not light the forge until Haran came down, because the forge was Haran's, and the forge in the dark hours was a forge a man lit alone if he was the man who had lit a forge alone for forty years, and Ash was not that man. He had decided, also, that he would, in the dark hours before Haran came down, work at the kettle ring. He had decided this for a week. He had not, for a week, done it well.

He set the kettle on the ring.

The kettle was the small kettle, with the dent at the side where it had been dropped a long time before Ash had come into the workshop. The ring was the ring he had built. The ring was the ring that bled. He had built it in the first months at the bench, and Haran had let him build it, and Haran had not, when it was built, said the word *bleed*. Haran had set the kettle on it and warmed his hands at the rising warmth and had said, *mm*, and had gone back to the work. The ring bled because Ash had not, when he built it, known how to make a ring that did not bleed. Ash now knew. Ash had not yet rebuilt the ring.

He put his palm a finger's width from the base.

He closed his eyes.

He felt, in the dark behind his eyes, for the thread.

The thread was the word he had given the thing because Haran did not give it a word. Haran did not give it a word because Haran did not give the things at the bench words. Haran did the things and the things had no name and the names were the names a man gave a thing in his own head if he was the kind of man who needed to give a thing a name to do it. Ash was that kind of man. Ash had been, for a year and a half, that kind of man. He had named the loose press. He had named the wide draw. He had named, now, the thread, because the thread was what Haran did when Haran warmed the kettle and what Ash did not do when Ash warmed the kettle, and the thread was the thing he was here in the dark to find.

He felt for it.

He did not find it.

He found, instead, the wide loose press he had been finding for a year and a half, and the press caught the base of the kettle the way the press always caught the base of the kettle, and he held the press a count, and then he eased it. He eased it the way Haran eased a thing he was thinning down. He eased it inward. He tried to gather it in the way Haran gathered it, which was to find the small narrow channel inside the wide channel, and to let the wide channel go. He eased it inward, and inward, and the press narrowed, and narrowed, and at a place he had come to know in the last week, the press narrowed past the place where it could hold, and the channel collapsed, and the warmth went out of the ring under his palm, and the kettle was a kettle on a cold ring.

He opened his eyes.

He did not, for a count, do anything.

Then he set his palm again at the finger's width above the base, and he closed his eyes, and he felt for the wide loose press, and the press came, and he held it, and he eased it inward, and the press narrowed, and the press collapsed.

He did this nine times.

On the tenth he caught it.

He caught it for the count of three breaths, and the warmth came under his palm in a way it had not come before, which was a warmth that was thinner than the wide warmth and was, for the count of the three breaths, more of a warmth in the metal and less of a warmth in his arm and his shoulder and the small ache that came in his shoulder by mid-morning, and the kettle's base began to tick the small tick of metal coming up under heat, and on the third breath the channel collapsed, and the warmth was gone.

He opened his eyes.

The kettle had warmed at the base. He could see, by the candle, the small change in the dull of the metal where the warmth had been. The water inside, when he tilted his ear to it, was not yet making the small inside sound of water that was about to make a sound, but the water at the base was warmer than the water at the top, and the warmth was a warmth that had cost less than the warmth he would have made with the wide loose press, which would have warmed the whole kettle and the ring under it and the iron plate beside the stove and a count of the air over the stove, and which would have left him in a count with the small ache in his shoulder and the small wide tiredness behind his eyes.

He sat with his palm on the cold ring.

He did not, for a count, try again.

He thought about the three breaths.

He thought about them the way a man thought about a thing he had done once and would have to do again, which was with a kind of careful watching, because the doing of the thing once was not the doing of the thing, and the doing of the thing twice was not the doing of the thing either, and the doing of the thing was the doing of the thing on the morning a man had not slept and the day a man had a headache and the count when a man was in a hurry, and Ash had not, this morning, been in any of those, and the three breaths had been the three breaths of a man in a quiet shop in a candle's light at an hour when the city had not yet started.

He took his palm off the ring.

He put it on the stove's iron plate, where the plate was a little warmer than the air, because the stove had been warmed yesterday and not yet given all its warmth back, and the warmth of the plate was a warmth he had not made, and the warmth was a small comfort to a hand that had been holding a press for nine collapses.

He looked at the candle.

The candle had burned down a finger's width.

He went back to the kettle.

\* \* \*

By the hour the boy from the lane below brought the morning post, Ash had caught the thread four times.

He had collapsed it twenty-some. He had stopped counting at twenty. He was not sure, on the four catches, that the four catches were the same thing. The first had been the three breaths at the candle. The second had been a count shorter and had felt, in his palm, like the press he could not quite gather. The third had been longer and had ended not in collapse but in a slow loosening, the way a held breath was let out and not punched out. The fourth had been the second again, very nearly, and on the fourth he had thought he understood that the catch was not a single thing, was a place a man could come into in different ways, and the place was a place and not a route, and the routes were the work.

The kettle, by then, was warm at the base and not yet at the top.

The water inside was making the inside sound now.

Haran came down the stair.

He came in stocking feet, with his boots in his hand, the way he came down the stair in the early hour, because the stair was a thing he kept quiet for the boy from the lane below and for the woman in the room above the bakery on the curve, both of whom he did not want to wake. He set the boots by the door. He looked at the bench, and at the long bench, and at Ash at the stove with his palm a finger's width above the kettle's base.

He did not say anything.

He went to the cupboard. He took down the second cup. He set it on the bench beside the first cup, which was already there, because Ash had set out the two cups when he had set out the candle. He took the small brown pot from its shelf, with the dry leaves in the cloth at the bottom of it, and he set it beside the cups, because the leaves wanted the warm pot when the kettle was poured and not before.

He went to the forge.

He laid the kindling. He set the bellows. He did not, for the count of laying the kindling, look at Ash at the kettle. He laid the kindling the way he laid the kindling, which was without watching anything but the kindling, and which was the way a man laid a thing he had laid every morning for forty years.

When the kettle whistled, Ash took it off the ring with the cloth at the handle.

He poured. He poured the first cup and the second cup, and he carried Haran's cup to him at the forge, and Haran took it without looking up from the kindling, and Ash went back to the stove.

He stood at the stove with his cup.

He did not, for a count, drink.

Then Haran said, "Four."

Ash looked up.

"Mm," Haran said.

"Four what."

"Catches. This morning."

"How."

"You've a face when you catch it. You had it four times. The last was around the count after the bell on the lane below."

"I didn't know I had a face."

Haran nodded the small nod he nodded when a thing had been said that did not need an answer, which was most things. He took a long match from the tin on the corner of the forge. He lit it from the candle on the bench. He carried it back to the kindling and set it under the cross, and the kindling caught, the way the kindling caught when Haran had laid it.

"Four," Ash said.

"Mm."

"In two hours."

"Mm."

"How many do you catch."

Haran did not answer for a count.

He worked the bellows on the slow rhythm a man worked them when the kindling was a kindling that was just catching and the catching wanted air but not too much. He watched the small flame come up under the kindling. He let it find the bigger kindling. He let it find the small coal at the edge.

"I don't count," he said.

"You don't count."

"I don't catch and not catch the way you catch and not catch. I do the work."

"Yes."

"Counting is a thing for a man who's learning. You're learning."

"Yes."

"Count. Until you stop counting."

"And then."

"And then you'll be doing the work."

He did not say it the way a man said a thing he meant to be a kindness. He said it the way Haran said things, which was to put the thing on the bench and go on with the working of the bellows, and the bellows worked, and the small flame came up the larger.

Ash drank his tea.

He drank it slowly.

He thought, while he drank, about the four catches. He thought about the count of three breaths on the first. He thought about the small wide tiredness, which had been smaller, today, than it had been on other mornings. He thought about the kettle's base, which had warmed, today, with less than the warmth he would have made with the wide loose press. He thought about how much less. He had not, this morning, kept track. He thought, sitting with the cup in his hands, that he could keep track. He thought that he could, on a morning like this morning, with the candle and the kettle and the dark before Haran came down, work the wide press for a count of mornings and the thread for a count of mornings and weigh the cost against the cost. He thought that the cost of the thread, on the catches, was perhaps a third of the cost of the press. Perhaps less. He did not, today, know.

A third less.

He sat with that.

He did the small math a man did in his head when a man did small math in his head. He put the third against the reservoir. He put the third against the year. He put the third against the years.

A third was not a small number when the number was a year.

A third was not a small number when the number was a decade.

He looked at his cup.

The cup was the cup, and the tea in it was the tea, and his hand around the cup was the hand it was, and the hand had warmed the kettle this morning with less waste than the hand had warmed any kettle in a year and a half. The hand was not, this morning, tireder than the hand had been on a morning he had warmed the kettle the wide way. The hand was, this morning, less tired.

He set the cup down.

He did not, for a count, do anything else.

Haran was working the bellows on the slow rhythm. The kindling was a kindling now, and the small coals at the edge were taking, and the forge was beginning to make the small clean smell of a forge at the moment a forge was a forge again. Haran did not, while he worked the bellows, look at Ash. Haran did not, while he worked the bellows, ask him what he was thinking. Haran was holding the temperature, and the temperature was the temperature.

The thinking did not stop after that morning.

It was, Ash understood by the end of the first day, the kind of thinking that did not stop because the thinking had found a thing to do, and the thing it had found to do was the thing the thinking did when the thinking found a thing it could chew on. He had had this kind of thinking before. He had had it in his father's kitchen, in the hour after the number had come on the paper. He had had it at Maren's table, in the long evening, when Maren had been at the Hall and Davan had been at the press. He had had it in the south block, the day Tesha had asked the question.

He had it now.

It came in the spaces between things.

It came when he was carrying the bracket up to the cooling rack, and his hand was on the bracket, and the cool of the bracket against his palm was the cool of a thing that had been hot and was now not, and his mind, in the count of the carrying, was on the third less. It came when he was walking down the curve in the evening with a bundle of letters from Haran for the post box at the lower gate, and the curve was the curve, and his feet were the feet they were on the curve, and his mind, in the count of the walking, was on the years. It came when he was sitting at the table with the bowl of stew the woman in the room above the bakery had brought up at evening, and the stew was the stew, and the spoon was the spoon, and his mind, in the count of the eating, was on the math.

The math was a simple math.

The reservoir was the number it was. The number it was, on the paper in his inside pocket, was a number he had drawn a line under twice. Below the line was the smaller number. The smaller number was the number of the reservoir as he had been spending it. The smaller number was a number that ran out in a count of years that was a count he had been holding, for a year and a half, in his head, as the count he had.

The smaller number with a third less waste was a different number.

The different number ran out in a count of years that was longer.

How much longer he did not, on the first day, know. He did not have a pencil. He did not have the bench paper out. The bench paper was in his inside pocket, where it had been for a year and a half, where Maren's note was now in the outside pocket on the other side. He did not, on the first day, take the bench paper out. He did the math in his head. The math in his head was not the math on the paper. The math in his head said *longer*. The math in his head said *some years longer*. The math in his head said, also, that a third less was a third less he was doing now, badly, in two hours of a morning, four catches in a wide field of collapses, and that the badly was a beginning, and that a man who began at a third in two hours could, with a year, with two, with five, perhaps come to a half. Perhaps to more.

A half was a different number.

A half was, in the count of the years, a long extension.

He did not, on the first day, name the thing the thinking was doing.

On the second day he came nearer.

On the second day he was at the long bench reading Joren's letter, which had come back by the evening post the day before, which was Joren's first letter to him, and which was three pages in a hand Ash had not yet learned to read at speed. Joren wrote about the wool. Joren wrote about the linen. Joren wrote about the four winters before the linen, which had been four winters of wrappings that wet through, and about the winter the linen had come, by chance, on a cart from a tannery in the lower country, and about the next winter, which had been the winter Joren had thought he understood it, and about the winter after, which had been the winter Joren had begun to understand he had not yet understood it. Joren wrote, at the end, that he had written long because Ash had asked a real question, and because it was a question he had been answering, in his own house, for nine years, and because it was a kindness, after nine years, to write the answer to someone who would read it.

Ash read the letter twice.

He set it down.

He sat with it.

He thought, sitting with it, about the nine years. He thought about Joren, whom he had not met, in a house in Shen Pass he had not seen, in the cold of winters he had not been in, wrapping a house in wool and watching the wool wet through, and unwrapping the house, and thinking, and trying again. He thought about the years that had been in Joren's letter and the years that were not in Joren's letter, which were the years before and the years to come. He thought about how a man came into nine years of a thing. He thought about how a man stayed in nine years of a thing.

He thought, then, the thing he had not, on the first day, named.

He thought: *if I work at the thread for nine years, the math will not be a third. The math will be a half. Perhaps more. Nine years at the thread will be the difference between running out and not running out in my lifetime.*

He sat with that.

He did not, for a count, move.

The thought sat on the bench in front of him the way Joren's letter sat on the bench in front of him.

The thought was a thought that felt, when he turned it under the lamp, rational. It felt responsible. It felt like the kind of thought a man had who had stopped being a boy and had become a man who thought past the year, past the five years, past the ten. It felt like the kind of thought Haran had had, perhaps, when Haran had been Ash's age, in a workshop on a curve in a city, with a reservoir that was already smaller than his father's had been, and which he had decided, then, to make last.

It felt like the kind of thought Haran had had.

Ash sat with that, too.

He sat with that for a long count.

He sat with it through the evening, and through the slow walk down the curve to the lower gate with Haran's letters, and through the stew at the table in the room above the bakery, and through the dark walk back up the curve, and through the lying in the narrow bed with his hand on his chest and his eyes on the ceiling beam, and through the slow turning toward sleep that was not, in the night of the second day, a turning that came.

In the workshop, before the candle, on the third day, he caught himself planning it.

He was at the kettle ring. He had not yet warmed the kettle. He was sitting on the stool at the stove, with his hands in his lap, and his palms turned up the way a man turned his palms up when he had not, for a count, decided what to do with his hands, and his mind was running a list.

The list was a list of things he could let go.

The list was: the catalytic heating of new materials in the south block, which the workers could now do, with the conventional method, in twice the time and with a worker who could be spared. The list was: the foundation reinforcements for the elder districts, which were a thing he had been doing because he had been able to, and which were a thing the elder districts had, in fact, been doing without him for two centuries by other means. The list was: the small repairs at the proof-of-concept district, which he had been doing because they were quick, and which the workers could do, and were, on most days, doing. The list was: the lattice work at the upper terraces, which he could do once a season instead of once a month. The list was: the press at Hira's, which he could not let go.

He sat with the list.

He thought, sitting with it, about which of the things on the list he could let go without the thing on the list breaking. He thought about which of the things he could let go without anyone in the city, today, knowing he had let it go. He thought about which of the things he could let go without anyone in his life, today, knowing he had let it go. He thought about how a man rationed a thing across a decade. He thought about how a man rationed a thing across two.

He had a number in his head for the press at Hira's.

He had a number in his head for the lattice.

He had begun to have a number in his head for the foundation reinforcements when he stopped.

He stopped because his hand had moved. His hand had moved to his coat at the peg by the door, and he had not, until his hand was on his coat, known that he had stood up. His hand was at the inside pocket. His fingers were on the bench paper. He had been about to take the bench paper out, and to lay it flat on the bench, and to write, beside the smaller number, a third number, which would be the smaller number stretched by a third less of waste, and a number beside that, which would be the smaller number stretched by half, and a number beside that, which would be the smaller number stretched by more, and to have, on the bench paper, in his inside pocket, a ladder of numbers that ran, in his hand, in pencil, into a count of years that was not the count of years he had been holding for a year and a half.

He stood at the peg with his fingers on the paper.

He did not take it out.

He stood a count, with his fingers on the paper through the cloth of the coat, and he understood, in the count, that what he had been about to do was not the math. The math was not the thing. The math was, today, a way of doing the thing, which was the planning. The planning was a planning of a life across decades on the assumption that the life across decades was the life he was going to live, and the life he was going to live was the life of a man who held the reservoir, and held it, and held it, and used it for the things he could not let go, and let go of the things he could let go, and was, in the holding, the kind of man who had something other men did not.

The planning was the holding.

The holding was the planning.

He took his hand off the paper.

He stood at the peg a count.

Then he heard, in the dark of the shop, in the small ring of the candle's light at the bench, Haran. Not a word. Not a word at all. He heard the small dry sound Haran made at the bench when Haran was about to say a thing he had not yet said, which was the small dry sound of Haran clearing his throat the small clearing he cleared it in, and the sound was not a sound that was in the shop, because Haran was upstairs in the room with the small window over the curve, and the room was the room a man slept in until the second hour of the morning. The sound was in Ash's head. The sound was the sound he had heard, every day for two years, in the workshop, in the count before Haran said the thing on the bench that the thing on the bench needed said.

He stood at the peg in the dark.

He listened.

The voice in his head was Haran's voice.

The voice did not, for a count, say anything. Then the voice said, *I didn't choose restraint*.

Ash stood with his hand on his coat.

The voice said, *I chose the workbench*.

The voice said, *the efficiency was the tool, not the point*.

Ash sat down on the stool.

He sat with his hands on his knees. He sat the way Haran sat at the bench in the count after Haran had said a thing, which was to leave the thing on the bench and go on with the work, and the work, this morning, was the candle and the kettle and the ring that bled and the thread. He looked at the candle. The candle had not, while he had been at the peg, burned down. The candle had been burning the count it had been burning, which was a count of minutes a man could, if a man wanted, count.

He stood up.

He went to the kettle.

He put his palm a finger's width above the base.

He felt for the thread.

The third day he walked to the proof-of-concept district in the afternoon.

He had not, in a count of days, been down. He had been at the bench. He had been at the kettle. He had been at the long bench with the letters from Haran's network, which now numbered more than thirty, and which he had been reading, and re-reading, and carrying, a few at a time, into the corner of his head where he was beginning to have the small map a man had who had been reading the letters from Haran's network. He had been writing. He had written, in three days, a letter to Joren, a letter to Beska, a letter to Curran, a small note to Iren that asked a single short question about the linen and not about the wool. He had not been to the district.

Tesha saw him from the door of the south block.

She was on the step with another woman, whom Ash did not, for a count, know, and a young man Ash had seen before but had not learned the name of. Tesha was holding a length of copper wire and turning it slowly in her hand the way she turned a thing in her hand when she was thinking about it. She saw Ash come up the lane. Mira was the one who had come into the district from the outer ring three weeks ago, and who had taken to the mechanical systems the way some people took to mechanical systems, which was by sitting with them on a stool and listening to them, and Mira had, in three weeks, become the second-best diagnostician in the south block, and was on the step today because the heating element in Building Three had failed, and the heating element in Building Three had, on its case, the small mark Ash had stamped on the casing of the elements he had built in the first month, which was a small triangle and a small numeral, and the small triangle and the small numeral were the mark of a thing Ash had made.

"Show," she said.

"Yes."

"Not fix."

She nodded.

She nodded the small nod a woman nodded when she had been about to say a thing and had not had to say it.

They went into Building Three.

The heating element in Building Three was at the back of the ground floor, in the room that was the laundry and the warming room, and that opened on the small courtyard at the back of the building where the children were, in the cold of the late afternoon, not. The element was a column of stacked iron plates with the channeling channels he had cut in the sides of the plates, and the channels carried the warmth through the plates by the slow conduction of the iron, and the column stood on a small base of fired brick. He had built it the way he had built the other forty in the district, which was to a pattern Haran had laid out on a piece of brown paper on the bench one evening and that Ash had refined for the materials at hand and that he had, by this point, built so many of that the building of one was, in his hands, the building of a thing he no longer thought about.

Pell had taken the side panel off.

The side panel was on the floor.

Mira was at the column with a small lamp and her left hand flat on the lowest plate, the way Ash had taught the workers to feel the column when the column was not delivering heat, which was to find, by hand, the place where the warmth stopped going up.

She was on the third plate.

"Third plate," Mira said.

"Third plate," Tesha said.

"Cold above. Warm at."

"Conduction or contact."

"Could be either."

"Pell."

Pell worked the wedge slowly. Pell worked it the way a young man worked a wedge under the eye of the woman who had taken him on a month ago.

The wedge went in.

The seam opened.

There was, in the seam, a small dark line, which was the line of the paste, and there was, beside the line, a small bright line, which was a copper filing that had migrated into a position where it bridged the seam in a way it should not have bridged the seam, and which had, in bridging the seam, shorted the channeling channel through the fourth plate to the side casing of the column, and which had, in shorting it, sent the warmth that should have gone up the column into the casing, and the casing had heated, and the fourth plate above had not, and the warmth in Mira's palm had stopped at the third plate.

Mira saw it.

She did not, for a count, say what she had seen.

Then she said, "Filing."

"Filing," Tesha said.

"Migrated."

"In the seam."

"How."

"Heat cycling. Probably."

"Years?"

"Months. Maybe."

"Mm."

They sat with it.

Ash sat with them.

He sat on the small stool by the door of the room, with his hands on his knees, and he did not, for a count, say anything. He could see, from the door, the small bright line of the filing in the open seam. He could see, by the lamp, the way the casing under the third plate had a small darkening at the place where the warmth had been going for the count of weeks the filing had been bridging the seam. He could see the shape of the fix.
