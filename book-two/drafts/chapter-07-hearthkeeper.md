# Chapter 7

Building Four was cold by the second hour.

Ash came in through the back stairwell with Tesha behind him and a slip of paper in his hand that had, on it, the same three words it had carried yesterday: *Four out again.* The stairwell smelled of damp stone and of the chalk a child had been drawing with on the underside of the landing. Someone had drawn a fish. The fish had a hat. The hat had a feather. He went past the fish and pushed the door at the top.

The hall on the second floor held no warmth that he could name. Not cold, the way the street was cold; cold the way a room becomes when the thing that has been keeping it warm has stopped, and the walls have not yet decided which temperature to be.

A woman stood in the corridor with a baby on her hip and a shawl wrapped twice. She did not look surprised to see him.

"It went out in the night," she said. "Around the third hour. My mother felt it first. She wakes for the cold."

"Which wall."

"The one between us and the Tarrens." She nodded down the hall. "Theirs is colder."

"We'll look."

"You said that yesterday."

"We did."

She adjusted the baby. The baby had the open, indifferent face of a baby being carried past a thing it had decided was not interesting. The shawl was the kind a woman knit herself, in a color that had been a color twenty years ago and was now the color a thing became after being washed in lower-district water for two decades.

"We'll fix it," Ash said.

"Mm."

She did not move out of the corridor. She watched them go down it. Ash heard the baby make a small sound behind him, the particular sound a baby made when it had decided to begin negotiating with the adult holding it, and the woman's voice, low, soothing, a few words he did not catch.

Tesha went down on one knee at the wall between Building Four and the Tarrens'. She set her palm flat. She held it there.

"Cold."

"Yes."

"Two seams."

"Where."

She slid her hand. She slid it again. She tapped, twice, with the back of her knuckle.

"Here. And here. The patch above the bracket and the one near the floor."

"You laid both of those."

"I laid the one above the bracket. The one near the floor was the new man. Berrin."

"It's failed first."

"It's the cold one. Mine's the warm one."

She said it the way she said everything about her own work, without pride and without apology, the way a person who has counted her stitches all her life counts them on this wall too. She got up off her knee. She pulled the cloth bag of tools from her shoulder and set it on the floor and opened it, and the small things inside it, the little hammers and the rolled spool of seal-cord and the short flat blade for cutting mortar, settled into the order she had decided they should settle into.

"I'll do both," she said. "The bracket one'll go again if I don't redo it next to the new one. They have to match."

"How long."

"This morning. If you bring me Berrin and we work both. Without him, this afternoon."

"I'll find him."

"He's at the basin. He's been at the basin since the eighth hour yesterday. He's not coming back."

Ash looked at her.

"He quit?"

"He quit. He told Vell. Vell told me. I was going to tell you when you came in."

"What did he say."

Tesha set the small hammer down. She put her hands on her thighs and pushed herself the rest of the way to standing, the way a woman in her fifties pushed herself up from a kneel after the third one of a morning.

"He said he was tired of being talked to like a child. He said he'd been laying brick since he was fourteen and he didn't need to be shown how to hold a trowel. He said other things. The shape of what he said was that."

"Did you say anything to him."

"I told him he wasn't laying brick. I told him a flue seam wasn't a brick. He said a wall was a wall."

"Was he angry."

"He was tired."

She bent and picked up the bag. She set the strap across her shoulder. The strap had worn the cloth of her coat in a line at the collarbone, the soft groove of a thing carried in the same place every day for years, and Ash noticed it for the first time, and noticed himself noticing it, and set the noticing down.

"That's two," he said.

"Two."

"In a week."

"Mm."

"What did the other one say."

"Marda? She said the same thing."

"The same."

"Different words. Same shape."

She walked past him down the corridor toward the Tarrens' door. The woman with the baby had gone back inside. Somewhere on the floor above a kettle whistled, a real kettle on a real stove, and a child shouted a word that might have been a name and might have been a complaint, and the building did its slow ordinary work of being a place people lived in while two people in the corridor took it apart and put it back together.

* * *

The pump in the eastern row was waiting when they came down.

Vell had taken the cover off and laid it folded on the platform. She was on her knees beside the cylinder with a finger inside the priming valve, feeling the seat. She did not look up when Ash crossed the square.

"It's the spring," she said. "I can hear it. It's the spring on the upstroke."

"The relief?"

"No. The check. The check on the discharge. It's clicking late."

"How late."

"Late enough that I lose half a stroke before it seats. I'm pumping water back through my own valve."

She pulled her finger out. She wiped it on her trouser leg. She sat back on her heels and looked at him for the first time, and her face was the face of a fifteen-year-old who had decided at some point in the night that this pump was hers and the pump's failure was therefore also hers, and the two things were one thing, and there was nothing on the platform between them but the question of when it would be fixed.

"Haran can't make the spring," she said. "He told me yesterday."

"He told you?"

"I asked him. He said the wire's wrong. He said the wire we have here goes soft at temperature. He said we need a steel from the foundry up at Karrhall and Karrhall hasn't run for two months because their channelers won't keep the furnace at heat anymore."

"He told you all that."

"He told me some of it. I asked the rest."

Ash crouched beside her. The cylinder was clean. The platform was clean. Vell kept her pump the way a person kept the only thing they had been allowed to keep, and the cleanness of it, the small perfect order of the discharge nut and the wiped flange and the cloth folded square on the platform, was a thing he had been seeing every morning for two months and had stopped seeing.

He saw it now.

"What do you do," he said, "in the meantime."

"I pump on the half-stroke. The cistern fills in fifty-eight minutes instead of forty-one. People wait."

"Do they complain."

"They wait."

She said it without inflection. She pulled the cloth from the platform and refolded it. She set it down again.

"There's a man," Vell said. "Came yesterday. Asked about the pump. Said he might know somebody who could wind the wire."

"What man."

"From outside the city. He's at the workshop with Haran now. He's been there since sunup."

"Has he been there since yesterday?"

"He slept somewhere. He came back. He was at the door before Haran was."

She looked at Ash. She looked at him with the steady look she had given him every morning since the morning she had said *this one's mine*, and the look was the look of a person waiting for a piece of information she had decided she was entitled to.

"Should I keep pumping," she said.

"Keep pumping."

"At the half-stroke."

"At the half-stroke. I'll come back."

She nodded once. She put the cover back on the cylinder. She set her hand on the crank and did not turn it yet, the way a person rested a hand on the back of a chair before she sat down to a long task, and Ash crossed the square toward the workshop with the cold of the morning on his shoulders and the sound of Tesha's small hammer just beginning, two streets away, on a wall between two apartments.

* * *

The man at Haran's bench was thin and did not stand when Ash came in.

He sat on the upturned crate by the door, the one Maren had sat on three weeks ago, and he had a cup of Haran's tea in his hands and a bag at his feet and the bag was the bag of a man who had walked three days to the city with what he could carry. The bag had been mended at one strap with a different leather. The boots had been mended at the heel with a leather that did not match either of them.

He looked up at Ash with the unhurried look of a man who had spent his life looking at people across distances that were measured in fields rather than in rooms.

"Wellspring," he said.

"Ash."

"Ash, then. Pell. Out of the Long Acre."

"Long Acre."

"Three days northeast. Past the second river. We farm it. We farm what's left of it."

He did not stand. He did not extend his hand. He held the cup with both hands the way a man held a thing whose warmth he did not intend to lose to handshakes, and Ash sat on the second crate and Haran, at the bench, did not look up.

"Vell said you came about the pump."

"Vell said that?"

"She said you said you might know somebody who could wind the wire."

Pell's mouth moved at one corner. It was not a smile, the way Haran's mouth was sometimes not a smile. It was a thing in the same family.

"I came about the heating," he said. "Vell decided I was about the pump."

"You're not."

"Could be both. I came about the heating. We've been putting stoves in walls in the Long Acre for six years. We do it badly. Yours is better."

"You've seen ours."

"I've been in three of your blocks. Block Three, mostly. I was there yesterday. I sat with my back against a wall for an hour and I felt how it gave the heat back. We don't do that. We make the wall hot. The wall cools and the room is cold again. Yours is slow."

"Mass."

"Mass."

He nodded once. He drank.

"I came to ask Haran how he'd done it," Pell said. "I have stayed because Haran asks more questions than he answers."

Haran, at the bench, made the sound that wasn't a word. The file moved.

"What does he ask," Ash said.

"What our wire's like. What our stone's like. What we do for a flue when the brick we have cracks at the throat. What the wind does on the south face of a barn in the eighth month. He asked me how my mother heats her bread oven."

"How does she."

"With a stove she built herself when I was twelve. It's wrong. It works. She's been telling me it works for thirty years."

He set the cup down on the crate beside him. He laced his fingers around one knee. He had hands that were not large but had the deep grain at the knuckle of a person whose work had been mostly outside, and the nails were short, and the thumb of the left hand had the flat shine of a thumb that had been used as a tool more often than it had been used as a finger.

"You want to know about the wire," Pell said.

"I want to know about the wire."

"There's a man we know. Half a day from us. He winds for the smith in Threehollow. He winds for the foundry up there too, when it runs, which is less now. He's old. He'll wind what we ask him to wind if we ask him before the autumn, because in the autumn he won't, because he goes to his daughter's. I can write you a letter. He'll wind for a letter from me."

"You'd do that."

"I'd do it for the heating. I'd do it for the heating because I want the design and you want the spring and we can put one against the other."

He said it the way a farmer said it. He said it the way a man said a thing whose terms he had thought through on the road, three days of road, the kind of thinking a man did when he had three days of walking and one cup of tea at the end of it. He had not used the word *trade*. He did not need to.

"All right," Ash said.

"All right."

"I'll have Haran put the heating on a sheet for you. With the dimensions."

"Haran's been putting it on a sheet since yesterday."

Haran's file moved.

"He has," Haran said.

The file moved again.

"There's a thing," Pell said.

"What."

"Your wall. Block Three's wall. I want to ask. Because what I felt against my back was not the heat of a stove."

"It's the stone."

"The stone."

"The plaster is reknit. The stone is set against the inside face of the brick. The mass holds. The flue is on the other side, drawing through the stove."

Pell considered. He did not, for a long moment, say anything. He looked at his cup.

"You channeled the plaster," he said.

"I did. We did."

"With your reservoir."

"With my reservoir."

He nodded. He nodded slowly. His face did not change in any way Ash could name, but the shape of the silence after the nod was the shape of a man weighing a thing he had not been told he would have to weigh.

"That doesn't carry," he said.

"What."

"That doesn't carry. To us. We don't have your reservoir. We can't reknit the plaster. So what I am taking back, when I take this design back, is a wall I cannot build."

"You can build the rest."

"I can build the rest."

"The mass. The flue. The cooperative maintenance. The training."

"I can build the rest."

He said it again, slower the second time, the way a man said a thing twice when the first time had not closed the matter for him. Then he drank his tea and the matter closed for the morning, the way matters closed for men like him, on shelves that would be opened later when there was a wall in front of them and time to think with their hands.

"I'll write the letter tonight," Pell said. "Your wire'll come in two weeks."

"Thank you."

"Mm."

He picked up his bag. He set it on his knees. He did not open it. He sat with it on his knees the way a man sat with a thing that reminded him, by its weight, of where he had come from and where he was going back to.

"Tell me about your mother's stove," Ash said.

Haran's file paused.

It paused for a half-stroke. It started again.

Pell looked at Ash with the small, slow look a man gave another man when the man had asked him an unexpected question that turned out to be the right one.

He told him about his mother's stove.

* * *

The broadsheet came at the eleventh hour.

It came folded into the apron pocket of a boy who had run from the upper terraces with a copy and a coin from someone who had told him to put the copy directly into the Wellspring's hand and not into anyone else's. The boy delivered it accordingly. He stood in the workshop doorway with his cap off and the broadsheet held out in both hands, and Ash, who was at the bench helping Haran trim a length of seal-cord, set the cord down and took the sheet.

"From who."

"He didn't say his name."

"What did he look like."

"A man with a coat."

The boy was perhaps eleven. He had run hard. He stood on the threshold with the high bright color of a child who has been entrusted with a thing and has carried it without dropping it, and Ash thanked him with the small coin from the dish on the bench, and the boy ran away with the small coin and the high bright color, and the broadsheet, unfolded, lay on the bench between Ash and Haran's tools.

The headline was set in the second-largest type the press kept.

THE WELLSPRING'S WORKSHOP: CAN HAND CRANKS REPLACE CHANNELING?

Beneath it, in the smaller hand of the byline, *Filed by Tarrin Vesch.*

Ash read it standing.

He read the first paragraph twice. Then he read the rest. The piece was good in the way a piece was good when the person who had written it had walked the ground and talked to the people. Vesch had been in Stonecurve. Vesch had stood at the basin and put a hand under the tap. Vesch had spoken to a woman on the second floor of Block Three and had described, in his careful balanced sentences, the warmth of her wall and the surprise of it, and the woman's name was Berrana, which was the name of the woman Tesha had taught to lap her own flue seam two weeks ago, and the description of Berrana's surprise was accurate and faintly indulgent in the way a man wrote about a woman in a building he did not himself live in.

The framing was Dorenne's.

Ash saw it in the third paragraph and again in the seventh and again in the closing line. The proof-of-concept was an *initiative*, not a *project*. The initiative was *under the auspices of the Office of Infrastructure*. The initiative was *exploring*. The Office was *exploring whether mechanical alternatives might supplement, in the long term, the city's existing channeling infrastructure during the present transitional period.*

The word *supplement* did a great deal of work in the piece.

So did *transitional.*

Ash read to the end. He folded the broadsheet. He set it on the bench beside the seal-cord.

"Mm," Haran said.

"Yes."

"The framing."

"The framing."

Haran did not look up. The file moved.

"You knew it would be that."

"I knew."

"You're not going to fight it."

"No."

"Mm."

Ash picked the seal-cord up again. He measured the length. He cut it with the small flat blade Tesha had left on the bench when she had come in for the second hammer. The cord cut clean.

"It's a good piece," he said.

"Mm."

"He walked the ground."

"He did."

"He talked to Berrana."

"He talked to Berrana."

"She'll like that."

"She will."

The file moved. Outside, in the square, the sound of Vell's pump was the sound of a half-stroke fill: a longer rhythm than yesterday's, with a small click in it that had not been there a week ago. He listened for it. He found it. He set the cord down again.

"I'll go up this afternoon," Ash said.

"To Dorenne."

"To Dorenne."

"Mm."

"Not to fight it."

"No."

"To say I read it."

"Mm."

* * *

The second piece of post came at the twelfth hour, and the third with it.

They came together because Dorenne had sent them together, two letters that had come into her office and that she had forwarded to Ash with no note, in the linen-paper folder she used for correspondence she did not wish to be seen handling. The folder arrived by runner. The runner did not wait.

Ash opened the folder at the bench.

The first letter was on cheap paper, written in a hand that had been taught to write by someone careful. The hand had moved fast. The ink had blotted at one corner where the writer's wrist had rested too long.

*To the Wellspring, from the Young Channelers' Coordinating Group.*

*We have read the piece in the broadsheet. We have read it three times. We have walked the district and seen the work and we will not pretend the work is not real. The walls are warm. The water runs. The teaching is honest.*

*But the question we are asking is not whether your work is real. The question is what the work is for.*

*You have an ocean inside you. You are using it to build hand cranks for the poor while the powerful keep their channeled comfort. The Council member whose office is heated by a private channeler did not lose her heat in the earthquake. Her seamstress did. Her seamstress's children have spent the winter at a stove that draws too poorly and gives back too little, and they are warmer this week because of you, and that is a real warmth, and we honor it. But warmth that arrives one district at a time is not warmth. It is a gift, and a gift is not justice, and we have been waiting for justice while the gifts are distributed.*

*When does redistribution start?*

*Not when you are ready. Not when the work is refined. Not when the broadsheets are friendly. When?*

*We are writing because we want to know if the question has reached you, or if you have, like the others, become the kind of person to whom the question is invisible. We hope it has reached you. We hope you have an answer. If you have one, give it to us, and we will measure it. If you do not, we will continue to ask, and we will ask louder.*

*With respect, and without apology,
The Coordinating Group.*

He read it once. He read it a second time. He set it down on the bench.

He picked up the second letter.

The second letter was on better paper, cream-colored, the kind a man kept in a drawer for correspondence he expected to be filed by someone else. The hand was old, careful, deliberate in the way an old man's hand was deliberate when the old man did not wish to be misread.

*Ash,*

*I have read with interest the broadsheet's account of your work in Stonecurve, and I write in the spirit of long acquaintance with your mentor and the institution he has, against my judgment, helped to shape.*

*Mechanical alternatives are, in the present circumstance, a retreat from progress. The decline of our pools is real. It is also, I must remind you, partial. The infrastructure of this city has been built and maintained by channeling for nine generations. To turn from it now, in a moment of difficulty, to the implements of our great-grandfathers' great-grandfathers, is not adaptation. It is surrender.*

*We should be strengthening what we have, not abandoning it. Pooling our channelers more efficiently. Allocating the strongest pools to the most critical infrastructure. Investing in the discipline of channeling, not in the workshops of those who would replace it.*

*You, of all people, should understand what is at stake. You are the one among us with the capacity to demonstrate that channeling has a future. The eyes of the young are on you. What they see, when they look, is a man with an ocean choosing to dig wells. I beg you, for the sake of the work that has held our city up for three centuries, to reconsider where your energies are best directed.*

*With regard,
Councillor Hennen Talvern.*

Ash read it through. He read it again.

He set it on the bench beside the first.

The two letters lay there, the cheap paper and the cream paper, the fast hand and the deliberate hand, the question without an answer and the answer without a question, and the bench beneath them was scarred from forty years of Haran's tools and the lamp above them burned the same flame it burned every afternoon at this hour.

"Two of them," Haran said.

"Two."

"Together."

"Together."

"Mm."

Haran did not ask what they said. Haran did not, generally, ask what a thing said until Ash had decided to tell him what it said. The file moved. The brass shaving curled.

"They're both right," Ash said.

"Mm."

"They're not right about the same thing."

"No."

"The first one's right about the optics. The second one's right about the cost."

"Is the second one right about the cost."

Ash thought.

"He's right that I haven't answered the cost. He's wrong about which cost. He thinks the cost is to the institution. The cost is to the reservoir."

"Mm."

"Which he doesn't know."

"He doesn't know."

The file paused.

"And the first one," Haran said.

"What about the first one."

"Is the first one right about the optics."

"Yes."

"Is the first one right about the answer."

Ash did not, immediately, say.

The bench was warm under his hand. It was warm because Haran's lamp had been burning above it since the eighth hour. It was warm because the workshop's stove, which Haran kept low, had been holding the room since dawn. It was warm because the wall behind the bench was reknit and held what it was given, and the reknitting had cost Ash perhaps half a percent of the reservoir, and the half a percent was now, every cold afternoon, paying itself back to the room in the slow steady way a wall paid back what had been put into it.

"I don't know," he said.

"Mm."

"I don't have an answer yet."

"Mm."

"I'm building one."

"That's the same sentence you said last week."

"It is."

Haran filed.

"Build faster," Haran said. He said it without inflection. He said it the way a man told another man to ream a pipe before tomorrow.

"I know."

"Mm."

The file moved.

* * *

He went up to Dorenne's office in the afternoon and was back before the fifteenth hour, and the conversation he had with her did not need to be recounted, because the conversation he had with her was the conversation they always had now, two people speaking carefully across a counter that had once been a desk they shared.

He came down through the upper terraces by the stair he had begun to prefer, the long stair that switched back across the face of the second cliff and gave him, at each landing, a view of the lower city that was different from the view he had had at the landing before. The light at this hour on this stair was a yellow that did not exist in the upper city. It was the yellow of dust off the lower roofs. It was the yellow of the linen the dyers in the third district hung on lines along the southern wall.

He took the long way, because the long way passed through the public square below the third terrace, and he had heard, from the runner who had brought the linen folder, that there would be a gathering at the fourteenth hour, and he wanted to see it.

He did not, when he set out, intend to stop.

He intended to walk past it the way a man walked past a cart he had heard about and wished to see in motion, to confirm the cart was the cart.

The square below the third terrace had a name nobody used. The name was on a stone set into the wall by the public fountain, and the stone was older than the fountain and the fountain was older than the people who lived around it, and the name on the stone was *Threwen's Square*, after a councillor who had served in a generation nobody now remembered, and the people who used the square called it *the lower square*, and that was the name it had.

He came into the lower square from the west.

There were perhaps thirty people. Maybe forty. Mostly young, mostly his generation or younger. Some channelers. Some not. He saw the channelers by the way they stood, the small slight rest of weight that a person who had been channeling that morning carried in the hips for the rest of the day, the way a porter carried the morning's load in the shoulders. He saw the ones who weren't by the way they leaned forward.

The speaker stood on the rim of the fountain.

She was perhaps twenty-six. She had a coat that had been mended at the elbow and a voice that did not need to be mended anywhere. Her voice carried clean across the square without being raised, and Ash heard her, three steps in from the western alley, before he saw her, because her voice arrived first the way the voices of people who knew how to use them arrived first.

"...and the maintenance," she was saying, "and the maintenance is what they don't talk about. They talk about the construction. The construction is the easy part. The construction is one expenditure, one spend, one push, and then the pictures get drawn for the broadsheets and the broadsheets go up and everybody nods. The maintenance is what asks you, every week, every month, whether you meant it. The maintenance is what asks the question that matters."

She paused. She let the pause sit.

"Who maintains."

She let it sit again.

"Who maintains the mechanical pumps in the lower districts when the springs go soft. Who maintains the heating in Building Four when the seam fails for the third time. Who carries the pots up the stairs when the elder on the second floor cannot carry her own. Who sits with the question, when the question is hard, of whether the design is the design or whether the design is *one man's design* and the maintenance is therefore the maintenance of one man's mind."

A woman near her, in the front, said *yes.*

It was not a shout. It was the *yes* of a person agreeing with a thing they had been waiting to agree with.

"They will tell you," the speaker said, "and they will tell you with respect, that the work in Stonecurve is real work. And it is. We have walked it. We have stood at the basin. We have put our hands on the walls. The work is real. The water runs. The walls hold heat. Honor the work. Honor it."

She paused.

"And ask the question."

The question came clean across the square.

"What is the work *for.*"

She did not raise her voice. She did not need to. She built the next sentence the way Ash had heard one of Haran's tinkerers build a sentence about a load-bearing beam, every clause supporting the one before it.

"Because if the work is for the future, the future is fine. The future has time. The future does not have a child standing at a stove that draws too poorly. If the work is for the present, the present is asking, and the present is here, in this square, and the present is not satisfied with one district a season while the powerful keep their channeled comfort."

A man, somewhere in the back, made a sound. It was not a word. It was the sound a man made when a sentence had named a thing he had been carrying.

She saw him.

She was good. She was good in the way a person was good who had been doing this for some time. She used the sound. She let it sit.

She turned her head.

She turned her head in a direction that was the direction Ash had come in from, and her eye, which had been moving across the crowd at the steady pace of a person taking the temperature of a room, stopped.

It stopped on him.

He had not, when he came into the square, intended to be seen.

He had taken three steps in from the western alley. He had not taken his hand off the stone of the alley wall. He stood, now, with the alley wall at his shoulder and the square open in front of him, and the speaker on the rim of the fountain looked at him for a half-beat that was a little longer than a half-beat, and the half-beat ended.

She did not change her voice.

She built the next sentence on top of her own pause the way she had built the others.

"And there he is," she said.

The square turned.

Forty heads. They turned at the speed of forty heads turning at once, which was the speed of a wave breaking, and Ash stood with the alley wall at his shoulder and the wave came and broke against him, and the speaker on the rim of the fountain held the pause she had built and built the next sentence onto it.

"The man with an ocean," she said, "asking the thirsty to wait."

The square did several things at once.

A woman in the second row drew her breath in. A man near the fountain turned and said something quick to the man beside him. A young channeler in a coat too thin for the season took a half-step toward Ash and stopped. A woman near the back, older, in a shawl the color of bread, put her hand on her mouth and kept it there.

Ash stood.

He felt the cold of the alley stone at his shoulder. He felt the warmth on his face from the southern light. He felt the reservoir, behind his ribs, the way he had felt it for three weeks now, the wall close, the boundary present, the volume held.

A young man near the front called out.

"Say something."

He said it the way a young man said it when a young man had decided the silence of a powerful person was itself an answer he disliked. Another voice, behind him, said *let him speak.* A third said *he doesn't have to.* A fourth said something Ash did not catch.

The speaker on the rim of the fountain did not move.

She watched him with the steady attention of a person who had built a sentence and put it where it would land, and was now waiting to see what kind of thing landed beside it.

He looked at her.

He looked at her for a half-beat.

He did not have an answer.

He had walked into the square with the broadsheet folded in his coat and the two letters folded under it and the conversation with Dorenne still in his shoulders, and the sentence the speaker had built, the sentence about an ocean and the thirsty, was the sentence he had been writing in his own head in different words for three weeks, and he did not, standing in the alley mouth with the wave broken against his chest, have a sentence to put against it.

He turned.

He went back through the alley the way he had come.

He did not run. He walked. He walked the way a man walked when he had been asked a question he could not answer in the time and the place the question had been asked, and the not-answering was its own answer, and he carried the not-answering with him out of the square the way he had carried the broadsheet in.

Behind him, when he was perhaps ten paces into the alley, the speaker resumed.

He heard her voice without hearing the words. He heard it carry over the alley wall the way her voice had carried over the square, clean, unraised, building. He heard the crowd respond. He heard a woman call out something. He heard, for one second, what might have been laughter. He could not, for the rest of the walk down to the lower stair, tell whether the laughter had been kind.

* * *

The light was low in Stonecurve when he came back.

The square had the warm dusty color it had in the third hour before sunset. Vell's pump was under its cover. Tesha had gone home an hour ago. The basin tap ran. A child crossed the square with a covered bowl, the way a child carried a bowl back to her family from a neighbor's stove, and the bowl was held at the angle a child held a bowl when she had been told the bowl mattered.

He did not go to the workshop.

He went to Block Three. He went up the stairs and stood on the second landing where the corridor branched, and he heard, before he saw it, the voice of a woman teaching.

He stopped at the landing. He listened.

"...and the third one's the cold check," the voice was saying. "If the third one's clicking late you've got a seat problem. You'll feel it in the handle. It'll come back at you on the upstroke, like that."

A second voice, younger, said something he did not catch.

"No. Like that. Feel it. There. That's it."

He came down the corridor slow.

The voice was Reska's. Reska was in the front room of the second apartment with a new recruit, a young man with his sleeves pushed up and a scrap of paper in his hand. She had a piece of the practice cylinder set on a low stool between them. She was not looking at the paper. She had not, Ash thought, looked at the paper since she had begun.

He stopped at the doorway.

He did not enter.

Reska saw him.

She did not stop teaching. She lifted her chin half an inch in the small acknowledgment a worker gave a foreman who had appeared in a door, and went on.

"The seat," she said, "is the part you can't see. You have to feel it. The way Ash, the way the Wellspring used to say, you can't trust a seat your eye can read. You have to ask it with your hand."

The young man nodded. He held the practice cylinder up. He tried.

"You'll feel a click on the third. You're not feeling it."

"I'm not."

"That's because the third one is what you're listening for. You can't listen for it. It comes when the second one is at the top of the stroke and not before. You let the second one come up. Then you wait. Then you feel."

She had her hand over his hand on the cylinder. She did not, Ash saw, look at where her hand was. She knew where her hand was.

"There," she said.

"There?"

"There."

The young man let his breath out. He laughed once. He had the open glad face of a person who had felt a thing he had been told he would feel and had, at the moment of feeling it, decided that he was going to be able to do this.

"Again," Reska said. "Slower. The seat doesn't reward speed."

"The seat doesn't reward speed."

"That's an Ash sentence."

"Is it."

"He says it about everything. You'll hear it. You'll start saying it. You'll catch yourself."

She laughed, the first laugh she had laughed in front of him that he could remember, the laugh of a woman teaching another person what she knew, and proud of what she knew, and proud of the man she had decided was the source of what she knew.

"Again," she said.

The recruit set the cylinder. He held it. He waited.

He felt the click.

He grinned.

She grinned back.

Ash stood at the doorway a minute. He stood through one more cycle. The cycle went the way the first one had. Reska said *the seat doesn't reward speed.* The recruit said *the seat doesn't reward speed.* Reska said *Ash says that*, and the recruit nodded, and the cylinder went around again, and the third click came when Reska said it would come.

Ash stepped back from the doorway.

He went down the stairs.

He stood for a moment in the stairwell, where the chalk fish with the hat and the feather had been drawn that morning, and he looked at the fish, and the fish was a fish a child had drawn, and the child would grow up in this building, and the building would be warm on the second floor, and the seam on the wall between the two apartments would, by tomorrow, hold the way Tesha's seams held, and the third click on the practice cylinder would come when Reska said it would come, and Ash had built this and was, in the building of it, present in every part of it, and he came out of the stairwell into the square with the slow careful step of a man who has been told something he does not yet know how to hear.

He stood in the square a minute.

Vell's pump was under its cover. The cloth was tied at the corners. The tap in the basin ran.

He went to the workshop.

Haran was at the bench. The same length of brass. The lamp burned.

"You're back."

"I'm back."

"Mm."

He sat on the crate by the door. He did not, immediately, speak. Haran did not, immediately, ask. The file moved. The lamp burned. Outside, in the square, the old woman with the covered pot, who walked the square at this hour every night now, walked across it on her slow steady track from one block to another. Ash had not, until this evening, known her name. He realized he still did not.

"Reska's teaching the new one," he said.

"Mm."

"She's good."

"She is."

"She uses my sentences."

The file paused.

It paused for a half-stroke. It started again.

"Mm," Haran said.

He did not say more. He filed.

Ash sat on the crate. The wall behind him was warm. The lamp was steady. The reservoir behind his ribs sat where it sat. The wall was there when he reached. He didn't reach.

Outside, in the square, the old woman with the covered pot reached the far side and was gone. The tap in the basin ran. A child somewhere on the second floor of Block Three laughed once, the laugh of a child who has felt a thing she has been told she would feel, and was hushed.

He thought *she's good.*

He thought *the seat doesn't reward speed.*

He thought *the man with an ocean, asking the thirsty to wait.*

He set the third sentence on the shelf with the other things he had set on it. He set the first sentence on a different shelf. He did not, that evening, decide which shelf the second sentence belonged on.

The file moved. The lamp burned. The workshop window, from outside, would have looked the way it had looked from the lower stair: lit, steady, a small warmth in the dusk holding what it had been built to hold.

He sat with his hands open on his knees a long while.
