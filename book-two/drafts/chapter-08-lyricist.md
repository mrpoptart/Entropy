# Chapter 8

The transcripts arrived at the second hour, before Ash had finished his tea.

Haran came in with the broadsheets under one arm and the bound transcript under the other and set them on the bench without speaking. He set them at the corner. He set them squarely. Ash watched the placement and registered the placement and Haran went to the file rack and took down the file he had been using the night before, and the file resumed.

Ash put the cup down.

The transcript was the official kind. Heavy paper, red string at the spine, the Council seal pressed into the cover in dry wax. *Public Hearing on Equitable Magical Resource Allocation, First Session.* He had not, in two months of district work, seen a Council document with that phrase on its cover. The phrase had been on broadsheets. The phrase had been on walls. The phrase had been in the letter from the Open Hand on the under-bench shelf. The phrase had not, until this morning, been on a Council seal.

He opened it.

The first page was the docket. The docket was long. He skipped to the testimony.

The first speaker was a woman named Selura Penn, of the Open Hand. The transcript noted her as *organizer, lower-quarter channelers' collective.* She had spoken for fourteen minutes. She had spoken in numbered points. The points were five.

*One. The decline is measurable. Two. The decline is unequal. Three. The unequal distribution is not a fact of nature but a consequence of policy. Four. Policy can be changed. Five. We propose the following.*

He read the following.

The following was a platform. Six articles. A registry of pools above a stated threshold. A schedule of contribution. A civic body to administer the schedule. A mechanism for hardship exemption. A timetable. A name for the platform: *The Even Draw.*

He read it twice.

The second speaker was a man named Toman Reach, who gave his trade as cooper and his pool as *modest, declining,* and who had spoken for nine minutes about the heat in his shop in winter. He had not given numbered points. He had given the temperature of his hands at the third hour on the morning of the first frost. He had given the name of his apprentice, who had left for the upper terraces six weeks before because the upper terraces were warmer. He had given the cost of the brazier he could not afford.

He had said, at the end: *I am not asking the Council to make me warm. I am asking the Council to ask why the terraces are warm and my shop is not, and to write the answer down where everyone can read it.*

Ash set the transcript on his knee.

He read on.

The third speaker had been a councillor. The fourth had been an elder channeler from the upper terraces who had spoken in the language of inheritance and stewardship and had said the word *prudence* eleven times by the transcript's count. The fifth had been a young man who gave his name as Davan and his pool as *weak* and his trade as *organizer,* and who had spoken for six minutes.

Ash read the six minutes.

The six minutes were short and the six minutes were tight. The six minutes did not name the Wellspring. The six minutes did not name a councillor. The six minutes named a number, which was the percentage decline in the lower-quarter pools over the past decade as measured by the registry the speaker himself had organized, and the six minutes named a pattern, which was that the decline was not even, which was that some pools were declining and some were not, and the six minutes named, at the end, a question.

*The question,* the speaker had said, *is not whether the decline is real. The question is who decides what we do about it. We are here, this morning, because we are tired of being a thing that other people decide about.*

The transcript noted the gallery's response. *Sustained reaction from gallery; chair calls for order; order restored after thirty seconds.*

Ash closed the transcript.

He looked at Haran.

Haran was at the bench. The file moved. The brass shaving curled.

"Davan," Ash said.

"Mm."

"You knew the name."

"I knew the name."

"For how long."

"A year. Maybe two."

He did not look up. He filed. He turned the work and filed the other side. Ash registered the turn, and the second side of the work, and the way Haran's hands moved on the second side at the same speed they had moved on the first.

"He's good," Ash said.

"He is."

"He didn't name me."

"He didn't have to."

The file moved.

Ash set the transcript on the under-bench shelf, on top of the broadsheet, on top of the two letters. The shelf had become a shelf where the things he was not yet ready to answer accumulated, and the transcript was on top now, and the transcript was the heaviest thing on the shelf.

He went out into the square.

* * *

The broadsheets came faster after that.

By Fourth-day there were three. By Sixth-day there were five. By the end of the week the broadsheets were piling on the under-bench shelf in a way that made Haran, on the morning of Eighth-day, take the oldest of them off the shelf and put it under the bench and say nothing, the way Haran said nothing about most rearrangements he made to the spaces other people had filled.

The platform had a name now, in print. *The Even Draw.* The name was on every broadsheet. The name was on a wall on the corner of the second terrace, which Ash had passed on his way up to his mother's on Twelfth-day and had not, that day, mentioned. The name was, by the second week, on a wall in Stonecurve.

He saw it from the workshop step.

It was on the eastern face of the building opposite the basin, painted in white lime, two letters tall, and the letters were not, he registered as he crossed the square to look, the letters of a child or a drunk. The letters had been laid out with a chalkline. The lime had been mixed with size. The painter had used a brush with bristles, not a rag. Whoever had put it there had wanted it to last.

*THE EVEN DRAW.*

He stood in front of it.

A woman came past him with a basket on her hip. She did not look at the wall. She looked at him looking at the wall, and she went past, and she did not, Ash registered, look back.

Reska came out of the cellar of Block Three.

"Morning," she said.

"It's been there how long."

"Three days."

"Three."

"It went up at night. The boy two doors down saw the painter. He didn't know the man."

"Did anyone wash it."

"No."

She said it the way she said most things. She said it without weight. She had her slate under her arm and she had the look of a person who had been on her way to the manifold and who had stopped because the conversation needed a beat, not because the conversation had taken her off her work.

"Should we wash it," Ash said.

Reska considered. She looked at the wall. She looked at the basin. She looked at the building above, where a window on the third floor was open and a curtain moved in it.

"It's not our wall," she said.

"No."

"And it's not a lie."

She said it without inflection. She looked at him a beat after she said it, the way Tesha looked at a worker after a phrase that had landed where the phrase was meant to land. Then she went past him to the cellar door of the next building, and the slate was under her arm, and the count was about to begin on the next manifold.

Ash stood in front of the wall a minute longer.

He went back to the workshop.

Haran was at the bench.

"It's on our wall," Ash said.

"I saw."

"Reska says we don't wash it."

"Reska's right."

"Mm."

He sat on the crate by the door. He did not, in the moment, pick up a tool. He sat and looked at the under-bench shelf, at the column of broadsheets, at the bound transcript on top of the column, and he registered that the column had grown another two fingers' height in a week and that the height of the column was the height of a thing he was not, by Sixth-day, going to be done thinking about.

* * *

The notice came on a Tenth-day.

It was small. It was printed on a half-sheet. It had been pasted, by someone, to the workshop door overnight, which was a thing nobody had ever done to the workshop door, and Haran had seen it on his way in at the second hour and had brought it inside without taking it off the door, which meant he had peeled it carefully, which meant he had decided, before Ash had seen it, that the notice was a thing to keep.

He set it on the bench.

*A Public Address. Halver's Square. Twelfth-day, Sixth Hour. Speakers: D. Telen, M. Reach, S. Penn, Davan. All welcome.*

Ash read it.

"Davan," he said.

"Davan."

"Last name on the bill."

"Mm."

"He's the draw."

"He's the draw."

Ash held the half-sheet between his fingers. The paper was thin. The print was clean. Whoever had set the type had set it carefully, without the small uneven lines that the lower-quarter shops produced, and the paper was a paper Ash had seen at the Council printer.

"They've got a printer," he said.

"They've had a printer."

"Better than I knew."

"Mm."

Haran did not look up.

"I'll go," Ash said.

"You'll go."

"I'll go in a coat."

"In a coat."

"Mm."

He set the notice on the under-bench shelf.

* * *

Twelfth-day came clear and cold.

He took the long way down. He took it in his oldest coat, the one from the year before the ascent, brown wool, the cuffs darned twice at the right wrist. He took the western stair to avoid the second-terrace market and he took the alley behind the cooper's shop to avoid the tea-house where two of the Council scribes took their afternoons, and he came into Halver's Square from the south, on foot, with his hands in his pockets, at the fifth hour and a half.

The square was already filling.

He had been to public addresses before. He had been to the address on the night of the Open Hand letter, and he had been, as a younger man, to the trade meetings at the Splits, and the difference between those gatherings and this one was visible in the way the people at the edges of the square were standing.

The people at the edges were not waiting.

The people at the edges had been organized into small clusters, four to six each, and each cluster had a person at its center who was holding a stack of half-sheets and was handing them, one at a time, to people coming in from the streets. Each cluster covered an entrance. A woman at the cluster nearest him handed him a sheet without looking at his face, and he took it without looking at hers, and he registered, in the half-second of the pass, that the woman had a list on a small slate at her hip and was, at the moment of his passing, making a mark on the list.

They were counting.

He read the sheet as he walked into the square.

The sheet was the platform. *The Even Draw. Six Articles.* The articles were numbered. The articles had subpoints. The articles ended with a line that read *Endorsed by 412 channelers and 1,109 residents of the lower and middle quarters as of First-day this week.*

He had not, on his shelf, seen that number.

He folded the sheet into his coat pocket.

The crowd in the square was perhaps four hundred. It was perhaps five. It was the largest gathering he had seen in Halver's Square in his adult life, larger than the festival on the spring of the year of his ascent, and it was not, in any visible way, festive. It was attentive. It was quiet in pockets and warm in pockets and it had the attention of a body of people who had come, on a Twelfth-day in cold, to listen.

He moved to the back. He stood by the eastern wall, in the doorway of the closed bakery, and he pulled his coat collar up, and he watched.

The plinth was occupied.

The first speaker was the man Ash had seen the night of the Open Hand letter, the tall one with the lawyer's voice. *D. Telen,* the bill said. He spoke for ten minutes. He laid out, the way he had laid out before, the structure of the morning. He named the speakers. He gave each one a sentence of introduction. He said Davan's name last. He said Davan's name without flourish, the way a man named the foundation stone of a wall he was building.

Ash watched the crowd while Telen spoke.

The crowd was not the crowd of the night of the Open Hand letter. That crowd had been thirty, forty. This crowd had clusters. This crowd had organizers at intervals, distinguishable by the slates at their hips, and the organizers were at intervals because the intervals had been planned. The organizers were holding the shape of the crowd the way the framing of a building held the shape of a building, and the crowd, registering the holding without naming it, was holding its shape.

Then Selura Penn spoke.

She had a voice like a teacher's. She was small. She was perhaps thirty-five. She read her five points in the order she had read them at the Council, and she read them without changing the order, and the crowd, on the third point, made a sound.

The sound was low. The sound was not a cheer.

It was the sound a room of people made when a thing they had felt without being able to say it had been said for them, and Ash registered the sound as a physical thing, as a thing the air did, and he registered that he had not heard a crowd make a sound like that in Solathis in his lifetime.

Penn finished. She stepped back. Telen returned to the plinth and named the third speaker.

Reach was a cooper. Reach was the man whose testimony Ash had read the week before. Reach spoke for four minutes about the temperature of his hands. He spoke without notes. He spoke with the small awkwardness of a man not used to a plinth, and the awkwardness was the thing that worked, and the crowd, when he stepped down, did not make the low sound. The crowd was quiet. The quiet was the kind of quiet a crowd was when it had been asked, by a man's small awkwardness, to sit with the temperature of his hands a beat longer than the morning had wanted to.

Telen returned. Telen named Davan.

Davan came up.

* * *

He was not what Ash had expected.

He had been expecting the tall man's build, the lawyer's height, the channeler's cleanliness of motion. Davan was none of those things. Davan was perhaps Ash's age. He was Ash's height, perhaps a hand shorter. He was thin in the way men were thin who had grown up eating what they had eaten, which was not enough, which had never been enough, and his coat was the coat of a man who had owned it for a long time and had it mended at the elbows by someone who knew how to mend an elbow.

He stepped onto the plinth without speed.

He stood for a beat without speaking.

Ash registered the beat. He registered that the beat was longer than the beats Telen had used and shorter than the beats Penn had used, and that the crowd, in the beat, had gone still in a different way than they had gone still for either of the speakers before.

Davan looked at the crowd.

He did not look at the back. He did not, in the first beat, look at Ash. He looked at the front, at the middle, at the side by the well, and he registered each section the way Othen had registered the square from the wall, without urgency, without performance.

Then he spoke.

"I want to start," Davan said, "with a piece of arithmetic."

His voice carried.

It carried without volume. It carried the way Haran's voice carried in the cellar, which was the way a voice carried when the speaker had decided that the voice was a tool for delivering a sentence and had made the sentence the work, and Ash, on the bakery step, on the eastern wall, two hundred feet from the plinth, heard *arithmetic* as clearly as he had heard Reska say *quiet morning* across the basin two days before.

"My grandfather," Davan said, "channeled. Modestly. He could heat a room. He could lift a beam off a fallen man if the beam was small. He used his pool, every day of his working life, in service of the trade he had learned, which was masonry, and which is mine, by inheritance and by hand."

A pause.

"My father channeled less. The decline, in our family, came down hard between his generation and my grandfather's. My father could heat a room only if the room was small. My father could not lift the beam. My father, when a beam needed lifting, used the lever and the wedge and the patience of a man who had been told, at twenty, that what his father had done with a hand he would do with a bar."

A pause.

"I do not channel."

The crowd did not move.

"My pool is weak enough that the registry the Open Hand maintains lists me as *trace.* I have the pool of a person who can light a candle in a still room on a good day, and only if the candle has been laid and only if the wick has been trimmed, and only if I have, that morning, eaten."

He did not say the last clause for sympathy. He said it the way he had said *masonry,* as a measurement.

"That is the arithmetic of three generations in one family," Davan said. "It is also the arithmetic of every family I know. It is the arithmetic of the woman who handed you the platform at the door of the square. It is the arithmetic of the man who painted *The Even Draw* on a wall in Stonecurve last week."

The crowd made a small sound.

The sound was different. The sound was not the low recognition. The sound was the sound a crowd made when a name they recognized had been spoken, and the name was the name of a place, and the place was a place some of them had been.

Ash held still on the bakery step.

"The arithmetic," Davan said, "is a slope. The slope goes down. The slope goes down in every family except some, and the some are the families that owned the masonry before mine and the foundries before my father's and the upper terraces before any of us could remember. Those families' arithmetic is also a slope. Their slope also goes down. But their slope started higher and goes down slower, and the gap between their slope and ours, this morning, is the gap between a room that is warm and a room that is not."

He paused.

He let the pause sit. The pause sat. The pause was, Ash registered, the same length as the beat Davan had taken before he had begun speaking, and the symmetry was deliberate, and the crowd, registering the symmetry without naming it, held with him through the pause.

"The argument," Davan said, "that we have all been having for as long as my mother has been alive is whether the slope is a fact of nature or a fact of policy. I am here to tell you that the question is wrong."

A beat.

"The slope is a fact of nature. The gap is a fact of policy."

The crowd made the low sound again. The low sound was longer this time. The low sound was not, Ash registered, applause. It was the sound of people who had been carrying a sentence in the back of their throat for years and who had just heard the sentence said aloud by someone who had said it well.

Ash heard the sentence land in his own chest.

He registered that he had heard it.

He stood very still.

Davan went on. He went on for another twelve minutes. He did not raise his voice. He did not speed up. He laid out the platform in different language than Penn had laid it out, in the language of the slope and the gap, and he named no person, and he named no Council, and he named no Wellspring, and the absence of names was not, Ash registered, an oversight. The absence of names was the architecture of the argument. The argument said *the gap is the thing,* and the gap had no face, and a man on a plinth who did not put a face on the gap was a man asking the crowd to put their own face on it, and to put the faces of the people around them on it, and to put the faces of the people the gap had been opened by on it, in their own time, in the order their own grief had assembled them.

Ash, on the step, registered the architecture.

He registered the joinery of it. The way the second sentence carried the load of the first. The way the third sentence took the weight the first two had laid. The way the closing sentences came back to the slope and the gap, and the way the slope and the gap were, by the close, not metaphors but objects, and the crowd had, by the close, been handed the objects.

The closing was eight sentences.

"There are people who say we should wait," Davan said. "There are people who say we should build alternatives. There are people who say the powerful will, in time, give what they have."

A beat.

"Waiting is what got us here. Alternatives are a question for our children, who will not have hands strong enough to use them if we do not, this winter, warm their rooms. The powerful will give what they have when we have made the keeping of it more expensive than the giving. That is the arithmetic of every change in the city's history that has survived its own enthusiasm."

A beat.

"We are not asking. We are not, this morning, asking."

A beat.

"We are organizing."

He stepped back.

He did not lift his hands. He did not open them at his sides the way Telen had. He stepped back from the front of the plinth and he turned, half a turn, toward Telen, who was already coming forward, and he came down off the plinth without ceremony, the way a man came down off a step he had finished using.

The crowd did not cheer.

The crowd made the low sound for a third time, and the third time it was sustained, and the third time it had, scattered through it, voices saying *yes,* and the *yes* was not the *yes* of a meeting hall. The *yes* was the *yes* of a single word repeated by a hundred throats at slightly different times, the way the responses had been when, as a child, his mother had taken him to the temple on the high feasts and the prayer had moved through the room as a thing each mouth had taken up at the moment the meaning had reached it.

Ash did not, in the moment, name what he was hearing.

He registered it.

He held still.

* * *

He looked at the crowd.

He had been, until that minute, watching the plinth.

He turned his head, slowly, and he looked at the crowd in the way he had learned to look at a system in the workshop, which was to take it in field by field, square by square, registering what was in the field before moving to the next.

In the first field he saw a man he did not know.

In the second field he saw the woman who had handed him the sheet at the entrance.

In the third field he saw Vell.

She was perhaps eight rows from the front. She had her hands in her coat pockets. She had her hair pulled back the way she pulled it back to work the pump, which meant she had come from her morning shift and had not, before coming, gone home to change. She was looking at the plinth. She was not, Ash registered, looking around. She was not looking for him. She had not come to the square to find him. She had come to the square to listen, and she was listening, and the listening was the listening of a person to whom a speaker had been speaking.

She was fifteen.

He had taught her, in the third week, how to seat a brass collar. He had taught her, in the fifth week, how to read the indicator on the cistern. He had taught her, in the seventh week, how to teach the new recruit how to seat a brass collar, and Vell had taught the recruit, and the recruit had, by Eighth-day, taught another, and the chain had gone four deep by the time Ash had stopped counting.

She did not see him.

He moved his eyes.

Two fields to the right, by the well, a man stood with his arms folded and his cap pulled down. The cap was a square gray cap. The cap was Velden's cap. It was not Velden. The cap was a cap of the trade, and the man wearing it was perhaps fifty, and he was not from Stonecurve. He was, Ash registered, a maintenance man from a middle quarter, which was a thing Ash could tell by the cap and by the cut of the coat and by the stance, the back foot weighted, the way Velden had stood in the workshop door.

Three fields further on he saw a woman in a blue scarf who had been at Building Four the morning the riser had gone cold. She had been on the fourth floor. She had been the wife of the man who had stood in the doorway with his arms folded. She had, that morning, said *thank you* to Haran in the cellar.

She was here.

She was listening.

She was nodding.

Ash registered the nodding.

He registered, in the next field, the boy who had quit Reska's crew. *Aren's boy,* Reska had said. *He stood behind Aren.* The boy was not standing behind anyone now. The boy was standing in his own row, with his hands in his coat pockets, in a coat that was not, Ash registered, a coat that the boy had owned a month before. It was a better coat. Someone had given it to him.

He moved his eyes again.

Five rows from the front, on the left, two men standing together. The first was tall. The first had the build of a stone-mover. The first was Tomek, who had come to the district in the fourth week, who had worked two days, who had not come back, and whom Reska had not, in the weeks since, mentioned. The second was a man Ash did not know, who had a slate at his hip, and who was, Ash registered, holding the slate the way the organizers at the entrances held theirs, which meant Tomek was, this morning, in a cluster, and the cluster had a center, and the center was the man with the slate.

He stopped looking.

He had looked enough.

He did not, in the moment, register the looking as a count. He did not name the count. The naming would come later, on the climb, when he would, two streets up from the square, set the count down on the inside of his chest and find that the count was the count of the people he had, in his own ledger, on his own shelf, marked as the people the work was for.

The work was for Vell. The work was for the wife in the blue scarf. The work was for the boy in the new coat who had quit because the slow had gotten to him. The work was for the maintenance man from the middle quarter, who had not, this morning, come down to ask about a supplemental, who had come down to listen.

He registered that the work was for them.

He registered that Davan had also been speaking for them.

He registered that the speaking and the work were not, this morning, on the same side.

He registered the registering and he set it down where the registering went, on the inside of his chest, and the place was getting full.

* * *

He left before the rally ended.

He left before Telen returned to close. He left in the middle of the low sound, while the crowd was still holding the third *yes,* and he went out the south entrance, the one he had come in by, and the woman with the slate at the entrance did not, this time, hand him a sheet, because the sheets were for the coming-in and not for the going-out, and he registered that the cluster had organized for both directions but that the going-out cluster's job was the count and not the platform.

She marked her slate.

He climbed.

He took the back stair behind the cooper's. He took the alley. He came up through the second terrace and he did not go to his mother's, although it was a Twelfth-day, and he did not, at the corner where the alley met the terrace street, stop to read the wall the lower city had been writing on, although the wall had been added to since he had passed it on his way down.

He climbed.

He did not, for two streets, think clearly.

He thought *the slope is a fact of nature.* He thought *the gap is a fact of policy.* He thought *I would have written that sentence.* He thought, *I did write that sentence, in my head, at twenty-two, walking past Hellen's house in the winter that the upper terraces' braziers had run all night and our brazier had run for an hour because my father had decided to save the rest for the morning when my mother would need to wash.*

He thought, *Davan said it.*

He thought, *Davan said it better than I would have said it.*

He thought, *Davan did not name me. Davan did not need to.*

He climbed.

He came down into Stonecurve by the western stair as the dusk began. The square was the square. The basin tap ran. Vell's pump was under its cover. Vell was not in the square. Vell was, perhaps, still in Halver's Square, walking back with the boy in the new coat or with the wife in the blue scarf, and the walk back was, perhaps, the walk on which the cluster did the work that the speaking had begun, the walk on which the platform became, for the people in it, a thing they had decided.

He did not go to the workshop.

He went to the eastern pump.

* * *

The pump was running.

He stopped at the edge of the platform.

He did not climb up. He stood at the edge, with his hands in his coat pockets, and he watched. The morning crew had gone home. The evening crew was setting up at the bench inside the cellar door. The crank turned. The hum was the hum. The arm had not, in the three weeks since the wrap, buzzed. The wrap held. Haran's wire and rosin held. The replacement arm would arrive on Sixth-day next.

The cistern indicator stood at its mark.

He registered the mark.

He registered the hum.

He registered the cover at the corners, the way Vell tied it, the way Reska had taught the crew to tie it because Vell had taught Reska.

He registered that the pump worked.

He registered that the pump worked without channeling.

He registered that the pump had been built, brass piece by brass piece, hand-pump by hand-pump, joint by joint, over eight weeks, by a crew that had been, at the start, six people, and that was, by this evening, perhaps eighteen people, counting the new recruits, counting Jora's evenings, counting the apprentices Reska had taken on the Sixth-day before.

He registered that the pump served, in the eastern block, perhaps four hundred households.

He registered that the city had two hundred and thirty thousand.

He registered the arithmetic.

The arithmetic, this evening, did not give him the comfort it had given him on the morning of Velden's visit, when he had said, with Haran filing in the background, *eight weeks, six pipefitters, two apprentices.* The arithmetic, this evening, was the arithmetic Davan had named, the slope and the gap, and the slope of the pump's spread was the slope of a project that, multiplied by every district in the city, would not, on Davan's clock, warm the wife in the blue scarf this winter or the boy in the new coat or the cooper whose hands Reach had described.

The pump was a proof of concept.

The proof of concept was working.

The proof of concept did not answer Davan's question.

Davan had not, in his speech, asked the question. Davan had not needed to. The question was the architecture. The question was the unspoken column the speech had been built on. The question was *why should they wait,* and the answer that the pump, running quietly in the dusk in the eastern block of Stonecurve, was a proof of, was the answer *because, in a year, the system will work.*

A year was a long time.

A year was, in the wife in the blue scarf's winter, a winter she would spend in a flat heated by a riser that had gone cold in the third hour on a Twelfth-day morning. A year was, in the cooper Reach's hands, a year of mornings at the third hour with the temperature he had named. A year was, in the boy in the new coat's pool, more decline.

The pump hummed.

He stood at the edge of the platform.

Jora came out of the cellar door.

"Wellspring," she said.

"Jora."

"Long day?"

"Long day."

She did not ask where he had been. She did not look at his coat, which was the brown coat, which was not the coat he wore in the workshop. She had decided, perhaps in the first week of evenings, that the asking was not her job, and she went past him to the bench inside the door and she set her slate down and she began the count.

He listened.

He could hear her counting through the door. *One. Two. Three.* She was teaching the new recruit, who had been a new recruit for a week now and was, by Jora's account, ready to lead the count herself by Sixth-day next.

*One.*

*Two.*

*Three.*

The count was the count.

The count was for looking. The count was when you found the buckle before it was a buckle. The count was Tesha's count and Haran's count and his count and Reska's count and Jora's count, and the count was, this evening, the new recruit's count, and the new recruit was a girl whose name he had not yet learned, and the girl, by Sixth-day next, would teach the count to another, and the chain would go five deep, and the chain would, by the spring, be the longest chain of teaching in any district in Solathis.

The chain was the work.

The chain was beautiful.

The chain was a year long.

He stood at the edge of the platform and he listened to the count, and the pump hummed, and the dusk came down over the basin, and somewhere two streets over a wall was being added to, and somewhere four streets over a half-sheet was being handed at a door, and somewhere in the lower-quarter print shop a press was being inked for the morning's broadsheet, and the count, in the cellar, went *one, two, three.*

He did not, in the moment, name what he was thinking.

He registered it.

He set it down on the inside of his chest, on the shelf there, beside the broadsheet, beside the two letters, beside the transcript, beside the half-sheet he had folded into his coat pocket at the entrance to Halver's Square that morning, and the shelf was full.

He turned.

He went back across the square to the workshop.

The lamp was lit. Haran was at the bench. The file moved.

"You went," Haran said.

"I went."

"And."

Ash sat on the crate.

"He's better than I am," Ash said.

Haran did not look up.

"At what," Haran said.

"At the sentence."

"Mm."

"He said it the way I would have said it. Better than I would have said it. He said it without naming me."

"He didn't need to."

"No."

The file moved.

"Did you see anyone you knew," Haran said.

"Yes."

"Who."

Ash thought.

"Vell. The wife from Building Four. The boy who quit Reska's crew. Tomek. A maintenance man I didn't know but who I knew the cap of."

"Mm."

"And a hundred more whose faces I have seen in this square in the last two months and have not, until this morning, registered as faces I would see in another square."

"Mm."

He said it the way he said most things. He filed. The shaving curled.

"They were listening," Ash said.

"They were."

"He said the slope was a fact of nature and the gap was a fact of policy."

"Did he."

"Yes."

"That's a good sentence."

"It's mine."

Haran looked up.

He looked at Ash over the rim of his glasses. He looked for a beat. He set the file down. He took the glasses off and wiped them on his shirt and put them back on, the way he had on the morning of Building Four, the way he did when a thing had been said in the workshop that he had decided to give a beat.

"It's everyone's," Haran said.

"It's everyone's."

"Mm."

He picked up the file.

"It's not yours because you thought it first, boy. It's everyone's because the city, at the slope it's at, has been thinking it for a generation. He said it well. That doesn't make it his. It doesn't make it yours either. The sentence is the sentence. The question's what you do with it."

"What do I do with it."

"You don't write back today."

"Sixth-day."

"Sixth-day."

The file moved.

Ash sat on the crate.

He looked at the under-bench shelf. The shelf was full. The broadsheet, the two letters, the transcript, the half-sheet. The slope. The gap. The arithmetic of three generations. The wife in the blue scarf. Vell at eight rows from the front. Jora's count through the cellar door. The pump humming in the eastern block. The wall in lime two letters tall on the eastern face of the building opposite the basin. *THE EVEN DRAW.* The man in the better coat who had quit the crew. The maintenance man with the cap.

He registered them.

He set them down.

He did not, in the moment, lift the cloth off the half-beat at his sister's cup.

He went up to the room above the workshop, and he lay down on the bedroll, and he did not, for a while, sleep.

He thought of Davan stepping back from the front of the plinth. The half-turn. The way he had come down off the plinth without ceremony. The way the crowd had not cheered, because Davan had not asked for a cheer, because the architecture had not been built to be cheered for.

He thought of the architecture.

He thought of his own.

He thought of Jora at the priming valve, of *the Wellspring says,* of the chain of teaching that, at its longest, was a year.

He thought of the wife in the blue scarf.

He thought *a year is a long time.*

He thought *I do not have a faster answer.*

He thought *that is the problem.*

He set the thought down. The shelf, on the inside of his chest, took it. The shelf had room for one more, perhaps. It would not, he registered as he closed his eyes, have room for many more after.

The pump, in the eastern block, hummed.

The count, in the cellar, ran *one, two, three.*

The wall, in lime two letters tall, held the dusk on it after the last light had gone, and the lime caught the lamps on the second terrace and threw them back in white, and the people who came past the wall on their way home from Halver's Square came past it slowly, and some of them stopped, and some of them did not, and the wall held the words for them either way.

He slept.
