# Chapter 17

The workshop was cold the way the workshop was cold before dawn, and Ash was at the bench with his palm a finger's width from the cold iron of the kettle, and he was reaching for the thread.

He found the wide loose press first. He always found the wide loose press first. It was the shape of the way he had learned to channel, which was the shape of pouring, which was the shape of a year and a half of putting the reservoir into the city without thinking about what was leaving him as heat and what was leaving him as work. He let it go. He breathed out. He felt for the thread.

Nothing.

He felt for it again.

Nothing.

He took his hand off the kettle and shook it out at his side and put it back. He waited a count. He thought of Haran's hand on the rod the morning after Ardenmere came in, the small steady contact and the way the iron had warmed without the air around it warming, and he tried to think the shape of the thing the way he had watched Haran think it, narrow and clean and only the metal, only the metal, only the metal.

The thread caught.

For three counts it caught. He felt it in his palm the way he felt the small bright edge of a needle on a pad of skin, narrow and present and almost nothing, and the kettle, under his hand, warmed the kettle's warming and not the air, and the ring of the stove under the kettle did not warm with it, and his palm, which had been warming with the kettle every morning for a year and a half, did not warm. The kettle warmed. Only the kettle warmed.

He held it.

He held it.

He lost it.

The wide loose press came back in like water finding the level it knew, and the air around the kettle warmed, and his palm warmed, and the iron ring beneath the kettle warmed too, and Ash took his hand away.

He stood at the stove a count.

Then he went to the bench. He took the small slate Haran kept for tally and chalked a single mark on the left side, which was the side where he had started, three days ago, putting the marks for the times the thread had caught. There were not many marks. There were more than yesterday. The right side of the slate was for the times he had missed it, and the right side was crowded. He looked at the two sides. He set the chalk down.

The kettle came to a boil in the wide loose way it had been coming to a boil for a year and a half.

He poured.

He drank standing.

The shop was quiet the way the shop was quiet before Haran came down. The forge was banked. The high window was the first gray of the day. The letters from yesterday's post were on the long bench in their rows, and the letter to Joren had gone out at the second hour after noon two days ago, and there had not yet been an answer, and there would not be an answer for some number of days, because the road was the road, and Shen Pass was three weeks of road if the carter took the safe pass, and the safe pass in spring was the slow pass, and Ash had read enough of Haran's letters now to know which passes were which.

He set the cup down.

He went back to the kettle.

He warmed his hand near it. He felt for the thread.

He missed.

He felt again.

He missed.

He felt again, and on the fourth try the thread caught the way it had caught the first time, and held for a count, and slipped, and he chased the slip and ran the press in behind it before he had meant to, and the air went warm around his hand in a small wasteful bloom, and the cup on the bench, which still had tea in it, warmed too, and the tea was a thing that did not need warming.

He took his hand back.

He did not chalk a mark. He did not chalk a mark for the catch, because the catch had ended in the press, and he did not chalk a mark for the miss, because there had been a catch in there somewhere, and he sat down at the bench instead, and put his hands flat on the wood, and breathed.

The slate was the slate.

The marks were the marks.

The thread, when he had it, was the cleanest thing he had ever felt with his hand. Marginal. Two of three on a good morning. A third less waste, perhaps, when he held it. Not enough to change the math, his father would have said, in the careful voice his father used for numbers, and Ash sat at the bench with the cold cup in his hand and thought, *not enough to change the math*, and thought it again, and thought it the way a man thought a thing he was not yet ready to set down.

\* \* \*

He was at the kettle again the next morning, and the morning after, and the morning after that, before Haran came down the stair.

The slate filled. The right side faster than the left. He found, on the fourth morning, a kind of slowness in the reach for the thread that was not the slowness he had thought he was supposed to find, which was a calm slowness, but was a held slowness, like a man holding his breath under water and thinking about the breath while he held it. The catch came easier when he stopped thinking about the catch. The press came in when he started thinking about it again.

He learned to stop thinking.

He could not learn to stop thinking for very long.

On the fifth morning he caught the thread three times in a row on the kettle, and the air did not warm, and the ring did not warm, and the cup he had set on the bench did not warm, and the kettle came to a boil in a way that was not Haran's way but was not, any longer, his way either. It was a third way. It was a way Ash had not had before.

He chalked three marks on the left side of the slate.

He stood at the bench with the chalk in his hand, and he looked at the marks, and he thought, *if I can do this with the kettle.*

He set the chalk down.

He poured the tea.

He drank it standing at the stove, and the tea was hot the way a man warmed a kettle when he was paying attention, and he stood with the cup in his hands and thought the thing he had been not thinking for five mornings.

If he could do this with the kettle, he could do it with the rod.

If he could do it with the rod, he could do it with the bracket, and the lattice, and the lift in the upper terraces, and the press where Hira worked.

If he could do it with the press where Hira worked, he could do it with the south block.

If he could do it with the south block, he could do it everywhere he had been doing it before, and the everywhere, channeled with the thread, would cost him a third less than the everywhere had been costing him for a year and a half. A third less. Not nothing. Not enough to change the math, today. But the math was not a today math. The math was a years math. He had years.

He stopped.

He had not, in five mornings of practicing, let himself put the sentence down in the order he had just put it down.

He set the cup on the bench.

He took the cup in his hand and set it down again, because the first setting had not been a setting. He sat on the stool. He put his palms flat on the bench, the way he had been putting them flat on the bench for five mornings to stop his hand reaching back to the kettle.

A third less.

If he kept practicing.

If he kept practicing, the way Haran had practiced, for years, for decades, the math could shift. Not a third. Maybe half. Maybe more. Maybe enough that the reservoir at thirty-six percent was not a reservoir running out in the year he had been telling himself it was running out in. Maybe enough that it ran out in three years. Maybe four. Maybe never, if you spent it slowly enough, on the right things, with the thread instead of the press.

The thought arrived the way a thought arrived when a man had been not thinking it for a long time. It arrived whole. It arrived with the calm of a thing that had been waiting to be invited in, and had been patient, and had not knocked.

He put his head in his hands.

\* \* \*

It did not stop arriving.

It came on the walk down the curve to the south block. It came in the meeting at the long table in the second floor of the district hall. It came when Tesha asked him a question about a fitting at the corner of the new pump and he answered the question, and the question was a small one, and his hand had been on the slate at the bench through the answering, and the back of his mind, while he answered, was running the math again, the way his back-of-mind had been running the math for three days.

If he held the reservoir.

If he refined the technique.

If he spent only what he had to.

The math was a math he could do at the small slate at the corner of the bench, with chalk, in a count, while a worker asked him about a fitting. He did it. He kept doing it. He found himself doing it.

He found himself, on the morning of the seventh day, walking down the curve to the south block with a list of the day's work in his head, and the list had next to each item a small annotation, which was *yes* or *no*, and the *yes* and the *no* were not the *yes* and the *no* of *can it be done*. They were the *yes* and the *no* of *do I do it*.

He stopped on the curve.

He stopped at the third turn, where the wall of the lower wynd was warm under his hand because the wall there caught the morning sun, and the city below him was the city in the morning, with the smoke from the Levelers' kitchens rising in the gray rising lines they rose in, and the bell at the second tier ringing the third hour, and he stood at the wall with his hand on the stone and his list in his head and the small annotations on the list, and he thought.

*Isn't this what Haran did?*

The question came in his own voice, which was the voice that asked his honest questions, and which was also the voice that, in his life, had asked him for permission to do the thing he had already wanted to do.

He kept his hand on the wall.

The wall was warm. The bell finished ringing. A cart went up the curve below him, and the carter said something to the mule, and the mule did the thing the mule did, which was go up the curve, and the carter and the mule went past Ash at the wall and around the turn and out of sight.

*Isn't this what Haran did. Didn't the old man choose restraint.*

The question sat in the air the way a question sat in the air when a man asked it in his own voice and listened, in his own voice, for the answer.

The answer came.

It did not come in his voice.

It came in the voice that had said, four days ago, with a hand on the bellows, *Write to Joren*. It came in the voice that had said, on the first morning of the letters, *What would you do when you got there. Pour the reservoir into their fields until you left, and then what.* It came in the voice of a man at a forge with a rhythm in his hand, and the voice did not raise itself, and the voice did not argue, and the voice said, with the calm certainty of a man who had spent forty years being certain about a small number of things, *no.*

Ash stood at the wall.

He stood at the wall a count.

*No*, the voice said.

*Haran chose the workbench.*

He stood with his hand on the warm stone, and he heard it. He heard it the way a man heard a thing said by a man at a forge that was not raising its voice, which was the way a man heard a thing he had already known and had not let himself know.

Haran had not chosen restraint.

Haran had chosen the workbench.

The thread was not the point. The thread was the tool that let Haran spend the small pool he had on the small piece he was making at the small temperature he was holding, and the small piece, refined, was a part of a wrap that Joren was working on with Iren in Shen Pass, and the wrap was three degrees warmer in the cold week, and the third less of the wood ration, and the meeting hall before next winter if the linen came. The thread was for the work. The work was the point.

Ash took his hand off the wall.

The stone where his hand had been was warmer than the rest of the wall. He could see, when he looked at it, the small darker shape of the warmth, on the lighter stone where his palm had pressed. He had not meant to channel. He had not, in the standing at the wall, meant to channel. The press was the press. The press came in when he was not paying attention.

He looked at the dark shape on the stone.

He went on down the curve.

\* \* \*

He came up through the south block in the late morning.

The block was the block. Tesha was at the pump in Building Six with a wrench in her hand and Mira on the lever, and Pell was on the roof checking the line to the upper rain catch, and the day's work was the day's work, and Ash went past the pump with a hand on Tesha's shoulder in the way of greeting and went into the second courtyard, where the heating element on the south wall served the four ground-floor rooms of the western terrace, and where, this morning, the heating element had failed.

He had heard about the failure on the way up. Pell had told him at the gate. The element had gone cold in the night, and the four rooms were cold, and the residents had moved their morning to the second courtyard where the sun was, and the crew was going to look at the element after the pump.

He stood in the courtyard.

The element was the element he had built last autumn with Tesha, in the third week of the south block work, when the south block work had been *building things for them* and not yet been *building things with them*, and the element was a copper coil in a clay sleeve set into the south wall with a fitting at the bottom for the channel input, and the fitting was a small brass thing he had cast himself at Haran's anvil, and the clay sleeve had been laid by a mason from the third terrace whose name was Vell.

The element was cold under his palm.

He felt for the thread.

He found it.

He held it for a count.

He let it go.

He took his hand off the element. He stood in the courtyard. The four rooms behind the wall were the four rooms he could, in twenty minutes, with the thread, warm to comfortable, and could, in another twenty minutes, with the thread, channel through the input fitting back into the working state, and could leave warm and working and not need to come back to for a season. The element took, at the press, perhaps eight thousand of the kind of unit Maren used at the Hall, and at the thread perhaps five. He had run the numbers. He had been running the numbers for seven days. The numbers were the numbers.

Tesha came into the courtyard with the wrench still in her hand.

"Pump's done," she said.

"Mm."

"Element's cold."

"It is."

"You looked at it?"

"I looked at it."

"And."

He stood with his hand near the wall but not on it.

"The fitting at the bottom," he said. "The brass one. Could be the seat's gone."

She looked at him.

She had a way of looking at him, Tesha, that had grown into itself over the months in the south block, which was the way of a woman who had asked him a question once that had changed the shape of the work, and who had earned, by the asking, the small further question of whether the man she had asked was going to keep asking himself the question after she had stopped. She looked at him this way now.

"You think it's the seat," she said.

"I think it could be."

"You're not going to channel it."

"No."

"You could channel it."

"I could."

"In twenty minutes."

"In twenty minutes."

She looked at the wall. She tapped the wrench against her thigh, twice, the way she tapped a wrench against her thigh when she was thinking.

"Pell's on the roof," she said. "I'll get him down. We'll pull the fitting and look at the seat. If the seat's gone we'll cast a new one. Ash."

"Mm."

"That's an afternoon."

"Yes."

"And the four rooms are cold an afternoon."

"Yes."

She tapped the wrench. She did not look at him. She did not, she had not, in the months they had worked together, made the thing she was about to say into a thing he had to thank her for.

"All right," she said.

She went to the gate of the courtyard. She put her fingers in her mouth and whistled the sharp two-note whistle she used to bring Pell down off a roof, and Pell whistled back, and Tesha came back into the courtyard and took her tool roll off the bench by the wall and laid it open and started on the fitting.

Ash sat down on the bench by the wall.

He sat with his back to the cold stone, and the cold stone was cold against his back through the coat, and the four rooms behind the wall were cold, and the residents of the four rooms were in the second courtyard with their morning, and he sat with his hands on his knees and watched.

Tesha pulled the fitting.

She pulled it in the slow careful way she pulled a fitting, which was the way of a woman who had been a smith's daughter and then a smith and then a woman who had laid down the smithing for the south block work and had not, in laying it down, laid down the hands. She pulled the fitting. She laid it on the bench beside Ash, and she said, "Seat's gone," and Ash said, "Mm," and she said, "I'll cast one," and Ash said, "Mm," and she said, "Pell can run the bellows," and Ash said, "Mm," and Pell came in through the gate with his hat in his hand and his face streaked with the gray dust of the rain catch, and Tesha told him about the seat, and Pell nodded, and the two of them went out through the gate to the small forge at the corner of the courtyard wall, which was the small forge the south block crew had built in the spring, and which was the small forge they cast their fittings on when the fittings had gone.

Ash stayed on the bench.

He stayed on the bench through the casting, which took the rest of the morning, because the seat was a small precise thing and the casting had to be done twice because the first one came out porous, and through the cooling, which took the early afternoon, because the brass had to come down slow if the seat was going to seat, and through the setting, which took the late afternoon, because Tesha was a careful setter, and through the test, which Pell ran by hand on the input lever while Tesha watched the seat for the bleed.

The element warmed under Tesha's hand at the fourth hour.

The four rooms warmed at the fifth.

The residents went back to their rooms in the small movements of people moving back to rooms that had been cold all day, which were unhurried, because the cold of the day had been what it had been, and the rooms were warm now, and the warming was the warming of an afternoon's work and not of a man's twenty minutes.

Ash sat on the bench through all of it.

He did not channel.

He watched.

He watched Tesha, and he watched Pell, and he watched the slow rough good work of two people who were not him doing a thing he could have done in twenty minutes. He watched the small wrong guesses, and the second wrong guess, and the right one. He watched the porous casting come out of the mold and be looked at and set aside, and the second casting come out and be looked at and accepted. He watched the cooling. He watched Pell lean against the wall in the count between the cooling and the setting, with his hat back on, eating an apple from the bench.

He watched, and the voice came, the way the voice had been coming for seven days. It came with the small careful patience of a thing that had learned, in seven days, that it could afford to be patient.

*You could fix this in seconds.*

*You could fix everything in this district in a day.*

*You could be the solution again.*

He sat on the bench with his back to the cold stone.

He did not answer the voice.

The voice waited.

*Stay extraordinary.*

*Stay safe.*

He watched Tesha's hand on the seat.

He thought of Haran at the forge holding the temperature, and the slow rhythm of the bellows, and the way Haran did not look up when a thing was being held that needed holding. He thought of Iren's letter and the wool batt and the linen and the three degrees, and the meeting hall before next winter if the linen came. He thought of Curran saying no to two of his lads. He thought of Tesha pulling the fitting in the slow careful way of a woman who was going to cast a new seat for an element she had laid the wall around.

The voice waited.

He did not answer the voice.

He sat on the bench, and the afternoon went, and the four rooms warmed, and at the sixth hour Tesha closed her tool roll and Pell wiped his hands on the rag at his belt, and Ash stood up off the bench, and his back was cold from the stone, and his hands were cold, and his pool was the thirty-six percent it had been at dawn.

"Tomorrow," Tesha said.

"Tomorrow," Ash said.

He went out through the gate.

The walk home was the walk home.

He went down through the south block and across the lower terraces and up the long stair to the bench road, which was the road the bench road had been when his father had pointed it out to him as a boy as the road that was the spine of the city and went, if you walked it long enough, from the gate of the lower city to the curve below Haran's workshop. He walked it slowly. The light was going the way the light went in spring at the seventh hour, which was to a long flat gold along the top of the eastern wall, and the city under the gold was the city it was at this hour, which was the city in the small movements of evening.

He walked past the Levelers' grids in the second tier.

The grids were warm.

He could feel them as he walked, in the way of a man who had been pouring the reservoir into grids for a year and a half. The Levelers' grids in the second tier were the grids the cooperative had taken on three winters ago, and the cooperative did them by shifts, four channelers a shift, four shifts a day, and the grids ran warm in the cold months and warm enough in the shoulder months and were, in spring, the slow steady comfortable thing they were, with the small heat coming off the iron lattices into the courtyards where the children of the second tier played at the seventh hour. He walked past three of the grids. He walked past four. The fourth grid was at the top of the long stair where the stair came up onto the bench road, and the grid was warm, and the children at the corner of the courtyard were the children at the corner of the courtyard, and a woman on the bench beside the grid was knitting.

He went on.

He went on past the elder districts, where the houses were the old houses, and the windows in the old houses were warm, and the smoke from the chimneys was the small clean smoke of an elder's fire, which was the fire of a person who could afford to channel the kindling clean and had done it and had set the wood, and the wood had taken, and the fire was the slow good fire it was. The elder districts held. The elder districts had held for the year and a half he had been pouring, and had held for the years before he had been pouring, and would hold, the math said, for some number of years more.

He went on past the elder districts, and the road went up.

He came onto the third tier where the young districts were.

The young districts were the districts that had grown in the last twenty years on the slope above the bench road, where the Hall had let the building go because the Hall had needed the housing, and where the houses were the new houses, which were new the way new things were new in a city that built slowly, which was to say twenty years old, thirty, forty. The houses were the houses of the young. The pools in the young districts were the pools they were. The math in the young districts was the math.

He walked through.

The first courtyard he passed was cold. There was no smoke from the chimney. There was a child at the doorway in a coat. The coat was a coat for the cold of the morning and was a coat that had been put on because the inside of the house was the cold of the morning still, at the seventh hour, in spring, because the kindling had not been channeled clean and had not taken, or had taken and gone out, or had not been laid because the day's pool had gone to the work and not to the kindling.

He went past the courtyard.

The second courtyard he passed was the same.

The third was warm. The fourth was cold. The fifth was warm in the way a courtyard was warm when one elder lived in the corner house and the elder shared the heating with the four houses around the corner, which was the way the young districts had begun, in the last three or four years, to do the warming, which was by finding an elder and pooling at the elder's hearth, and which was a thing the young districts had begun to do because the young districts had no other thing to do.

He went on.

He thought, as he walked, of the hill country.

He thought of Iren's village above the second pass, with the two houses below the meeting hall in the wool batt and the tarred linen, three degrees warmer in the cold week, a third less of the wood ration. He thought of Beska's settlement and the wheel above the creek. He thought of Curran's village on the coast with the new sail. He thought of the people in those places, who were in their houses tonight, at the seventh hour, and the houses were warm or were not warm, and were warm not because Ash had been there, and not because Ash was going to be there, and the warmth, in the houses where there was warmth, was a warmth that did not have his name on it.

He thought of the courtyards he had just walked past.

He thought of the cold ones.

He thought of the ones where, if he had walked in, he could have laid his hand on the kindling and made the kindling take, and the fire could have burned tonight, and the child at the door could have been inside, and the coat could have been off.

He thought, *if I held the reservoir.*

He stopped.

He stopped on the bench road in the long flat gold above the third tier, and he stood at the wall above the cold courtyard, and the cold courtyard was below him, and the child at the door was below him, and the coat was on the child. He stood with his hands on the wall.

If he held the reservoir.

If he did not channel for the heating element this afternoon, and did not channel for the kindling tonight, and did not channel for the courtyard tomorrow, he could, in some number of years, with the thread, in some refined version of himself, channel for many courtyards over many years.

If he did not hold the reservoir, the courtyards he could channel for were the courtyards he could channel for now.

The math was the math.

The math had two answers.

The math, he understood, standing at the wall, was not a math.

It was a question.

The question was whether he was going to be a man who built something that lasted past him, or a man who held himself in reserve for the courtyards he could reach.

He stood at the wall.

The child went inside. A woman came to the door behind the child and shut the door, and the door was a door, and the courtyard below him was the courtyard, and the smoke came up from the chimneys of the elder districts behind him in the small clean lines, and the grids in the second tier below him were the warm grids, and the hill country was the hill country, three weeks of road in the safe pass, with the meeting hall not yet wrapped because the linen had not yet come.

He took his hands off the wall.

He went on up the road.

He came to the curve below the workshop at the eighth hour.

The curve was the curve. The lamp at the gate was lit, which meant Haran was in. The workshop above the gate was the workshop above the gate, with the high window dark because Haran did not, in the evenings, work in the high window's part of the shop. The forge would be banked. The bench would have, at the corner, the going-out place, and the going-out place would have, by now, perhaps, a letter. Or perhaps not.

He stood at the gate a count.

He did not, in the count, draw a line.

He did not, in the count, let the relief come back.

He understood, at the gate, what he had understood on the bench in the courtyard with the cold stone at his back, and on the wall at the third turn of the curve, and at the wall above the cold courtyard. He understood it not as a revelation, because revelations were things that came in bright light and changed the shape of a man, and this was not bright light, and he was the same shape. He understood it as a recognition, which was the way a man understood a thing he had already known and had been not letting himself know for some number of days.

He was going to keep building.

He was going to keep spending.

He was not going to hold the reservoir.

The reservoir was going to go where it had been going, which was into the city and into the work and into the lattices and the elements and the foundations and the catalytic firings of the new fittings at the new forges of the new district crews, and into the south block, where Tesha was, and into the second tier, where the grids were, and into the courtyards he could reach, and the reservoir was going to go because the reservoir was the thing he had, and a thing held was a thing not used, and the not-using, in the years he had, was not a thing he was going to do.

But.

He stood at the gate.

But the thread was the thread. The thread did not have to be the tool of the holding. The thread could be the tool of the work. The thread, applied to the spending, was not a slower spending. It was a smarter one. The element at the courtyard, refined, cost five of the unit instead of eight. The lattice, refined, cost less. The catalytic firing, refined, cost less. Each act, with the thread, did the work of three.

He had been thinking, for seven days, that the thread was for the holding.

The thread was not for the holding.

The thread was for the work.

He put his hand on the gate.

The gate was the gate. He pushed it open. He went up the path. The lamp at the door of the workshop was lit. He went in.

The forge was banked. The bench was the bench. The going-out place at the corner of the bench was empty. The letters from Haran's network were in their three uneven rows on the long bench, and Tomas's third letter was at the head of the leftmost row, and Iren's letter was in the middle row, and Beska's three lines were at the foot of the rightmost row. The cloth from Ryn's bread was folded at the corner. Haran was not at the bench. Haran was, the evening sound of the workshop said, up the stair.

Ash stood at the bench.

He took the bench paper out of his inside pocket.

He unfolded it. He laid it on the bench. The two lines were the two lines, in the small careful hand he had drawn them in two weeks ago, the line for the current rate and the line for the half-waste rate, and the half-waste line went a long way to the right of the current line, and the long way to the right was the years the thread could buy if he did the holding.

He looked at the lines.

He did not, this time, draw a third line.

He took Maren's note out of his outside pocket. He unfolded it on the bench beside the paper. He smoothed it with the side of his hand. The list was the list. The four words at the foot of the page were the four words.

*This is what it looks like.*

He stood at the bench with the bench paper on one side and Maren's note on the other, and he looked at the two of them, and the two of them did not contradict each other. They had been, in his coat, on opposite sides, for seven days, and he had walked the city with them on opposite sides, and they had been, the whole walk, the same paper.

He folded the bench paper.

He folded Maren's note.

He put them back in their pockets, which were on opposite sides of his coat, and which were, he understood, on opposite sides of his coat because that was where his coat had pockets, and not because the papers were on opposite sides.

He went up the stair.

He came down before dawn.

The shop was cold. The forge was banked. The high window was the first gray.

He went to the stove. He put the kettle on. He stood at the stove with his palm a finger's width from the iron, and he reached for the thread.

He found the wide loose press.

He let it go.

He felt for the thread.

It caught.

It held.

It slipped, and he chased the slip, and this morning he did not run the press in behind it. He let the slip be a slip. He felt for the thread again. It caught again. It held. It slipped. He felt again. It caught.

The kettle warmed the kettle's warming.

The air did not warm.

The ring did not warm.

His palm did not warm.

He held the thread for a count, and another count, and another, and the kettle came up the way a kettle came up when a man was paying attention, which was slow, and even, and without the small wasteful bloom, and the steam, when it came up, came up the way steam came up when only the water had been heated, which was thin, and rising, and going to the ceiling in the thin rising line steam went in.

He poured.

He stood at the stove with the cup in his hands.

The shop was the shop. The light through the high window was coming up in the slow gray way it came up in spring at this hour, and the dust above the bench was the dust, and the forge was banked the way Haran banked it, and the slate at the corner of the bench was the slate.

He went to the slate.

He chalked a mark on the left side.

He set the chalk down.

He stood at the bench a count. He did not, in the count, draw a line. He did not, in the count, run the math. The math had been the math for seven days, and the math was not, this morning, the thing the morning was.

The morning was the kettle, and the thread, and the cup in his hands, and the slate with one new mark on the left side, and the letters on the long bench in their three uneven rows, and the going-out place at the corner of the bench, which was empty this morning, and would, before he went down the curve to the south block, have a letter in it, because he had been thinking, on the walk home last night, of a question about the seat on the brass fitting that he wanted to ask Joren, who had wrapped a house in wool alone and watched the wool wet through, and who would know, perhaps, something about the porosity of a brass that came out porous on the first casting, because the porosity of brass and the wetting through of wool were perhaps the same kind of thing, which was the kind of thing a man learned by trying it, and which was the kind of thing a man saved a winter for the next man by writing down.

He drank the tea.

He drank it slowly.

He thought, with the cup in his hands, of the courtyard he had walked past in the long flat gold, with the child at the door in the coat for the cold of the morning, and he thought of the seat on the brass fitting, and he thought of the thread, and he thought of the years he had, and he thought of the years he did not have, and he thought of Haran at the forge holding the temperature in the slow rhythm.

The fear was there.

He had not lost the fear. The fear was there with him at the stove, in the cup, and in the cold of the shop before dawn, and in the slate with its uneven sides, and in the bench paper folded in his inside pocket, and in Maren's note folded in his outside pocket. The fear was the shadow the choice cast. The choice did not abolish the shadow. The choice stood in the morning light, and the shadow stood beside it, and the two of them were the shape of him at the stove with the cup in his hands.

He was going to become ordinary.

He was going to become ordinary deliberately.

He was going to do it with his eyes open, in the slow rhythm of a man who had been learning, for seven days, to feel for a thread, and who was going to spend the years he had spending the reservoir on the work, with the thread, until the reservoir was gone and the years were the years and the work was the work.

He set the cup down.

He went to the bench. He took a piece of the cheap paper Haran kept for letters from the stack under the lamp. He took the stub of pencil. He sat at the bench. He sat with the paper in front of him in the slow gray light of the high window, and he did not, for a count, write.

He listened to the workshop.

The workshop was quiet.

Above him, in the room above the stair, Haran was the small distant shape of a man in a bed before dawn, which was a shape that did not make a sound, and which would, in some count, make the small sound of a man getting up, and would come down the stair, and would come to the bench, and would say, perhaps, *Mm*, and would go to the forge, and would unbank the forge, and the day would be the day.

Ash put the pencil to the paper.

He wrote *Joren*.

He stopped.

He thought about the brass that came out porous, and the casting that took twice, and the seat that Tesha had set in the late afternoon, and the four rooms that had warmed at the fifth hour, and the children who had come back to the rooms. He thought about the wool that wet through, and the linen that was the part that mattered, and the meeting hall that would be wrapped before next winter if the linen came. He thought about the thread.

He wrote.

He wrote slowly.

The question was a real one.

It was not the right one.

He wrote it anyway, in the careful hand, on the cheap paper, in the slow gray light, and when he had finished he folded the paper, and he set it at the corner of the bench in the going-out place, with the folded edge facing the wall and the open edge facing the room, and he sat back on the stool, and the workshop was the workshop, and the slate at the corner of the bench had one new mark on the left side, and the kettle was warm on the iron plate, and the cup was empty on the bench, and the day was beginning to be the day.

Above him, on the stair, Haran's foot came down on the top step.

Ash listened.

He sat with his hands on his knees, and he listened to the slow careful sound of an old man coming down a stair he had been coming down for forty years, and the sound was the sound, and the day was the day, and the reservoir at thirty-six percent was the reservoir, and the years he had were the years he had, and he was going to spend them.

He was going to spend them well.

He stood up.

He went to the stove and put the kettle back on the ring, and the ring was cold, and he warmed it, and he warmed it with the thread, and the thread caught, and held, and slipped, and caught again, and the kettle came up in the slow even way of a kettle coming up clean, and Haran came down off the last step into the shop in the gray light of the morning, and Ash poured a second cup, and set it on the bench at Haran's place, and went back to the stove for his own.
