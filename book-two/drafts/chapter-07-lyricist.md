# Chapter 7

Building Four went cold an hour before dawn.

Ash heard it from the square. He heard it the way you hear a thing missing, the absence of the small ticking sound the radiator core made when the lattice was working, and he was up the stairs of Block Three with Dael two steps behind him before he had thought to put his coat on.

The flue was warm. The intake was warm. The seam at the cylinder was cool enough to lay a palm flat against.

"Lost the seal," Dael said.

"Where."

"Don't know yet."

Dael had a candle going under the cylinder. The flame leaned. Leaned. Steadied. Leaned the wrong way.

"There," Ash said.

"I see it."

The draw was wrong. The flue was pulling air it shouldn't have been pulling. Somewhere along the riser the plaster had cracked and the system was breathing in a place it wasn't built to breathe.

Ash sat on his heels next to the cylinder. He pulled his coat closed against his throat. The room was cold the way the city had been cold all winter, the channeled-warmth kind of cold, the cold of a thing that had been working and had stopped.

A child was crying in the next flat. A woman was telling the child it would be warm soon. The woman did not say *as soon as.* She said *soon.* The child accepted *soon* the way children accept any word their mother offers them at a fourth-watch hour.

"Get Tesha," Ash said.

"She'll be at the bench by sixth."

"Get her now."

Dael went.

Ash put his hand on the cold cylinder. He did not channel. He sat with his palm against the metal and let himself feel, for the count of ten, what it felt like to have built a thing that did not work.

Then he stood up and went looking for the crack.

* * *

By eighth-watch he had the crack. By tenth he had the patch. By noon Tesha had a worker on the riser with a mallet and the right rhythm, and Building Four was warming again, slow, the way a stove warms a room from the iron out.

The eastern-block pump was a different problem.

The part was a brass collar that fit between the priming valve and the cylinder head. The collar had cracked along a casting flaw that nobody had seen at fitting and nobody had seen at the second inspection and the third inspection, and the crack had run through the wall of the collar overnight. There was no replacement collar in the workshop. There was no foundry in the district that cast collars to that spec. There was a foundry in Velden that did, and Velden was four days by cart and seven by foot, and the collar was the kind of part you ordered three months ahead and waited for.

Haran turned the cracked piece over in his palm. He held it up to the lamp. He set it down on the bench.

"We'll cast one," Haran said.

"Where."

"The Splits."

"The Splits doesn't cast brass."

"They will if I ask."

He said it without lifting his head. He said it the way he said most things he had decided. Ash did not, generally, argue with the way Haran said most things he had decided.

"How long."

"Eleven days. Twelve."

"Twelve days without that pump."

"Twelve days without that pump."

Ash rubbed the bridge of his nose. The eastern block had two hundred and forty residents. The pump fed the cistern that fed the basin that fed the four buildings. Without the pump the basin filled by hand from the lower main, and the lower main was a block and a half down a stair, and the stair was iced at the second landing this week and the next.

"We can run buckets," Ash said.

"We can run buckets."

"For twelve days."

"For twelve days."

The bench was quiet a moment. Haran picked up the cracked collar. Set it on the shelf above the bench. Picked up the file.

The file moved.

* * *

Two of them quit by midday.

Reska came first. She came into the workshop with her coat buttoned and her hair tied back the way she tied it for travel, and she did not sit. She stood by the door with her hands in her pockets and waited until Haran looked up.

"I'm going," Reska said.

"All right."

"It's not the work."

"All right."

"It's the way it's taught."

Haran set the file down. He did not stand. He looked at her over the rim of his glasses.

"Say it," Haran said.

"Like children."

"Mm."

"You teach us like children. The pause, the look, the rhythm. The three taps and the wait. I'm thirty-one. I've held a mallet for sixteen years."

"You haven't held this mallet."

"It's a mallet, Haran."

"Mm."

She stood with her weight on one foot, then the other. Her color was up. Ash watched her from the second crate and did not speak.

"I'll be at Pellor's," Reska said. "Pellor said he'd take me back."

"All right."

"That's all you're going to say."

"What did you want me to say."

She looked at him. She looked at the bench. She looked at Ash, briefly, and Ash, who had not earned a part in this conversation, did not take one.

"I wanted you to ask me to stay," Reska said.

"I won't ask you that."

"No."

"Pellor's a good shop. Tell him I said so."

She stood a moment longer. Then she nodded once, quick, the nod of a woman who had said the thing she had come to say and had received the answer she had half-expected. She turned. She went.

The door closed. The file did not pick back up.

"Go on," Haran said, to Ash.

"I didn't say anything."

"Go on anyway."

Ash went out into the square. The basin tap ran. Vell was at her pump, the morning fill, the crank's pulse steady through the flagstones.

The second one quit before the bell at second-watch. A man named Garran, who had lasted six weeks, who had been good at the lapping and impatient with the pause. He did not come into the workshop to say so. He sent a boy with a note. The note said three lines. *Not for me. Wages owed through Tenth-day. Tell Haran no hard.*

Ash read the note. He folded it. He put it in the drawer where Haran kept the wage book.

He stood at the bench a minute. The brass shavings caught the light. The lamp burned low against the morning.

Then he went out and worked the eastern-block bucket line until fourth-watch, hauling water up the iced stair with the residents who had no pump, and the residents who had no pump did not know yet that they would have no pump for twelve days, and Ash did not, in the hours of the bucket line, tell them.

* * *

The water still ran.

That was the thing. The water in the rest of the district still ran. The walls in three of the four blocks still held the warmth they had been built to hold. The basin in the east square filled twice a day. The flue at Block Three drew clean. The seam at Block Two, the one Tesha had spent a week on, did not leak. Children carried buckets that were lighter than the buckets their mothers had carried at their age. An old man on the third terrace stood on the second day of the cold snap with his palm flat against his inside wall and said, to nobody, "That's a wall."

The residents had begun to name things.

The pump in the east square was *Vell's pump*, and had been since the first morning, and now the pump in the western block was *the cousin* because Vell's older cousin worked it and had named it after herself the way Vell had named her own. The basin was *the basin* still, but the cistern on the roof of the corner building was *the high one*, and the cistern on the roof of the bath-house had become, by some route Ash had not traced, *Granny.* Granny was full at sixth-watch. Granny was low at fourteenth. *Go ask Granny* was what a mother said to a child sent for water.

The heating systems had earned worse names. The system in Block Three was *the cough* because of a small intake noise it made when the wind came east. The system in Building Four, the failing one, was *the lazy one* before the failure and *the bastard* after, and the residents said *the bastard* with the affection a man saves for a tool that has, in the end, mostly worked.

Ash stood in the east square at fifth-watch on a clear day and listened to a woman say *Granny* to her daughter and felt, for the second it took the word to land, that the work he had done had become something he did not own.

He was not sure, in the second after, whether the feeling was a good one.

* * *

The journalist came on a Sixth-day.

She was forty, perhaps. She wore a coat the color of slate and carried a leather case with two notebooks in it and a stub of charcoal sharpened to a point that suggested she sharpened it more often than she used it. Her name was Sevra Hain. She wrote for *The Morning Plate*, which was the broadsheet Dorenne read, which was the broadsheet Ash had been reading since he was twelve.

"Mister Torren," she said, and held out a hand.

"Ash."

"Sevra."

She had a handshake that was a handshake. She did not try to make it more.

"I have an hour," she said. "Maybe two. Walk me through."

He walked her through. He showed her Vell's pump and let Vell answer the questions Vell wanted to answer. He showed her Block Three and let Tesha answer the questions Tesha wanted to answer. He took her up to the high cistern and made her climb the ladder, which she did, in her slate coat, without comment, and at the top she stood with her hand on the cistern lip and looked down at the square and made a note in a hand so small Ash could not, looking sideways, read it.

"This is your reservoir," she said.

"That's the cistern."

"I meant the design. The whole thing. The cisterns, the gravity feed, the cranks. This is your reservoir. The Wellspring's reservoir, on the roof, where everyone can see it."

"You can put it that way."

"Will I?"

He did not answer. He let the question sit between them, the way he had learned, in three months of watching Dorenne, to let questions sit between.

She made another note.

"The hand-cranks," Sevra said. "Be honest with me. Could a channeler do this faster."

"A channeler could do it faster."

"So why."

"Because there aren't enough channelers."

"There are enough for the upper terraces."

"There are enough for the upper terraces."

She looked at him.

"You're not going to fight me on this," she said.

"No."

"I'm going to write a piece that frames this as a complement to channeling. Not a replacement. Mistress Vol's been very clear."

"I know what she's been clear about."

"And you're not going to fight that."

He put his hands in his coat pockets. He looked out across Stonecurve, the slate of the roofs, the chimney stacks pale against the older brick, the small bright patch of the basin where Vell's pump caught the light.

"The work matters more than the framing," Ash said.

"That's a thing people say when they're losing the framing fight."

"It's also a thing people say when they mean it."

She made a note.

She wrote the piece. The piece came out three days later. The headline ran across the top of the second page in black letters a finger high:

**THE WELLSPRING'S WORKSHOP: Can Hand Cranks Replace Channeling?**

The piece was favorable. Curious. Slightly condescending. It quoted Vell. It quoted Tesha. It quoted Dorenne twice and Ash once, and the line it quoted from Ash was *the work matters more than the framing,* which Sevra had set, in the body of the piece, in a paragraph that made Ash sound like a man who knew he was losing the framing fight and had decided to accept it.

Ash read it at the bench with Haran reading over his shoulder.

"Mm," Haran said.

"Yeah."

"You're not fighting it."

"No."

"All right."

Haran went back to the file.

Ash folded the broadsheet. He set it on the shelf next to the wage book. Dorenne would have read it by now. Dorenne would, by tomorrow, have referred to *the workshop* in three meetings as *the workshop within the Wellspring's portfolio,* and the phrase *within the portfolio* would do, in those meetings, the work the headline did on the second page of *The Morning Plate.*

The work mattered more than the framing.

He told himself that. He told himself that on the walk back from the bench to the basin, and he told himself that at the basin where the tap ran clean, and he told himself that at Vell's pump where Vell was teaching a girl half her size how to find the priming stroke.

The walls were warm. The water ran. The framing was Dorenne's.

He could carry the framing for a while.

* * *

The middle-district crew chief came on a Ninth-day at the third hour.

His name was Korvin Delth. He was sixty-one. He was a square man with hands the size of a ham and a coat that had been good twenty years ago and was still serviceable because he had not let it get worse than it was. He arrived alone. He did not, immediately, say what he wanted.

He stood at the basin. He turned the tap. He held his palm under the water. He turned the tap off. He turned it on. He turned it off.

"That's a pressure," he said.

"Six meters of fall."

"Six is enough."

"It's enough."

He grunted. He walked to the pump. He did not put his hand on it. He looked at the linkage. He looked at the cylinder. He looked at the spring valve. He squatted, for an old man easily, and looked at the underside of the platform, and then he stood and brushed his trousers.

"You weld the brace," Korvin said. "Or pin it."

"Pinned."

"With?"

"Iron pins. Two."

"Three."

"Two's holding."

"Three's keeping."

He did not, for a few minutes, say anything else. He walked the perimeter of the square. He stopped at Granny's downpipe and looked at the bracket. He did not touch the bracket.

"My pipes won't hold pressure," Korvin said. "Channeling's thinning. We've got two channelers on rotation for three hundred families and one of them's sixty-eight."

"How long since they've held."

"Six years. Maybe seven. The drift's slow. You don't see it until you're on the wrong side of the morning fill."

"You're on the wrong side."

"I'm on the wrong side."

He said it without weight. He said it the way a man says a thing he has been saying to himself in the mirror for a year.

"What do you need," Ash said.

"I don't know yet. I came to look."

"You've looked."

"I've looked."

"And."

"And I'll come back."

He turned to go. He stopped. He turned back. He pointed at Vell's pump.

"That girl trained how long."

"Twenty minutes for the crank. Three days for the priming."

"I have a girl her age who'll train in ten."

"Bring her."

"I will."

He went.

Ash watched him cross the square. The square man walking even, his coat hanging straight from shoulders that had carried, Ash guessed, every shape of pipe a maintenance crew chief carried in forty years of work. Korvin did not look back. Korvin had, Ash thought, not looked back from a thing in his life since he was twelve.

* * *

The rural liaison came at the seventh hour and stayed until midnight.

His name was Beddan Haye. He was thin and tall and weather-cracked, and he came with a satchel over one shoulder and dust to the knee of his trousers, and he had walked the last half-day from the cart-stop on the lower road. His accent was not the city accent. He drew his vowels longer. He said *thee* where a city man said *you* in a register of formal respect, and he said it without putting any weight on it, the way a man uses a word that is just the word in the place he comes from.

He did not, at first, talk to Ash.

He looked at Ash. He nodded. He turned to Haran.

"Master Haran."

"Just Haran."

"Haran."

"Sit. There's tea. It's bad."

"Tea's tea."

He sat on the second crate. He set his satchel between his boots. He did not, immediately, take anything out of it. He drank the tea. He made no face.

"Pools went out at ours in the third year," Beddan said. "First year, late. Second year, half. Third year, the channelers couldn't hold the trough lines. We've been mechanical six years."

"Six."

"Six."

"What kind."

Beddan reached into the satchel. He took out a folded square of oiled paper and unfolded it on the crate between his knees. The paper was a drawing. The drawing was a pump. The pump was crude. It was crude in the way a thing was crude when a community had built it without a man like Haran, which was to say it was a pump that had been built by people who knew what a pump had to do and did not, for the most part, know what a pump should be.

Haran looked at the drawing. He took his glasses off. He put them back on. He set the file down.

"Bore?"

"Twelve."

"Bore's too wide."

"It's what we cast."

"Stroke?"

"Thirty."

"Stroke's too long for a twelve bore. You're losing the back end of every pull."

"We are."

"How's the linkage."

"Wood."

"Wood."

"Wood."

"In a wet system."

"In a wet system."

Haran took the drawing in both hands. He turned it. He held it sideways. He held it upside down. He set it back down on the crate.

"You're losing forty percent of your work to the linkage and the bore mismatch," Haran said.

"That sounds about right."

"Forty percent."

"It works. We just have to crank it longer."

"Forty percent."

He said it a third time. He said it the way a man says a thing he is offended by on behalf of the pump that is being asked to do its work badly.

Beddan, for the first time, smiled. It was not a wide smile. It was the smile of a man who had walked half a day to be told he was wasting forty percent of his work and had walked the half day specifically because he had hoped someone would tell him so.

"That's why I've come, Haran."

"Mm."

"We've a brass collar foundry. We've timber. We've the wrought iron from Tessen. We've twenty-two villages in the basin and the pools are gone, all of them, and the wells are deeper than the city's wells and we can't, in the year we've got, train a channeler we don't have the pool to train."

"Twenty-two villages."

"Twenty-two."

"And you've been cranking through wood linkages."

"We have."

Haran looked at the drawing. He looked at it for a long time. Ash, who had sat on the first crate the whole conversation and had not spoken, watched Haran's face and saw what he had not seen on Haran's face since the morning Ash had brought him the first survey of Stonecurve and Haran had looked at the map and said, *all right, let's begin.*

"Show me the trough lines," Haran said.

Beddan reached into the satchel. He took out a second sheet, and a third, and a fourth, and the crate between them was no longer big enough for the drawings. He set them on the floor. He set them on the bench. He moved Ash's tea cup and put a fifth drawing under it.

The lamp burned low and Haran did not trim the wick. Beddan ate, at some point, a piece of bread Ash put in his hand. The drawings covered the bench by the ninth hour and the floor by the tenth, and by the eleventh hour Haran had a piece of charcoal and was writing on the margins of three of them, and Beddan was nodding the slow nod of a man who had walked half a day to be given the thing he had walked for and was, in receiving it, not surprised.

At the eleventh and a half hour Haran sat back. He took his glasses off. He set them on the bench.

"You'll stay," Haran said.

"A week."

"Two."

"I haven't got two."

"You've got two if you mean it."

"I've got two if you mean it."

"I mean it."

"I'll send word."

Beddan rolled the drawings up. He tied them with a length of cord from his satchel. He set the bundle on the bench. He drank the last of the cold tea.

"Mister Ash," he said.

"Ash."

"Ash. The work in this district. We've heard of it down to the basin. The piece was in the broadsheet that comes on the cart."

"You read it."

"I read it. The framing was, thee'll forgive me, not for me. The work was."

"Thank you."

"I came for the work. Not the framing."

He stood. He shouldered the satchel. He nodded once at Haran, and once at Ash, and he went out into the night to the lodging Haran had, without asking, told him to take above the bakery on the upper square.

The door closed.

Haran sat at the bench. He did not pick up the file. He looked at the bundle of drawings tied with the cord. He looked at it the way he looked at a thing he had been waiting for and had not, until the thing arrived, known he had been waiting for it.

"Twenty-two villages," Haran said.

"Twenty-two."

"Their pools went six years before ours."

"Six."

"We've been late, Ash."

"We've been late."

He picked up the file. He did not, for some minutes, move it.

* * *

Two letters came on the same day.

Dorenne's runner brought them in a leather sleeve, the Wellspring's seal in dry wax on the flap. She had not, Ash noticed, written a note. She had forwarded the letters with no comment, the way she forwarded the things she wanted Ash to read without putting her own framing on them, which was, in itself, a framing.

He opened the first at the bench. Haran was at the file. The lamp burned. The brass shaving curled.

The first letter was on cheap paper. Ink that had bled at the descenders. The hand was young, urgent, controlled, the hand of a person who was learning to write the way a movement wrote and had not, yet, fully unlearned the way a student wrote.

> *To the man called the Wellspring,*
>
> *We are writing to ask a question we do not believe you can answer with the work you are doing.*
>
> *The Wellspring builds toys for the poor while the powerful keep their channeled comfort. The pumps in Stonecurve are admirable. The walls in Block Three hold heat. We have visited. We have looked. The work is real.*
>
> *The work is not enough.*
>
> *You hold the largest reservoir alive in this generation. The elder councillors hold pools they will not share. The young channelers, our generation, hold pools that thin every year because the world holds less of what we are made of. Meanwhile you build hand-cranks for the people whose pools have already gone, and the powerful continue to channel their comfort, and nothing of what they hold is asked of them.*
>
> *When does redistribution start?*
>
> *We will ask you this in private as long as we can. We will ask you this in public when we must.*
>
> *—The Hands of the Common Pool*

Ash read it twice. He folded it. He set it on the bench.

He opened the second.

The second was on heavy paper. Ink that had not bled. The hand was older. The signature was a councillor's signature, with the small flourish on the second letter of the family name that the elder generation used and the younger did not.

> *Mister Torren,*
>
> *I have read with interest the piece in The Morning Plate concerning your district workshop. I write in my private capacity, not in council session, though I will not pretend the two are unrelated.*
>
> *Mechanical alternatives are a retreat from progress. The channeled infrastructure of this city has held three centuries. It has thinned, certainly. It will thin further if we do not invest in it. Your work in Stonecurve has demonstrated that residents of less-favored districts can be provided for through means that do not require channeling. It has also demonstrated that they can be asked to crank a handle for water their grandparents had at a tap.*
>
> *I do not believe the conclusion that follows is the one you are drawing.*
>
> *I would welcome a conversation, at your convenience, regarding the strengthening, rather than the substitution, of our channeled systems. The Wellspring's reservoir, deployed strategically, could extend the working life of the upper aqueducts by a generation. This would benefit, I should add, the residents of Stonecurve no less than the residents of the upper terraces, in the long view.*
>
> *Yours in service to the city,*
>
> *Councillor Maerith Vahn*

Ash read it once. He folded it. He set it on the bench next to the first.

The two letters lay side by side. Cheap paper. Heavy paper. A young hand. An old hand. *When does redistribution start.* *A retreat from progress.* The reservoir, in the first, asked to be redistributed. The reservoir, in the second, asked to be deployed. The reservoir behind his ribs sat where it sat.

"Mm," Haran said. He had not, that Ash had seen, read either letter.

"What."

"Two of them."

"Two."

"They came together?"

"They came together."

"Mm."

He did not say more. He filed.

Ash sat with the two letters in front of him. He thought, for a minute, about the speaker at the square he had heard about from Dael, the gathering on the lower terrace that had grown each Sixth-day for a month. He thought about Korvin Delth, who had gone back to the middle districts with a girl he meant to send for training. He thought about Beddan Haye, who was, that morning, three days into his two weeks at Haran's bench. He thought about the reservoir.

He folded both letters. He put them in the drawer with the wage book and the broadsheet. The drawer, when he closed it, did not close all the way. There was a piece of paper at the back catching the runner. He did not take the paper out. He pushed the drawer until it closed enough.

It was enough, he told himself. Enough for the day.

* * *

He found the gathering on his way back from the upper market on a Sixth-day at the fifth hour.

He had not been looking for it. He had been carrying a sack of brass fittings, the small kind, the ones for the priming valves that had needed replacing on three of the pumps after the cold snap. He came down the lower stair and across the small square they called Two-Step, because of the double step at the southern entry, and the square was full.

Thirty. Forty. Mostly young. A mix of channelers and not, Ash could read it in the way they stood, the channelers with the small ease in the shoulders, the not-channelers with the held-in stance of a person who had been told all their life that a square meeting was not for them and had decided, this season, that it was.

In the center, on a low cart that had been pulled into the square as a platform, a man in his late twenties was talking.

He was good.

That was the first thing. He was good. He had the precision of a man who had built his argument in private and had walked it across, point by point, until the joints fitted. His voice did not strain. He spoke the way an engineer would speak if an engineer had been raised in a hall and trained to address rooms.

"Three centuries of channeling," he said. "Three centuries of channeling have built every aqueduct in this city. Every wall. Every stove. The infrastructure of Solathis is a stored asset. A stored asset paid for in pool by every channeler who ever worked it. That asset is held in common. That is the first principle. Do not let anyone tell you otherwise."

He paused. The crowd held.

"The asset is held in common. The use of the asset is not. The upper terraces draw the asset. The lower districts pay into it and do not draw it. The young channelers, our generation, top up an asset whose returns flow to the elder generation, who hold their personal pools in private and refuse to deploy them on the city's behalf."

He let that settle.

"This is the second principle. The asset is held in common. The flow of the asset is private. There is a word for this, friends, and the word is theft."

The crowd moved. Not loudly. The way a crowd moves when it has been waiting for a word and has been given the word, and can now hold the word in its mouth.

Ash, at the back, set the sack of brass fittings down between his feet.

"They will tell you," the speaker said, "that the asset is thinning. That we must conserve. That we must build alternatives. They will point you to a district where a man with the largest reservoir alive has built hand-cranks for the people whose pools have gone, and they will say, *look, the alternatives are coming, be patient.*"

He paused again.

He saw Ash.

Ash saw him see Ash. The recognition was the recognition a young man's face made when the face he had been describing in the abstract, the face he had perhaps been preparing for in private, had walked into the square on his own legs.

The speaker did not falter. The speaker was good. The speaker pivoted the way a good speaker pivoted, as if he had built the pivot into the argument all along, as if Ash's arrival had been a beat the rhythm had been waiting for.

"And there he is," the speaker said.

The crowd turned.

The crowd turned the way a crowd turned in a square when a name had been spoken and the bearer of the name was standing at the back with a sack of brass fittings between his boots. Faces. Forty faces. Some hostile, the kind of hostility that had been waiting all afternoon for somewhere to put itself. Some hopeful, the worse kind, the kind that had been hoping all afternoon that the most powerful person alive would turn out to be on their side.

"The man with an ocean," the speaker said, "asking the thirsty to wait."

The square was very quiet.

Ash stood at the back. The sack at his feet. His hands in his coat pockets. He did not, for a moment, do anything. He looked at the speaker. He looked at the faces. He saw a young woman in the second row whose mouth was open the smallest amount, the way a mouth opens when a person has held a hope a long time and is about to either speak or close. He saw a young man at the cart's side who was waiting, with the discipline of a man trained in waiting, for whatever was going to happen next.

The speaker waited.

The crowd waited.

He could speak. He could climb the cart. He could do, in this square, the thing the most powerful person alive could do in any square in this city, which was to take the air in his lungs and use it.

He did not.

He bent. He picked up the sack of brass fittings. He hooked it over his shoulder. He turned.

He walked away.

He walked across the southern entry of Two-Step, down the double step that gave the square its name, into the lane that led back toward Stonecurve. He did not run. He did not hurry. He walked the way a man walked when he had decided that walking was the answer he was prepared to give, in this hour, to a question he had not yet built an answer for.

Behind him a voice rose in the square. He did not know whose. He did not turn. He did not, as he walked, hear the words clearly. He heard the rhythm. He heard the rhythm of a speaker picking back up where he had been interrupted, and he heard the rhythm of a crowd that had been given a thing it could carry home.

The lane bent. The square went out of his hearing.

He walked a hundred paces. He stopped. He set the sack down. He stood with his hands on his knees and breathed.

*The man with an ocean. Asking the thirsty to wait.*

He had felt the line in his chest the way you felt a line when the line was true.

He did not, in the lane, decide what to do with the truth of it. He set the sack of brass fittings back on his shoulder and walked the rest of the way to Stonecurve, the line in his chest the whole walk, and the walls of the lower terrace passing him on his left and his right, and the city he had not, ever, asked the thirsty to wait building itself up around him as he went.

* * *

The shift change at Stonecurve was at the seventh hour.

He came into the square as the last light was leaving the upper roofs. The basin tap ran. The lamps in the workshop window were lit. Vell had gone home an hour before. The pump cover was tied at the corners.

At the far end of the square, by the smaller pump that fed the second basin, a woman was standing with a girl perhaps fourteen years old. The woman was named Bess Gallen. She had been one of the early skeptics, and she had been one of the early skeptics loudly. She had said, in the second week of training, that the workshop's hand-cranks were a way of telling poor women that the city had decided their wrists were the city's wrists now. She had said it to Ash. She had said it to Haran. She had said it to her own mother, who had been one of the first to put her name on the rotation.

She had come around in the fourth week. Quietly. She had put her own name on a rotation slip and had not, after that, said the wrist sentence again.

Ash stopped in the doorway of the workshop. He did not go in. The lamp was lit and Haran was at the bench and Beddan was at the bench beside him with the drawings in front of them, and Ash, in the door, was not, for a moment, ready to be in a room with people in it.

He stood and watched the pump.

Bess had her hand on the crank. The girl, the recruit, stood at her elbow.

"You'll feel it," Bess said. "The first stroke. You'll feel the thing fight you. That's the priming pulling. Don't muscle it. The crank knows what it's for. Let the crank do its work."

The girl nodded. She was thin. Her hair was tied with a length of brown cord.

"What's your name, again," Bess said.

"Lin."

"Lin. All right. Hand on the crank. Other hand on the brace. Set your shoulder. Don't lock the elbow. The elbow's a hinge, it's not a brace. The brace is the brace."

Lin set her hand. Set her shoulder. Did not lock the elbow.

"Now," Bess said. "Three taps."

"Three taps."

"You'll find a rhythm. Three pulls and a pause. The pause is for looking. Don't skip the pause. The pause is when you find the buckle before it's a buckle."

Ash, in the doorway, went still.

He did not move for the count of four.

The pause is for looking. Don't skip the pause. The pause is when you find the buckle before it's a buckle.

It was Tesha's line. It was Tesha's line word for word, the line Tesha said at the bench when she taught the lap, the line Ash had heard her say to three workers on the morning Maren had stood in this same doorway and watched. Bess had not been at that demonstration. Bess had been on the eastern bucket line. Bess had heard the line, somewhere, from somebody, and had taken it into her own hands and was, this evening, giving it to a girl named Lin at the second basin pump.

Bess was not lapping. She was cranking.

The line did not, on the face of it, fit.

The line fit anyway.

Bess said it the way a person said a thing they had heard said at the bench and had decided was the truth of how a body learned a working motion. The pause is for looking. The pause is when you find the buckle. The line was not Bess's. The line was not, Ash thought, Tesha's either, originally. The line had come out of the workshop, out of the way Haran taught a hand to know what a hand was doing, and Tesha had given it to Bess in a passing minute, and Bess had given it to Lin tonight, and Lin, in some week or month to come, would give it to a girl whose name nobody had said yet.

Lin pulled the crank. She felt it fight. She did not muscle it. She let the crank do its work.

"Good," Bess said. "Good. Three. Pause."

Lin paused.

"Look at the linkage. Look at the cylinder. Look at the priming valve. What do you see."

"Nothing wrong."

"What do you see."

"Linkage's tight. Cylinder's catching. Priming's set."

"That's what you see. Good. Now three more."

Three more.

Pause.

Three more.

The crank's pulse was steady through the flagstones. Lin's shoulder set into the rhythm at the eighth stroke, at the rhythm Vell had set into at the eighth, at the rhythm Vell's cousin had set into at the eighth, at the rhythm a wrist found when the wrist had been told what the wrist was for.

Bess stood beside Lin with her own hands at her sides. She was patient. She was competent. She was, as Ash watched, proud, the small unmistakable lift of the chin that a person made when a thing they had taught a younger person had landed in the younger person's body.

Ash watched.

Pride was a thing he had built, then. Bess was the thing he had built. Lin was the thing Bess was building, and Bess was building Lin out of the pieces Ash had built Bess out of, the same pause, the same rhythm, the same line about the buckle.

He stood in the doorway.

He thought *this is it. This is the thing. People who know things teaching other people who don't know them yet.*

He thought it the way a man thought a thing he had been working toward for months and had, that evening, seen with his own eyes.

He did not think the other thing.

He did not, in the doorway, see what the scene did not show. He did not hear that Bess's three sentences had been Tesha's three sentences, and that Tesha's three sentences had been Haran's, and that Haran's, in the long line of where lines came from, had also been Ash's, in the way that Ash's framework had become the framework that taught Tesha to teach, that taught Bess to teach, that was, this evening, teaching Lin. He did not see that the rhythm in Lin's shoulder was a rhythm whose origin was in another room, on another bench, in another wrist than Lin's. He did not, in the doorway, ask whether the line about the pause and the buckle was a line that Bess could have found, alone, by working a pump for six weeks, or whether Bess had found it because somebody had given it to her.

He saw the teaching.

He did not see the lineage.

The reader, in the square in the doorway, saw both.

The reader saw Bess's chin lift the way Tesha's chin had lifted that morning. The reader heard *the pause is when you find the buckle* in the voice it had been said in first, and in the voice it had been said in second, and in the voice it had been said in tonight, and the reader, because the reader was outside Ash's body and not inside it, heard the three voices as one voice. The reader saw Lin take the rhythm from Bess and not from her own wrist, and the reader saw the chain run back from Lin's wrist to Bess's mouth to Tesha's bench to Haran's hand to Ash's framework, and the reader, who had watched Ash build the framework in the months that had brought the city to this square, knew whose voice the chain ended in.

Ash did not hear it.

He stood in the doorway with his hand on the jamb and watched Bess teach Lin the pause, and his face had on it the small open look of a man who had, for an evening, set down the thing in his chest about the speaker and the ocean and the thirsty, the line he had carried home in the lane, and had taken up instead the line about the pause and the buckle, and the line about the pause and the buckle was, in the doorway, the answer.

It was an answer.

It was not the only answer.

The lamp in the workshop burned behind him. Haran's file, beyond the lamp, moved across the brass in even strokes. Beddan, beside Haran, drew a margin note on a fifth drawing in a hand that was not the city's hand. The basin tap ran. Vell's pump was under its cover. Granny was full. Block Three's flue drew clean.

In the square, Bess said, "Three more."

Lin said, "Three more."

The crank turned.

Ash watched.

He did not, that evening, in the doorway, name what he did not see. He named what he did see. He named it inside himself, quiet, the way a man named a thing he was not yet ready to say out loud.

*People,* he thought. *Not pipes. People.*

He went into the workshop.

The door closed behind him.

In the square, Bess and Lin worked the pump. The pause came when the pause was due. The pause was for looking. The crank turned. The basin filled. The light left the upper roofs of Stonecurve a quarter-inch at a time, and the lamps in the lower windows came on, one and then another and then another, and the district held what it had been built to hold.

Behind the closed door of the workshop, the file moved. The lamp burned. The drawings on the bench were the drawings of twenty-two villages in a basin three days' travel away, where the pools had gone six years before they had gone in the city, and where, by the end of the second week, Haran would have given Beddan a design that would, Ash hoped, do for those villages what the workshop's design had done for Stonecurve.

In the lane on the other side of the lower terraces, in a square called Two-Step, a speaker was finishing the argument he had begun before Ash had come and stayed after Ash had gone. The crowd was carrying the line home. *The man with an ocean. Asking the thirsty to wait.*

In a kitchen on the third terrace, his mother was setting a pot on the back of a stove that worked. In a room above an office in the upper district, his sister was warming a cup of tea, and the half-beat between her intention and the cup's response was a quarter-beat longer than it had been the week before, and she did not name it, and nobody named it for her.

In the square, Bess said, "Good. Lin. That's enough for the first night. Cover the crank."

Lin covered the crank. She tied the cloth at the corners the way Vell had tied it. The knot was not, the first time, the right knot. Bess showed her the right knot. Lin tied it again. The knot held.

Ash, inside the workshop, did not see the knot.

The reader did.
