# Chapter 6

The third message from his mother came folded into the apron of a woman delivering bread.

The woman was not a courier. She was a baker from two streets over who had grown up in the same building as Leska Torren, and who, by the indirect logistics of the lower city, had ended up with a folded square of paper inside her apron pocket on the morning she walked her usual round through Stonecurve. She stopped at the workshop door because the workshop had once been a bakery and the smell of new mortar had not yet driven out the older smell entirely. She stood in the doorway and looked at Ash with the polite suspicion of a woman who had heard things about a person and was reserving judgment.

"You're Leska's boy."

"Yes."

"She said you'd be the one with the bench."

She held out the folded paper. The crease was sharp. His mother folded paper the way she folded laundry, flat and definite, the corner exactly aligned. He took it.

"She said I should hand it to you, not leave it. So I'm handing it."

"Thank you."

"She said if you read it standing up I should tell her that. So I'm watching."

He sat down on the upturned crate by the door. The woman watched him sit. She did not smile, but something at the corner of her mouth moved.

"Tell her I sat."

"I will."

She left. He read the message standing up after she was gone, because his mother had outflanked him with a witness and not with the message itself, and the message had its own claim. The letter was four lines.

*Dinner Tuesday. The good chair is mended. Your father has a question about a valve. I have asked twice. This is the third asking.*

Underneath, in the cramped second hand she used when she ran out of room: *Maren says she will come if she can.*

He folded the paper along the same crease. He put it in his coat pocket. He went back to the bench, where Haran was scoring a length of brass tube against a steel rule, and he did not say anything for several minutes.

"Tuesday," he said at last.

Haran did not look up. "Tuesday what."

"I'm going home for dinner."

The score continued. Haran turned the tube a quarter and ran the rule down the next axis.

"Good," he said.

That was all.

* * *

He worked through Monday without sleeping much, because the work was the easier of the two paths in front of him and he had always taken the easier path when both were open. Tesha lapped two more flues. Dael set the new feeder pipe into the upper cistern at Block Three. Vell had a cold and worked her morning shift through it, her face set against the chill in a way that suggested she had decided in advance to ignore weather. Ash channeled four wall seams in the second row of buildings and watched the reservoir tick down a fraction of a fraction. The boundary sat where it had sat. He did not reach for it.

By Tuesday afternoon he had run out of postponements. He washed at the basin in the workshop yard. He combed his hair with his fingers because the one comb he owned was somewhere at Stonecurve and the other was somewhere in his old room and he had not, in three months, bought a new one. He wore the same coat he had worn for eight days. Haran looked at him from the bench with the expression of a man considering whether to say a thing, and decided not to.

"I'll be back tomorrow," Ash said.

"Take your time."

"I won't."

"I know."

Haran picked up his file. The valve seat under it brightened.

* * *

The new apartment was on the third floor of a building two streets up from where the old one had been, in a part of the lower city that had cracked less in the earthquake and held its plumbing better. Dorenne had arranged it in the week after the collapse, with the same efficiency she arranged everything: a list, a signature, a key in his mother's hand. Ash had been at Stonecurve when his parents moved. He had carried no boxes. He had not chosen a wall.

He climbed the stairs slowly. The landing on the second floor smelled of someone else's cooking, lamb and a spice he did not recognize. The third-floor landing smelled of his mother's stew, the one she made with the dark broth that took two days, and his hand was on the door before he had decided to knock.

The door opened before he knocked. His mother had heard his step.

"You're early."

"I'm on time."

"You're early by the building's clock. The hallway clock is fast."

She did not move out of the doorway. She looked at him for the long fraction of a second she always took before she let him in, the inventory she had been doing since he was four years old, the one that catalogued whether he had eaten and slept and washed and whether the answer to any of those was a lie.

"You haven't been sleeping."

"I've been sleeping."

"At the workshop."

"At the workshop is sleeping."

"It is sleeping the way standing is sitting." She stepped back and let him through.

The apartment was small and clean and almost familiar. The almost was the work. She had brought the cracked bowl from the old kitchen and set it on a shelf above the new stove, where it could not hold anything but where the eye could find it. The dented kettle sat on the new burner, the dent on the side away from the door, because his mother did not display damage but she did not throw it away either. The singed curtain hung in the bedroom doorway, the scorch mark folded into the gather so that you saw it only if you knew to look. He looked. It was there.

He stood in the front room and turned a slow circle and his mother watched him do it.

"You moved the table."

"I didn't."

"It looks moved."

"The room is a different shape. The table is in the same place relative to the door."

She said it the way she said most things, by establishing the geometry first and the feeling afterward. His father came out of the kitchen with his hands wet and a dish towel over one shoulder. He had aged less than his mother had, or aged differently. His hair was greyer at the temples and the same everywhere else. He smiled the smile he kept for his son, which was the same smile he kept for a finished joint or a balanced load, the satisfaction of a thing that had come together.

"You came."

"I came."

"Your mother said you would and then said you wouldn't and then said you would. I was ready for any of them."

"I told her I'd come."

"She tested it twice anyway. You know how she is."

His mother, behind him, made the small sound she made when she was being talked about in her own kitchen. She did not deny it. She put a hand on Ash's shoulder, briefly, the way she did, the touch that confirmed he was actually there and was not a letter.

"Sit."

"Where."

"The good chair. I mended it."

The good chair was the one with the carved back his great-grandfather had made, the one that had cracked across the seat in the earthquake. She had mended it with two iron straps and a new dowel and a coat of the wax she made herself from beekeepers in the hill country. The wood had darkened slightly where the wax had taken. The crack was visible if you knew to look. He sat. The chair held.

"It holds," he said.

"It holds."

His father sat across from him and set his elbows on the table the way he always had, the elbows at the angle of a man who had, all his life, leaned forward to listen.

"Tell me about the pipe joints."

"Father."

"Not yet. I know. Your mother first. But put it in your pocket. I have a question and I want to ask it before I forget."

"You won't forget."

"I might. I'm fifty-four."

"You won't forget."

"Put it in your pocket."

He put it in his pocket. His mother brought the bread.

* * *

She asked about the project the way she had asked about his lessons when he was nine, the way she had asked about the magicless certificate when he was nineteen, the way she had asked about the Wellspring after the first reservoir reading. She asked with a precision that lived in the small joints of the questions, in the order she put them. She did not ask *how is it going.* She asked, first, *how many people work there now,* and then, *who feeds them at midday,* and then, *who are you trusting with the keys.* She built the picture in his answers the way a mason built a wall. Each course set before the next went on.

"Forty-two," he said. "On the books. About thirty most days. Some come and go."

"Why do they go."

"Other work. Family. One man's wife had a baby. He'll be back in a month."

"Who feeds them."

"Petra runs a kitchen out of the old guildhall on the square's south side. Soup at midday. Bread."

"Petra is the woman with the supply contracts."

"Yes."

"You've said her name three times in three months. I've placed her now."

She had. He could see her placing her, the way she placed everyone, the cumulative file she kept of his life in her head. She did not write things down. She did not need to.

"Who has the keys."

"Haran. Felden, when he comes. Tesha at the workshop. Dael for the cistern lock. Me."

"Vell."

"Vell doesn't need a key. Her pump is on the square."

"You've talked about Vell more than the others."

He stopped. He thought about it. He had not noticed.

"She's good at it," he said. "It's a thing to talk about."

His mother nodded once. She did not press. She tucked the observation into wherever she tucked things, and went on.

"And what is it you actually do all day."

"Plan. Channel the seams. Train. Walk through the buildings."

"In what order."

"Different every day."

"In what order most days."

He thought about it. He realized, in thinking about it, that there was an order, and that he had been keeping it without naming it.

"Plan first. With Haran. Before light. Then the seams, while my hands are steady. Then training, midday. Then walking, late. Then planning again, after. With Felden if he's there. Without him if he isn't."

"That's a long day."

"It's a full day."

"It's a long day."

She let that sit.

"I could help."

He looked up.

"Help."

"With the work. Some part of it. I am not a channeler and I am not a builder. But you are running a kitchen and a roster and a training program through three other people, and I have been running a household with no errors for thirty years. I can keep a roster."

She said it the way she said everything: flat, complete, the offer placed on the table for inspection. He answered without thinking.

"Yes."

He heard himself say it. He saw her see him say it without thinking. Her expression did not change, but it became a fraction more attentive, the way a face does when a person is recording something.

"You said yes very fast."

"It's a good idea."

"You said yes before you knew what I'd do."

"I know what you'd do."

"Then you should have asked me what I'd do, so I could correct you."

His father, across the table, was smiling at his bread. He did not look up. He had been married to her for thirty-two years and he knew the shape of these conversations the way he knew the shape of a saw stroke.

"All right," Ash said. "What would you do."

"Roster first. Who's in, who's out, who said they'd come and didn't. Then supply. Petra has too much in her head and not enough on paper. Then the training schedule. Tesha's running it from memory."

"How do you know that."

"You said her name and your shoulders dropped a quarter inch. I asked. You answered."

"I didn't notice."

"You don't, with her. She matters to you."

He did not have a clean answer for that. His mother let him not have one. She poured the broth.

"Tuesdays and Thursdays," she said. "Mornings only. I will not move into your workshop."

"All right."

"And I will not call any of them *children.* I know what you are afraid of."

"I wasn't going to ask."

"You were going to ask later."

She was right. He took the broth.

* * *

His father waited until the bread was halfway gone before he asked his question, and when he asked it he asked it the way he asked all his questions, by setting the analogy down on the table first and then walking around it.

"You know how a man builds a fence."

"Father."

"Bear with me. You know how a man builds a fence."

"Posts first."

"Posts first. Deep enough to hold against the frost. Then the rails. Then the boards. The boards take the weather. The rails take the boards. The posts take everything. If you set a post too shallow, the fence stands for two winters and goes over in the third. You can nail the boards as tight as you want. The post is the fence."

"All right."

"Pipe joints are posts."

"They are."

"I have been thinking about your pipe joints. The ones you have been describing in your letters."

"I haven't described them."

"You've described enough. You said *the joint at the cistern collar held under thermal cycling.* That tells me three things. It tells me you are running heat through a joint that was originally cold. It tells me you are testing it across temperature swings. And it tells me you are worried about it, because you only describe things you are worried about."

"That's true."

"What are the joints made of."

"The collars are cast brass. The pipe is lead-tin solder over copper, drawn at the workshop. The seal at the cistern is a gasket of jute soaked in pitch."

His father set his bread down. He laid his palms flat on the table. He looked at the table the way he looked at a piece of wood he was about to plane, the gaze that read grain.

"Jute and pitch will work for a year. Maybe two. The pitch goes brittle in cold and it weeps in heat. You will have a gasket that is fine in the autumn and starts to leak in the second summer. By the third summer the jute is rotten. You will be replacing every gasket on a two-year cycle. Forty cisterns. Forty gaskets a year, after the second year. Are you setting up for that."

Ash sat with it. He had not thought of it. Haran had specified the gasket, and Haran did not specify things lightly, but Haran had been working in The Splits where the temperature swings were not the lower city's swings, and the lower city's roofs in summer baked the way the upper terraces did not.

"I don't know," he said.

"Hemp," his father said. "Soaked in tallow, not pitch. Older recipe. Holds longer in heat. Less brittle in cold. The hill country uses it in their well rings. I've patched a barn roof with it. It outlasts the wood it sits against."

"Where do you find it."

"I can find it."

"Father."

"I can find it. The tannery in the lower market still does tallow. The hemp comes up from the river towns in bales. I know a man in cordage who knows a man at the tannery. Give me a week."

"That's not your work."

"It's a question I've asked. I'll find the answer."

"Father."

"I'll find the answer, Ash. Then I'll bring it to you and you'll do with it what you do with it. If it's wrong I'll have learned something. If it's right you'll have a gasket."

He said it the way he had said *I'll patch the door* and *I'll plane the chair* and *I'll fix the kettle's handle* across thirty years of small repairs. He said it as a man who had no other way of saying *I am here.* Ash looked at his father's hands on the table. The knuckles were swollen at the joints in the way that came with weather and use. The skin on the back of the right hand had a long pale scar he could not place. He had not asked, and now was not the time.

"All right," Ash said.

"I'll bring you a sample."

"All right."

His mother, at the head of the table, was watching them both. She did not speak. She lifted her broth and drank, and the bowl in her hands was the cracked one, the one from the old apartment, mended with a fine line of brass leaf along the crack the way a thing mended in the river towns might be mended. He had not noticed her bring it to the table. She had served everyone else from the new bowls and had served herself from the cracked one. She caught him looking at it. She did not smile. But the corner of her mouth moved the way the baker's had at the door.

"It still holds water," she said.

"Yes."

"Better than before, in some lights."

"Yes."

She set the bowl down. The broth in it had not spilled.

* * *

Maren came at the second course.

The knock was light. She did not use the bell. She had not used the bell as a child, either, because the bell was for guests and Maren was not a guest in any house her family had ever lived in. His mother went to the door and opened it, and the conversation in the front room paused for the length of a breath.

"Sorry. The Hall ran late."

"You're here. Come in."

She came in. She set her satchel on the bench by the door and unwrapped her scarf and hung her coat on the peg his mother had put there for her. The peg had been there since the move. His mother did not assume Maren would come; his mother prepared for it anyway.

He stood when she came in. He did this without thinking, the way he had done it since he was twelve. She crossed the room and hugged him, and he hugged her back, and the hug was the hug it had always been, brief and tight, the small percussion of her hand on his back twice that meant *yes, I see you, sit down.*

She was thinner.

He did not name it. He did not, at first, even fully see it. He registered it the way a man registered a change in a friend's gait, a thing not yet a thing, a detail filed under nothing in particular. The bones at her wrist where the cuff had ridden up. The angle at her collarbone where the necklace she always wore sat a fraction lower than it had. Her face was not gaunt. Her face was the face of a person who had not had time to eat lunch for a stretch of weeks, and had told themselves that lunch was negotiable, and had won the argument enough times that the body had begun to adjust.

He sat down. She sat beside him. His mother brought her bowl, which was warm. Maren had not asked for it warm. His mother had warmed it.

"How long do you have," his mother said.

"Two hours. Maybe three. I told them not to find me."

"They will find you."

"I told them not to."

"They will find you."

Maren smiled. The smile was real and incomplete, the smile of a person who was tired and was glad to be in a room with people who saw her as a sister and not as an instrument. She drank the broth. She ate the bread. She listened to his father describe the gasket problem and asked one technical question (*pressure or temperature dominant?*) and his father answered (*temperature, but pressure cycles compound it*) and she nodded and went back to the broth.

"I want to see it," she said to Ash.

"The district."

"Stonecurve. Whatever you call it. The water and the heat. Tomorrow if you'll have me."

"Tomorrow."

"I have the morning. Then I have to be back at the Hall by two. Can you do a tour in three hours."

"Yes."

"Then tomorrow."

She said it the way she said most things, in the shortest grammar that would carry the meaning. He had grown up beside that grammar. It had not always been hers. As a child she had talked the way children talked, in long inventive sentences with side roads and switchbacks. The grammar had narrowed when she had entered the Hall, the way handwriting narrowed under a deadline, and he had not, until tonight, looked at the narrowing as a thing that had cost her something. He looked at it now. He looked away.

"Eat more bread," his mother said.

"I'm eating."

"Eat more."

"I'm eating, Mama."

"Eat more anyway."

Maren ate more bread. His mother, satisfied, refilled the broth without asking. The broth in Maren's bowl was steaming faintly. Ash watched his sister cup her hands around the bowl the way she had cupped her hands around bowls since she was eight, the small habit of a person who took warmth where it was offered. She did not channel. She did not need to. The bowl was warm and the broth was warm and the room was warm and her hands took it.

He filed nothing about it. He did not know yet there was anything to file.

* * *

After dinner, his mother set the kettle on. The dented one, from the old kitchen.

"You bought a new one," he said.

"I did."

"It's on the shelf."

"It is."

"You're using the old one."

"It boils faster."

"It doesn't."

"It boils the same. I know where the dent is. It pours without splashing if you hold it the way I hold it."

"That's not a reason."

"It's a reason."

She poured. She did not splash. The tea was the dark tea she made from the leaves the woman down the road brought up from the river towns, and she poured it into four cups, and she set the kettle back on the burner with the dent away from the door.

His father took his cup. His father always took his cup first because his mother always handed it to him first; this had been true since before Ash had been alive and would be true after. He drank. He put the cup down. He stretched his hands above the table the way a man stretches before a long sit.

"I'll go look at the cordage tomorrow," he said. "I'll find your hemp."

"You don't have to do it tomorrow."

"I'll do it tomorrow. The Wellspring waits for no man."

"Father."

"It's a joke."

"It's not a joke."

"It's a joke, Ash. Your mother thinks it's a joke."

His mother, at the burner, did not commit. "I think it's nearly a joke."

"Nearly."

"Nearly is enough."

His father grinned at his tea. Maren, beside Ash, was holding her own cup. She had drunk half of it. The other half had cooled, the way tea cooled in a small apartment with a faint draft from the window. She wrapped her hand around the cup. She closed her eyes for a fraction of a second. She opened them.

The tea steamed again.

He saw it as a man saw a thing his eye had been trained to see. The warming was faint, the kind of channeled adjustment any decent pool could perform without thought, the casual maintenance a person did with their tea a hundred times in a winter. He had watched her do it since they were children. The tea always warmed at the small drop of her shoulder, the half-second when the pool reached and the warmth rose in answer.

It rose now. But it rose a half-beat after the reach.

He saw the reach. He did not, at first, see the half-beat as separate. He saw, only, that the warming had happened, and then his mind, returning to the gesture an instant later because his eye had snagged on something it had not yet named, replayed it. The reach. Then the small interval. Then the warmth. The interval was nothing. A second. Less. The width of a breath he had not noticed her take. But it was there, and her tea had warmed every other time he had ever watched her do it without an interval.

She drank. Her face did not change. She set the cup down and said something to his father about the cordage market and where the river-town bales came from, and his father answered, and the conversation moved, and the moment closed over itself the way water closed over a stone.

He did not say anything.

He sat with his own cup. He thought, briefly, *she's tired,* and the thought was a coat he put on without examining the lining. She was working too hard. She had said so. The Hall ran late. She had told them not to find her and she expected them to find her anyway. A tired channeler made a tired pool. A tired pool reached a half-beat slow. He had reached his own pool a half-beat slow on the days when he had not slept, and the lag had been nothing more than a lag. It would correct with rest. She would rest.

He did not look closer.

He did not, at the time, know there was a closer to look at.

* * *

He walked her home, partway, because she had said she could walk it alone and his mother had said *Ash will walk you to the bridge,* and the matter had been settled by his mother and not by Maren, and Ash had been glad of it.

The street was cool. The lamps along the second cross were channeled lamps, the old kind, the upper-district style that the lower city had bought used and rehung when they wore out on the terraces. Two of them were dark. The lamp-maintenance schedule had shifted twice since the earthquake; nobody had quite settled on whose problem it was. Ash had stopped tracking.

Maren walked with her hands in her coat pockets.

"He's good," she said.

"Father?"

"Yes. He's good. He's better than he was last winter."

"He's keeping busy."

"He is. He needed it. He didn't take well to being unemployed."

"I know."

"Mama."

"What about her."

"She's fine. She's the same. She'll always be the same. She was the same when I was four and she'll be the same when she's eighty."

"I asked her to help."

"I know. I heard. I'm glad."

"You heard?"

"I heard the kitchen go quiet for the half-minute before you said yes. She had won by then. Whatever the question was, she had won it. I just didn't know what."

"She wants a roster."

"She wants a chair at the table. The roster is the chair. Give her the chair."

He nodded. They walked.

"You're working too hard," he said.

She didn't break stride. She did not turn her head. She made the small sound that was almost a laugh and was not quite.

"That's a thing people say."

"It's a thing I'm saying."

"Noted."

"Maren."

"I am working. I am working hard. The amount is the amount the Hall requires. If the Hall required less I would do less. It does not."

"You could push back."

"On what."

"On the amount."

"Ash."

"I'm asking."

"I know you're asking. The answer is that the work is the work. Some of it is mine because I'm good at it. Some of it is mine because nobody else will do it. Some of it is mine because Dorenne put it on me and I haven't yet found a way to give it back. I'll find a way. I'm not going to make a noise about it before I've found one."

"All right."

"And I don't want you carrying it for me. You have a district."

"I have a district."

"Then carry the district. I'll carry the Hall. We'll meet for dinner."

She said it lightly. He heard the edges of the lightness, the small effort it took to keep it light. He did not push. They reached the bridge. The water under it was low, the way it ran in late spring, and the channeled lamp at the bridge's far end was lit, the upper-district kind, steady and clean.

She hugged him again. The hug was the same as the hug at the door, brief, two pats, definite.

"Tomorrow," she said.

"Tomorrow."

"Don't dress it up. I want to see it as it is."

"It doesn't dress up."

"Good."

She walked across the bridge. He stood and watched her until she had reached the lamp at the far end and turned the corner, and her shape went out of his eye, and he stood a little longer with his hands in his pockets and the river running low beneath him.

He told himself she was tired.

He walked home. He slept four hours. He woke before light and went to Stonecurve.

* * *

She came at nine.

She came on foot because the Hall did not provide a carriage for personal visits and Maren did not ask for one she had not earned. She walked into the square the way she walked into rooms: directly, scanning, the lieutenant's habit of cataloguing exits. She stopped at the basin.

"This is the tap."

"This is the tap."

"And the cistern is on the roof above."

"Above and behind. Two roofs back. Gravity feed runs along the inside wall."

"How long did the run take."

"Six days. Four to lay the line, one to test, one to redo a section that bled."

"Show me the bled section."

He showed her. She knelt at the joint, ran her thumb along the seam, frowned, ran it again. She did not channel. She looked.

"It's clean."

"Yes."

"You channeled this."

"I channeled this."

"How much did it cost you."

"Less than the Velden pump. More than the cistern."

She looked at him sideways. The line was Haran's; she heard it that way.

"You're quoting Haran."

"I'm quoting Haran."

"Is he answering you when you ask in numbers."

"No."

"Why."

"He says don't measure him against me. Says measure me against me."

She nodded once. She stood. She brushed her knees.

"That's a good answer."

"Is it."

"It's the answer you needed. I don't know if it's the answer you wanted."

She moved on.

* * *

She was thorough. She walked the cistern run from the basin up to the rooftop tank and down the eastern wall to the second feeder. She stood on the rooftop with her hand on the tank wall and her eyes unfocused for the half-second of the channeler's check. She came down the ladder backward, the way a builder did and not the way a Hall channeler did, and he saw, in the descent, the small grace of a body that had once climbed scaffolds with their father on summer afternoons.

She walked Block Three. She put her palm to three of the wall seams he had channeled and held it. She did not say anything for a long time after the third one.

"These are good."

"They're holding."

"They're better than good. The seam at the corner of the second room, where the inner wall turns. That's the hardest geometry. It's a clean fusion."

"Haran walked it after me. He said clean."

"Haran says clean when it's clean. He doesn't say it otherwise."

"I know."

She moved through the room. She put her hand on the warm wall beside the stove. She held it there.

"It's the stove that's warming this."

"Yes."

"And the wall is holding it because the seam is closed."

"Yes."

"And the fiber is in the cavity because the seam was opened, packed, and reclosed."

"Yes."

"Without a channeler in the room."

"Without a channeler in the room. Once it's built."

She looked at him. Her face did the small thing it did when she was impressed and was deciding whether to say so. She decided.

"This is real."

"It's real."

"It scales."

"It can."

"Show me the pump."

* * *

Vell was at the crank when they came down. She had finished the morning fill an hour earlier and was running a second, lighter cycle for the upper basin in Block Three. She did not stop when she saw them. She nodded at Ash. She looked at Maren. Her glance held for a half-second on Maren's coat, which was Hall-cut, and then went back to the crank.

"Vell, this is my sister."

"I know."

"How do you know."

"You said your sister was coming. She came. She has your eyes."

Maren laughed. The laugh was a real laugh. Vell did not smile, but her crank pace did not break, and the line of her shoulders did not tighten, and for Vell that was an open door.

"May I try it."

"Try it."

Vell stepped off the platform. She did not, as some workers had done with visiting Hall officers, demonstrate first. She gestured at the handle and stepped back and stood with her arms folded and watched.

Maren took the handle.

He had watched his sister do harder things than crank a pump. She had run thirty-second sustained channels in the Hall's testing room when she was sixteen. She had sealed an emergency main during the spring flood the year he was at the workshop with his head down and not looking up. She was strong; she had always been strong, the kind of strong that came from a pool that did its work without complaint and a body that had grown up doing what the pool asked.

She turned the crank.

The first ten strokes were clean. She had the rhythm faster than he'd expected, the elbows tight, the hips driving, the way Haran had shown Vell once and only once and Vell had passed to no one except by example. Maren had not been shown. She had read the motion off Vell in the half-minute they had been standing there.

By stroke fifteen he saw the breath change.

It was not labor. It was the wrong size of labor. The breath came at a depth that suggested her chest was working harder than the movement required. She was not pulling against resistance she did not have. She was pulling against something else. He watched her face. The face was the discipline-mask she had been wearing since she was twelve, the one she used in the Hall, the one she used when she had decided in advance that whatever the body did the face would not. The face held.

She pumped to twenty. She pumped to twenty-five. At thirty she stepped off the platform, smoothly, as if she had decided thirty was the figure, and brushed her hands against each other twice and looked at the basin.

"Good action," she said. "Easier than I'd thought at the top of the stroke. Harder at the bottom."

"It's the valve return," Ash said. "There's a stiffness we haven't tuned out."

"You'll tune it out."

"We will."

Vell stepped back onto the platform without being asked. She set her hands on the crank and resumed the cycle. Her pace was Vell's pace, the still-faced count, the metered breath. She had pumped the morning fill and was now in the middle of a lighter cycle and she had not, as far as Ash could see, exerted herself yet.

Maren watched her for a moment. Her face did a thing he saw and did not, in the moment, name. A flicker. An adjustment. The smallest possible movement in the corner of her mouth, the kind a face made when it was correcting itself away from an expression it had nearly let through. Then she turned to him.

"She's good."

"She's fast."

"She's faster than she should be at her weight. That's training."

"That's Haran. Once."

"Once is enough if the once is right."

She said it lightly. The lightness, again, with the small effort under it. He almost said something. He did not. He did not have a sentence for the thing he had not named. The not-naming was the path of least resistance and he took it because the work was waiting and his sister had three hours and the project had pipes.

* * *

She left at noon.

She hugged him at the square's edge, where the side-street ran up toward the bridge and the Hall. The hug was the same hug. Two pats. Brief. He felt, through her coat, how thin her shoulders had gotten. The shoulder blade under his hand was sharper than he remembered. The remembering was not precise; he was comparing now to a now from two months ago, and two-month-old observation was unreliable; but the unreliability did not change what his hand had felt.

"It's good work, Ash."

"Thank you."

"Scale it."

"I'm trying."

"Try harder. There's a window. It won't stay open."

"What window."

She looked at him. The look was the look she had given him when he was nine and had asked her if their father was angry. It said *I am not going to lie to you and I am also not going to tell you the whole shape of the thing.*

"The Hall is a building made of people," she said. "People retire. People get tired. People change their minds. The arrangement that lets you work in Stonecurve right now is not a permanent arrangement. It is a window. Build through it."

"I'm building."

"Build faster."

"I'm building as fast as I can."

"Then build smarter."

"Maren."

"I'm not criticizing. I'm telling you what I see from the Hall side. Dorenne has cover for you right now. The cover is conditional. The conditions are not stable. Build."

She hugged him again. The third hug was longer than the first two. It was the hug a person gave when they had not decided whether they would see the other person again soon, and were not going to admit to themselves that the question had crossed their mind.

"Eat lunch," he said.

"I will."

"You won't."

"I will today."

"Today."

"That's what you asked for."

She turned. She walked up the side-street toward the bridge, and her coat moved against her shoulder blades the way coats moved against people who had been working too hard for too long, and he stood at the edge of the square and watched her go until the lane bent her out of his sight.

He filed it under *Maren is working too hard.*

He filed it because the file had a place to go and the other file did not.

He went back to the workshop.

* * *

Haran was at the bench. He had a brass collar in a small vise and was tapping a thread into it with a tap-handle that had been his father's. He did not look up when Ash came in.

"Sister gone."

"Gone."

"Good visit."

"Good visit."

"She liked it."

"She liked it. She told me to scale it. She told me to scale it faster."

"She's right."

"She is."

Ash sat on the crate. He watched the tap turn. The chips of brass came up the flute in tiny bright spirals and dropped onto the bench. Haran reversed the tap a quarter, advanced it half, reversed a quarter. The thread cut clean.

"Father's bringing me a hemp gasket sample next week."

"What."

"Hemp soaked in tallow. He says the pitch-jute will fail in two years on the cistern collars. He says hemp-tallow will hold."

Haran's tap paused. He did not look up. He restarted, two quarter turns, no chips this time, the thread already there.

"Your father said this."

"Yes."

"At the table."

"At the table."

"Hm."

"He's bringing a sample."

"Bring it to me when he does."

"I will."

"He's right, by the way."

"About the gasket."

"About the gasket. I'd been sitting on it. The Splits doesn't see the summer the lower city sees. I'd written down *revisit gaskets in year two.* I hadn't done the revisit."

"He saved you a year."

"He saved me a year."

The tap finished its thread. Haran broke it free, blew the chips out of the collar, held it up to the light, and set it on the bench beside three other identical collars.

"Tell him thank you."

"I'll tell him."

"Tell him by name. Not *my father.* By name."

"Rendell."

"Tell Rendell thank you."

"I will."

Haran picked up the next collar.

* * *

Late in the afternoon, when the workers were breaking for the small bread Petra brought around at three, Ash sat on the wall by the basin with his coat unbuttoned and his elbows on his knees and watched Vell run through the third cycle of the day. The square's sun was slanted. The water in the basin held the slant, and a child he did not know walked up with a tin cup, dipped, drank, and walked off without looking at anyone, the way a child drank from a tap they had decided was theirs.

He thought about his mother's bowl with the brass leaf.

He thought about his father's hands flat on the table.

He thought about Maren on the bridge, walking away.

He thought, again, about the warmth rising in her cup a half-beat after the reach. He thought about it because his mind, having filed it, had begun, in the way minds did, to test the file. He turned the moment over. He looked at it. He could not, in the looking, fully decide whether the half-beat had been a half-beat or whether his eye had invented one. He had been watching. He had been looking for nothing. He had seen something. The looking had been honest. The seen had been small.

A tired channeler. A long week. A sister who had told them not to find her and had been found anyway.

He held it there. He did not open the file further. He did not, yet, have the courage to ask the question that opening it would require him to ask, and he knew this about himself, and he forgave himself for it the way a man forgave himself for things he intended to come back to later.

Vell finished the cycle. She locked the brake. She wiped her palms on her trousers.

"Wellspring."

"Vell."

"Your sister channels."

"Yes."

"Good pool?"

He did not answer for a moment. He looked at the basin. He looked at the slant of light on the water.

"She did. She does. I don't know."

Vell considered this. She did not press. She was fifteen, and she had spent enough of her fifteen years around adults who answered carefully to know that careful answers did not get walked back by a follow-up question.

"My pump's good."

"Your pump's good."

"I'll be here tomorrow."

"I'll be here tomorrow."

She nodded. She walked off across the square, her hands in her coat pockets, the late sun on her hair. The basin tap ran. The water came clean.

He sat on the wall a while longer.

The reservoir sat behind his ribs. He had not channeled today. He had walked the district with his sister and watched her hands and his hands had stayed at his sides and the pool had not moved. The boundary was where the boundary had been, almost. He could not tell, in the noise of the day, whether it was a hair closer or a hair farther. Probably the same. Probably forty, or just under. The math sat where it had sat.

He thought of his mother again. The kitchen going quiet for the half-minute before he had said yes to her offer of help. Maren had heard the silence from the door. His mother had won a thing in that silence. He did not, even now, know what she had won. She had seen something in him saying yes too fast. She had seen something in his shoulders dropping when Tesha came up. She had asked, three times, before he had come. She had sent the message by the baker, and the baker had watched him sit.

She had not asked him about Ryn until almost the end of the dinner, when his father had gone to the kitchen for more bread.

* * *

The asking had happened in the small lull. His father had stood, his back at the burner, his hands on the bread board. His mother had been pouring tea. Maren had been on the way to the door, unfastening her satchel for something she'd brought.

His mother, into the quiet between two pours, had said:

"Have you seen Ryn."

He had not, in the moment, answered immediately.

"No."

"In how long."

"A while."

"How long is a while."

"Six weeks."

"Seven."

"Seven, then."

She had finished the pour. She had set the kettle down. She had not said anything else. Her face had not done anything particular. Her face had done, instead, the thing it did when she had said the thing she had come to say and had decided to leave it on the table without flagging it, the way a woman left a coin on a counter and walked out and let the counter decide what to do with it.

*You're doing it again,* her face said. Without saying.

He had taken his tea.

"I'll write him," he had said.

"Write him."

"I will."

"Tonight."

"Tomorrow."

"Tonight."

He had nodded. He had not written that night. He had not written this morning. He had filed it under things he would do when the work allowed, and the work would not, in the way of work, allow.

He sat on the wall by the basin in the late slant of afternoon and looked at the file in his head and did not open that one either.

He stood. He went back to the workshop. The tap kept running.

Behind him, in the district he was building, water moved by gravity into a stone basin, and a child he did not know drank from it without paying anyone, and somewhere up the curve of the city his sister walked back into the Hall of Infrastructure with her shoulders set against a thing she had not told him about, and somewhere in his mother's kitchen the dented kettle sat on the burner with the dent away from the door, and his father was already drafting a list of cordage suppliers in his head, and Ryn was somewhere Ash had not been in seven weeks.

He went inside. Haran did not look up from the bench. The lamp was lit. The thread was clean.

He sat down. He picked up a file. He went to work.
