# Chapter 9

Davan came at the second hour past noon, and he came alone except for two.

Ash saw them from the workshop window. He had been at the bench since the morning, with the brass blank and the file and a cup of tea Haran had poured at the eleventh hour and which Ash had let go cold without noticing, and he was watching the square through the wide pane the way a man watched a thing he had decided not to flinch at. The square was the square. The basin tap ran. Vell was at the eastern pump with the cover off, with her sleeves rolled to the elbow and her cap on the edge of the trough, and she was teaching the morning's new boy the count.

The three of them came in past the bakery.

Davan was in the middle. He had not, on the cart at the Cooperage, looked tall in the way a tall man looked tall; he had looked, against the lamps, the height of a man who had been built to fit a room. In the square, in daylight, walking, he was Ash's height, perhaps a finger taller, with the working coat from the Cooperage buttoned to the throat and the same plain cap. He was the only one not carrying anything. The woman to his left had a leather satchel against her hip and a pencil tucked behind her ear, and she walked with the half-stride of someone who had been taking notes since the gate. The man to Davan's right had his hands in his coat pockets and his shoulders set the way a man set his shoulders when he had decided, before arriving, that he was not going to like what he saw.

Ash stood up from the bench.

"They're here," he said.

Haran did not look up. He had a small spring laid out on the bench in three pieces and the longest piece between the jaws of a vise, and he was working a fine file along the cut edge with the slow patient stroke of a man for whom the spring was, this minute, the only spring in the world.

"Mm," Haran said.

"You'll come out."

"I'll be in here."

"Haran."

"In here," Haran said, "is where the spring is."

The file moved.

Ash pulled the workshop door behind him as he stepped into the square. He had not put on a coat. The square was cold but not the bone-cold of yesterday's canal towpath, and he wanted, going to meet Davan, to come out of his own door without armor on. He registered this the way he registered most of his small choices in the year. He registered it and let it stand.

Davan saw him. Davan stopped at the basin and put one bare hand on the rim of the stone the way a person put a hand on a thing to settle the introduction it was about to have, and he turned, and he waited for Ash to come the rest of the way to him.

"Davan," Ash said.

"Ash."

There was a half-beat. Davan held it the way Davan held everything, without strain, the way a man held a tool he had picked up before. Then he raised his hand off the basin rim and held it out. Palm up. The old gesture. The gesture the city had used for two hundred years and still used between strangers who wanted to mean it, and the gesture that had, for Ash, in his first twenty-three years, always carried the small private humiliation of being a thing he could not return.

Ash put his palm against Davan's.

There was no pulse. No channeling, on either side. Just the press of skin, dry and cold from the air, and the small fact that Davan had offered the gesture knowing what Ash had been once and was now, and had not, by a flicker of the eyes, made the offering carry weight it did not need.

"This is Ressa," Davan said. His left hand opened toward the woman with the satchel. "She writes for us. And Korin." The man with his hands in his pockets nodded, once, without taking the hands out.

"Ressa. Korin." Ash gave them each the slight bow of the head the city used in lieu of touching strangers' palms more than once in a meeting. Ressa bowed back. Korin did not.

"Thank you for coming," Ash said.

Davan's mouth moved, not quite a smile. "You said come."

"I did."

"Then we came."

Ash registered this. The line was the kind of line Davan said the way Davan said most things, which was without flourish, which was the flourish.

"I thought we'd start at the basin," Ash said. "Then the pump. Then the chimney work in Block Three. Then back here, if you've got time. I'd like, if you'll let me, to ask you about a thing while we walk."

"Ask," Davan said. "We'll see."

* * *

He took them to the basin first.

Vell, at the eastern pump, had registered them at the gate. He saw her register. She did not stop the lesson with the new boy. She did not, on Davan's coming into the square, do the thing she had not done at the Cooperage either, which was look at Ash and ask what to do with her face. Her face did what her face did, which was the small steady set of a girl who had been in the yard yesterday at the third row and was, today, at her pump.

Ash put his hand on the basin tap.

"This is the run-off," he said. "It comes off the cistern through the west manifold. The pressure's set by the head, not by channeling. The valve's mechanical."

He turned the tap. The water came. The arc of it ran into the basin's drain the way it ran every hour of every day in the square now, and Davan watched it, and Ressa watched it, and Korin watched it with the same set shoulders he had brought in past the bakery.

"How much is the head?" Davan said.

"Four meters at the upper reservoir, two at the manifold. We're losing about three percent over the run."

"That's the loss to friction?"

"And one minor leak we haven't found. The crew's tracking it."

"Mm." Davan crouched. He went down the way a man with strong knees went down, in one motion, and he put his hand under the arc of water and let the water run across his palm. He did this for two seconds. Then he stood and dried his hand on the inside of his coat and looked up at the cistern roof above the square.

"Built when?" he said.

"The cistern's old. The exchange retrofit, the manifold work, the standpipe, the basin, all of that's the last six weeks."

"Channeled?"

"The exchange catalysis was me. The rest was crew work, hand-tooled. I can show you the records."

"Ressa will want them."

Ressa already had her pencil out. She made a mark in the small bound book and waited.

"Yes," Ash said. "I'll have them brought to the workshop while we walk."

Davan turned to Vell.

He turned the way a man turned to a person who had been at a meeting, not to a piece of equipment, and Vell saw him turn. She set the wrench in her hand on the rim of the trough, the way she set tools down when a thing more interesting than the tool was happening, and she straightened.

"You're the morning crew?" Davan said.

"Yes."

"How long have you been on this pump?"

"Six weeks. From the start."

"And the boy?"

"His third day."

"What does he know?"

"He knows the count. He knows the prime sequence. He doesn't know yet what to do when the prime doesn't take." She said this without looking at the boy beside her. The boy was, Ash registered, very young, perhaps thirteen, and his hands had gone still on the crank handle the moment Davan had turned to Vell, and he was listening the way a boy listened when he had been spoken about and was hearing whether the speaking would land hard.

"What do you do when the prime doesn't take?" Davan said. To the boy.

The boy looked at Vell. Vell did not look back at him. He looked at Davan and said, the way a thirteen-year-old said a thing he was three days into knowing, "I, you check the suction line for air. If there's air, you bleed the cap. If the cap's clean, you go up the line to the foot valve."

"And if the foot valve's stuck?"

"You ask Vell."

Davan smiled. It was the first real smile Ash had seen on his face. It was small and mostly in the eyes and it lasted half a beat and was gone, and Davan said, "That's the right answer."

The boy's hands started moving on the crank again.

Vell, with her face still doing what her face did, said to Davan, "You were at the yard."

Davan paused. He did not, Ash registered, look at Ash.

"I was," Davan said. "You?"

"Eight rows in."

"Mm." Davan nodded. He nodded the way a man nodded who had heard *I was there* and had decided, in the half-second after, not to make it a thing. "Thank you for the work on the pump."

Vell did not say *you're welcome.* She picked the wrench up off the trough rim and went back to the boy and the count.

Ash, two paces back, registered the small clean thing Davan had done, which was not to make Vell's being in the yard a question and not to make Ash's not knowing about it a question, and to thank the girl for the pump while leaving alone, in the same breath, every other thing the thanking might have said.

He thought *he's good at this.*

He thought *he was good at this on the cart.*

He thought *of course he is.*

* * *

The pump was the pump.

He showed them the cylinder. He showed them the linkage. He showed them, with the cover off, the bronze foot valve at the bottom of the well casing where it came up through the paving, and Davan went down again on his good knees and looked at it, and Korin, finally, took his hands out of his pockets and crouched beside him, and Korin asked the first question he had asked since the gate.

"What's the seat?"

"Leather. We replaced it from the bronze that came with the pump. Bronze on bronze took chatter."

"How long does the leather last?"

"We don't know yet. The estimate's eighteen months. The crew's tracking it."

"Who replaces it?"

It was a real question. Ash heard it as a real question. He heard it the way he had been hearing real questions in the workshop for nine months, which was with the small adjustment a man made when the question was the question he had been quietly asking himself.

"Right now," Ash said, "Vell."

"Without channeling."

"Without channeling. The replacement is mechanical. The fit's the part that takes practice."

"And if Vell isn't here."

"Reska. Or Jora. Or, in eight months, the boy."

Korin straightened. He put his hands back in his pockets. He looked at Ash. He did not look at him long. He looked at him the way a man looked who had come into the square decided, and was, against his own decision, registering a thing he had not expected to register, and was filing the registering away to be considered, later, against everything else he knew.

"Mm," Korin said.

Davan, still crouched, ran his thumb along the bronze rim of the valve seat. He did not look up.

"Show me Block Three," he said.

* * *

Block Three was warm.

Ash had registered, three weeks in, the small shift in how Block Three smelled in winter, which was the shift in how a building smelled when the people inside it had, for the first time in their lives, been able to leave their coats on the hooks by the door instead of on their shoulders at the kitchen table. The smell was the smell of cooked food and laundry and bodies that had been able, the night before, to sleep at the temperature their bodies wanted to sleep at. The smell was, in its way, the proof.

He took them up the southern stair.

He took them past the small lit lamp in the cellar door and up to the third landing, where Reska's cousin had a flat she had moved into ten days ago, and Reska's cousin was at the door before Ash knocked because the boy from the bakery had, Ash registered, already run up the stair to say *the Wellspring's coming up with three.*

She opened the door six inches.

"Tesha," Ash said. "Sorry. We're not coming in. I just wanted to show them the wall."

Tesha looked past him at Davan. Tesha had been at the Cooperage too. Ash had not seen her there. He registered now, in the half-second of her looking, the small adjustment in her face that said she had been there and was, at the door, doing the same thing Vell had done in the square, which was hold her face the way her face held when more than one of her loyalties was in the same room.

She did not, Ash registered, have to choose between them at her door. They were not, at her door, asking her to.

"The wall's the wall," Tesha said.

She opened the door the rest of the way. She did not invite them in. She stepped to one side so that Davan could see, from the landing, the inside corner of the kitchen wall, where the old stone met the channeled exchange and where Ash, four weeks ago, had laid his hand against the seam and fused the new conduit through the seam without touching the stone of the room itself.

"That seam," Ash said. "Old wall to new conduit. The conduit carries warm water from the Block Three exchange. The exchange runs off the cistern, which runs off the basin, which runs off the morning crew's labor. The whole thing is mechanical from the cistern down."

"And from the cistern up?" Davan said.

"That's me."

"How much of you?"

"Three tenths of a percent for the catalysis. One time."

"And the maintenance?"

"None, so far. I'll come back to that."

Davan did not, with his face, do anything visible. He looked at the seam. He looked at the wall. He looked at Tesha, and he said, "You're warm."

"I'm warm," Tesha said.

"The first winter?"

"In the flat? Yes. In my life? No."

It was, Ash registered, the first sentence Tesha had said in his presence that was not a sentence about pumps. Tesha did not say things about her life. She said things about the cousin and the crew and the bakery boy and the count, and she said them the way she said *the wall is the wall,* without heat. She had said *in my life? No,* the way a woman put a small flat coin on a table and walked away from it.

Davan looked at her. He looked at her for a beat. He did not, Ash registered, perform any of the things a man could have performed in the beat. He nodded, once, the way Vell nodded. He said, "Thank you for letting us see the wall."

"Mm," Tesha said. She closed the door.

Ressa's pencil moved.

* * *

They came back to the workshop at the fourth hour.

Haran was at the bench. The spring was not in three pieces anymore. The spring was in two pieces and was, Ash registered as he came in, half-set into a small brass housing he had not seen Haran cut. Haran did not look up when they came in. He turned the housing a quarter-turn against his palm the way he turned a thing he was checking the seat of, and he set it down, and he picked up the kettle, and he poured for four cups before anyone had asked for tea.

He did not pour for himself.

"Sit," Haran said. He said it to the room.

There were, in the workshop, two stools and the crate Ash sat on most mornings and the long bench that ran along the south wall. Korin took the bench. Ressa took a stool. Davan took the other stool. Ash took the crate. Haran went back to the housing.

"Thank you," Davan said. He picked up his cup.

"Mm," Haran said.

Korin, on the bench, with his back against the wall and his cup balanced on one knee, looked at Haran the way a man with set shoulders looked at a man whose shoulders had been, for forty years, the kind of shoulders that did not set. He did not say anything. Haran did not, in the looking, either.

Ash registered this. Haran was, in the way Haran had been in the square the night Ash had come back from the Cooperage, present in the room without occupying any of it. The teacup was on the bench by Davan's hand because Haran had put it there. The kettle was on the stove because Haran had put it there. The man was filing a brass housing.

Davan had not, Ash registered, missed any of it. Davan, with his cup against his lower lip, registered Haran the way Davan registered everything, with the precise attention of a man whose attention was what he had instead of theatrics.

"How long have you been here?" Davan said. To Haran.

"Forty-two years."

"This workshop?"

"Was my master's. He was at the bench when I was twelve. He was at the bench when I was twenty-three. He was at the bench when he wasn't anymore."

"Mm," Davan said.

"You'll find," Haran said, and the *you'll* was not, Ash registered, addressed to Davan exactly, "that the bench outlasts the man at it. Which is, mostly, the point."

The file moved.

Davan let it land. He let it land for the half-beat the line wanted. Then he turned to Ash.

"Walk me through the catalysis math," Davan said. "Not the pamphlet version. The version you'd write down for yourself."

Ash walked him through it.

He talked for an hour. He talked about the cistern exchange and the foot-valve loss and the head pressure and the catalysis cost in the three points of a percent, and about the chimney work, and about the insulation, and about the standpipe, and Davan asked questions. Davan asked questions the way Davan had asked the boy at the pump what to do when the prime didn't take. They were not ambush questions. They were the questions the work was already asking itself. Ash heard them and answered them, and where he did not have an answer he said *I don't know,* and Davan, on the second *I don't know,* nodded and moved to the next question and did not come back to the not-knowing, and Ash registered this, and registered, with the slow clean register an engineer used for a load on a beam, that he had not, in the year since the activation, had a conversation in which his not-knowing had been treated as a fact instead of as a problem.

Ressa's pencil moved.

Korin's shoulders, by the third quarter-hour, had come down off their setting. He was leaning forward with his hands around his cup. He had asked twice. Both questions had been good. Both had been about the leather seat in the foot valve.

Haran filed.

* * *

They came to the diagnosis at the fifth hour.

It was not, Ash registered, an arrival. It was a place the conversation had been walking toward without naming, the way a man and another man at a workbench who had been talking, for an hour, about a single failing seal eventually said, without deciding to say it, *the design is wrong.*

Davan said, "The decline is real."

"Yes," Ash said.

"And what we know about pools is what we've known for eighty years. They get smaller. They get smaller in the next generation, and smaller in the generation after that, and the rate isn't slowing."

"It isn't."

"And the gap between the elder pools and the rest isn't closing. It's widening. Because the elder pools are the last generation that grew up at full strength, and when they go, the cap goes with them, and what's left below the cap is what we have to live in."

"Yes."

"And the institutions that were built to manage this haven't managed it. They've managed the appearance of it. The Council has run subhearings on equitable allocation for two months and produced a report. The report is patient and well-written and recommends a study."

"I've read it."

"Of course you have. Dorenne's good at studies."

Ash registered the Dorenne. He did not, Ash registered, register surprise. He registered the small clean fact that Davan had said the name without theatrics, the way Davan had said his own name on the cart.

"Dorenne's brilliant," Ash said. "She's the best institutional manager the city's had in my lifetime. She's running a system that wasn't built to do what it's being asked to do, and she's running it inside the constraints of what it can be made to do without changing it."

"Yes."

"And the system can't do it."

"No," Davan said. "It can't."

There was a pause. Ressa's pencil moved twice and stopped.

"The elder generation," Davan said, "is hoarding."

"They're hoarding," Ash said. "Most of them not on purpose. They were warm yesterday. They're warm today. The system that warmed them yesterday warms them today. If they were asked to take less they would, mostly, take less, and a few of them would not, and the few would be enough to break the asking."

"Yes," Davan said.

"And the asking has not been done," Ash said. "Not seriously. Not with mechanism behind it."

"No."

"And the Council won't do it."

"The Council can't do it. The Council is the elder generation."

"The Council is the elder generation."

"And the institutions," Davan said, "are the elder generation's institutions. They are not, structurally, capable of the redistribution they're being asked to consider. They will study it and study it and the study will outlast the people who needed the redistribution. And this is not malice. This is what institutions do."

"This is what institutions do," Ash said.

He heard, Ash registered, his own voice say it. He had said it, at twenty-one, in his head, walking past Hellen's house. He had said it at twenty-two at the long table. He had not, in the year, said it out loud to another human being who said it back to him in the same words.

He registered the room. Haran was at the bench. Haran was not, Ash registered without turning, looking at him. Haran had moved from the housing to the spring's second piece and had it under the file again, and the file moved, and the file made the small dry sound the file made.

Korin, on the bench, had set his cup down.

Davan said, "You lived without magic for twenty-three years. You know what's coming for everyone. Why aren't you angry?"

Ash did not answer at once.

He had been, he registered, waiting for the question. He had been waiting for it since the door of the workshop had opened, and before that, since the eastern wall of the Cooperage. The question was the question. The question was the small sharp center of the gap he had been listening for under everything else they had said.

"I was angry," Ash said, "for twenty-three years."

Davan did not interrupt. Davan, Ash registered, did not interrupt anybody.

"I was angry walking up forty flights of stairs every morning. I was angry at my parents' kitchen table. I was angry the night my sister scored highest in her cohort and the family had spent the evening on me. I was angry at a vendor's stall where the woman opened a channeled lock without looking at me. I was angry at twenty-one, and twenty-two, and twenty-three, and I was angry at the door of the infrastructure office on the morning I went up to be told I couldn't apply." He paused. "Being angry didn't build anything."

Davan, across the bench, took this without flinching.

He took it the way Davan, Ash registered, took everything, which was as the next sentence in a conversation he was not afraid of. He let the sentence sit. He let it sit the way the cart had let *we are done asking* sit, half a beat past comfortable.

Then he said, "Anger is a diagnosis."

"Yes."

"You're saying it's not a treatment."

"I'm saying," Ash said, "that the people I was angry for are still cold tonight. Whether I'm angry or not."

Davan nodded. He nodded slowly. He set his cup down on the bench.

"And I'm saying," Davan said, "that patience is what the warm are allowed to recommend to the cold."

A pause.

It was not, Ash registered, a hostile pause. It was the pause two engineers stood inside when they had identified the seam and were each, separately, looking at the load that would be placed across it. The pause had Ressa's pencil in it, not moving. The pause had Korin's cup, not moving. The pause had the file, on the spring, still moving, because the file was Haran, and Haran did not stop a thing in his hand for any pause in any room.

Ash did not, in the pause, say the next sentence. He could feel the next sentence at the edge of his mouth. He could feel its shape. The next sentence was the sentence that began *but the prescription,* and he could feel the prescription and the redistribution and the four mechanisms underneath the sentence, and he could feel, as he could feel the cold at his collar, that to say the sentence now would be to close the thing the conversation had spent four hours opening.

He let the sentence go.

Davan, across the bench, watched him not say it.

Davan, Ash registered, did the same. There was a sentence at the edge of Davan's mouth too. Ash could see, in the small set of Davan's jaw, the shape of it. The sentence was the sentence about the dark windows. The sentence was the sentence about *one pool that could, by itself, hold the lower districts in heat through the next three winters.* Davan let it go.

They sat with the not-saying.

It was, Ash registered, the most honest thing two people could do who had agreed about every sentence except the last one, and who had decided, at the workbench, on the first afternoon, that the last sentence could wait.

"All right," Davan said. He picked up his cup. The cup was empty. He set it down again. "Show me the chimney work."

* * *

He showed him the chimney work.

They did Block Three's flue and the eastern row's insulation and the standpipe's check-valve, and Korin went down on his knees twice more, and Ressa wrote, and the light in the square went the slow flat orange the light went when the city's winter sun had made up its mind to go. By the sixth hour Davan was standing at the workshop door with his coat buttoned, and Korin was at his shoulder with his hands in his pockets again but with the shoulders no longer set, and Ressa was packing the satchel.

"I'd like to come back," Davan said.

"Come back," Ash said.

"I'd like to bring two of our engineers. I'd like them to look at the foot valve."

"Bring them."

"And I'd like, before then, to send you a letter."

"Send it."

Davan nodded. He did not, this time, offer the palm. He offered, instead, the slight bow of the head Ressa had given at the basin, and Ash returned it, and the three of them went out past the bakery and across the square and out by the western gate.

Ash watched them go.

He stood in the workshop doorway with his hand on the doorframe and watched them across the basin and past Vell's pump, where Vell was, at the sixth hour, putting the cover back on for the night, and Vell did not, this time either, look at him. The three figures went out by the western gate and were gone.

He stayed in the doorway.

The square was the square. The basin tap was off. The lamps in the windows of Block Three were beginning to come on, the small irregular pattern, the pattern that was, he registered, warmer than it had been three weeks ago and warmer in a way the people in the windows had begun to take for granted, which was the point.

The dark windows of the canal quarter were not in his line of sight from the doorway.

He did not, he registered, walk around the corner of the workshop to put them in his line of sight. He stood in the doorway. He stood in the doorway long enough that Haran, behind him, said his name once, and he did not turn, and Haran did not say it again.

He went in.

* * *

Haran was at the bench.

The spring was finished. The spring was in its housing and the housing was on the bench in the small clear space Haran kept clear at the south end, and Haran had cleaned the file and laid it across its block and was, when Ash came in, lifting the kettle to pour again.

He poured one cup.

He pushed it across the bench.

"Sit," Haran said.

Ash sat.

He sat on the crate and put his hands around the cup, and the cup was hot, and the heat went up into his hands the way the heat went up into his hands every evening Haran had poured for him in nine months, and Ash, for the first time since the basin tap that morning, registered that he had not eaten.

"Mm," Haran said.

"He's good," Ash said.

"Mm."

"He listens. He doesn't, he doesn't argue at you. He asks, and he waits, and the question is the question you would have asked if you were the one asking. And then he asks the next one."

"Mm."

"He asked the boy at the pump what to do if the prime didn't take. He asked him in front of Vell. He let the boy say *I ask Vell.* And then he thanked Vell for the work."

"Mm."

"He's not what I thought."

Haran did not answer for a beat. He set the kettle back. He moved the spring's housing a quarter-inch, the way he moved a finished thing on the bench when he was not yet ready to put it away. He sat on the second stool, which he did not often do. He did not look at Ash.

"What did you think," Haran said.

"That he'd be a speaker. That he'd come in and, I don't know. Perform. The way he performed on the cart. The way good speakers perform when they're standing on a thing."

"He performed on the cart," Haran said, "because the cart was a cart."

"Yes."

"He didn't, today, perform on a workbench."

"No."

"Mm."

Haran sat. He did not, in the next minute, say anything else. He put his hand around his own cup, which he had not poured, and he sat with the not-pouring and the not-saying, and Ash drank the tea and let the heat go down into his chest the way the heat went down into his chest most evenings, and the small sound of the workshop's stove making itself heard for the first time since the morning came up around them.

Then Haran said, "What did he ask that you couldn't answer."

Ash thought.

He thought back through the hour at the workbench and the hour in the square and the hour at the chimneys, and he ran his hand through it the way a man ran his hand along a seam looking for the line where the file had taken the oxide off.

"He didn't ask anything I couldn't answer," he said.

"Mm."

"He didn't, Haran. He asked the right questions. He asked them and he, he. He didn't put the wrong question in. He could have. He could have asked me, today, about the canal quarter. He didn't ask. He let me see that he wasn't asking. And he. He. He did the thing on purpose."

"Mm."

"He left it for later."

"Mm."

The kettle ticked.

"He'll ask," Haran said.

"I know."

"Mm."

* * *

Days passed.

They were the days the days had been before the Cooperage, with one difference, which was that on the second day a letter came.

The letter came by hand. Ressa brought it. She came alone, in her good coat, with the satchel on her hip, and she came at the third hour past noon, and she handed the letter to Ash at the basin without coming into the workshop, and she said, "He asked me to wait if you wanted to write something back."

Ash read the letter at the basin.

It was four paragraphs. The first paragraph was a thanks. The second paragraph was Davan's sketch of the foot-valve question, with two suggestions, neither of them ones Ash had thought of. The third paragraph asked about the catalysis cost and whether Ash had a curve for it across construction methods. The fourth paragraph said, *I will not, in correspondence, ask you the question I did not ask at the bench. I am keeping it for when we are sitting down again.*

Ash read it twice.

He folded it. He went into the workshop and wrote two paragraphs back, on a piece of bench paper, with a pencil. The first paragraph answered the foot-valve question, and the answer was that Davan's second suggestion was the better one and that Ash would, with permission, try it on the eastern pump in the next week. The second paragraph said, *I will not, in correspondence, answer the question you did not ask. I will, when we are sitting down again, try.*

Ressa took the letter. She bowed at the basin. She left.

Haran, when she had gone, said nothing.

* * *

Davan came back on the fifth day.

He came with two engineers who did not, Ash registered, look like the engineers Dorenne sent. They looked like men who had built things with their hands. One of them was older than Haran. He went into the well casing with a lamp and came up with a thumbprint of green oxide on his sleeve and a list of three small things, and Korin, who came again, asked the questions about the questions, and the afternoon was an afternoon at a pump.

Davan and Ash worked side by side for an hour at the eastern pump.

They did not, in the hour, talk about the platform. They did not, in the hour, talk about the canal quarter. They talked about the foot-valve seat and about the bronze that the city was no longer casting and about an alloy a foundry on the second terrace had been experimenting with that Davan thought might serve and Ash thought might not, and the disagreement about the alloy was the kind of disagreement two engineers had when they were both, in their disagreement, building.

At one point Davan, with his hands in the cylinder, said, "Pass me the smaller punch."

Ash passed him the smaller punch.

It was, Ash registered, the small unremarkable thing. The kind of thing a man said to another man at a workbench when the workbench was the workbench. The kind of thing that did not, when said, mean anything more than what it meant, and that meant, by meaning only what it meant, more than most other things in the day.

He passed the punch. Davan took it. The cylinder seated.

* * *

Haran watched.

Ash registered, on the sixth day and the seventh day and the day after, the particular silence Haran kept around the developing thing. Haran did not, in the workshop in the evenings, ask about the letters. Haran did not, when Davan was there, sit at the bench in the way that asked to be talked to. Haran filed, or filed nothing, or stood at the stove with the kettle, or went out to the bakery to sit a while with the boy who slept above the workshop, and his absences and his presences were not, Ash registered, accidental, and were not, Ash registered, comments.

On the eighth day, at the third hour, Haran came in from the square with bread.

He set the bread on the bench. He set, beside the bread, a small piece of paper Davan had left at the gate when Davan had stopped by at the second hour to drop off a sketch and had not, that day, come into the workshop. The paper was Davan's sketch. Haran had, in the bringing-in, glanced at it. He had not, Ash registered, read it.

"He came," Haran said.

"Mm."

"You weren't here."

"I was at Block Four."

"Mm."

Haran sat on the second stool. He sat the way he had sat on the evening of the Cooperage, the way he did not often sit, with both hands on his knees and his back straight, and he looked at the bench. He did not look at Ash. He looked at the small paper Davan had left.

"He's good for you," Haran said.

Ash did not, at first, register the sentence. He registered it on the second beat. The sentence was not, Ash registered, the sentence Haran said. Haran did not say *good for you* about people. Haran said *mm* about people. Haran said *he listens* or *he does the work,* and on a hard day Haran said *he'll come around,* and Ash had, in nine months, not heard Haran say *good for you* about a person.

He turned to look at Haran.

Haran was not looking at him.

"Mm," Haran said.

"Haran."

"He's good for you. He's good for you the way the bench is good for you when the bench is the only thing in the room you can put your hands on. The bench doesn't argue. The bench takes the weight. The bench is, for an afternoon, the answer to the question you came in with."

Ash waited.

Haran took a breath.

"And then," Haran said, "you walk out into the square."

He stood up. He set his hand on the bench. He looked at the spring's housing for a minute, which was on the bench where he had left it on the day of the visit, and he picked it up and put it on the under-shelf with the small things he kept in case a young tinkerer came by who needed to see one. He did this without looking at Ash.

He went back to the file.

Ash sat at the bench with the bread between his hands and the small paper sketch under his right thumb, and he looked at Haran's back, and he did not say anything, because Haran had said the thing Haran was going to say, and Haran did not, ever, say a thing twice.

* * *

Davan came on the tenth day at the seventh hour.

He came alone, this time. He came in his working coat, and he came without Ressa and without Korin, and he stopped at the workshop door before he opened it, the way a man stopped at a door when he had decided, before opening it, what the conversation in the room was going to be.

Ash was at the bench.

Haran was at the stove.

"Tea?" Haran said.

"Please," Davan said.

Haran poured. He poured for three. He set the cups on the bench and he turned the kettle off the stove and he took, off the peg by the door, the heavy coat he had not worn since the autumn, and he put it on, and he went out by the back door without saying anything, and the back door closed, and Haran was gone.

Davan watched him go.

He watched him with the half-beat attention he gave to anything that meant. Then he turned back to the bench and sat on the stool he had sat on a week before and put his hands around the cup.

"He's letting us talk," Davan said.

"He is."

"Mm." Davan took a swallow. "He doesn't, much, leave."

"He doesn't."

"That's a kindness."

"It is."

Davan looked at the cup in his hands. He looked at it for a longer beat than he had looked at most things in the workshop.

"I want to ask the question I didn't ask," Davan said.

"I know."

"I'm going to ask it now."

"Ask it."

He did not ask it at once. He set the cup down. He laid his palms flat on the bench, on either side of the cup, the way a man laid his palms on a thing when the thing was the table he was about to put the question down on.

"The problem with building for the future," Davan said, "is that people are dying in the present."

He paused.

He paused, Ash registered, the way he had paused on the cart between *every winter* and *that comes,* which was the pause of a man whose pauses were beams.

"Your pump works," Davan said. "I've seen it. I've watched the boy at the crank. I've put my hand under the water. I've seen Tesha at the wall. The proof of concept is real. It's also one district. It's also a year, at the speed your crews are training, away from helping anyone who isn't in this square. Probably more. Probably two. Probably three for the canal quarter. Probably five for the lower terraces. Probably never for the harbor flats."

He waited.

He waited for Ash to interrupt. Ash did not interrupt.

"What do they do until then?" Davan said. "The people in those districts. The people whose windows are dark this winter and were dark last winter and who are old and who are sick and who will not be at the basin in your square in the year you reach them. What do they do."

The question sat on the bench between them.

Ash had, he registered, prepared an answer in the eight days. He had prepared three. He had run them, in his head, on the towpath in the evening when he walked, and at the bench in the morning when he filed, and at night above the workshop in the dark when the reservoir sat behind his attention the way it sat. He had, by the eighth day, settled on one.

He looked at the cup in his hands.

The honest answer, he registered, was the answer he had thought on the towpath the night of the Cooperage. The honest answer was *they wait, because the alternative is worse.* The honest answer was that opening the pool against the canal-quarter cisterns this winter would cost a percent of his reservoir and warm the canal quarter and would, at the same time, by warming the canal quarter, prove to the city and to the Council and to every elder watching that the pool was the answer, and that the answer was a man, and that the man was the system, and that the system Ash had spent nine months building, the system that did not depend on him, was a pretense; and that having proved this, the city would arrange itself, fast, around the new fact, and the new fact would last as long as Ash lasted, and would last not a day longer, and the dark windows of the next generation's canal quarter would be, then, dark in a way that was no one's to help.

The honest answer was that this was the answer of every powerful person in the history of the city who had been asked to share.

He looked up.

He looked at Davan. Davan was looking at him. Davan was looking at him the way Davan had looked at the boy at the pump, which was as a person, with the precise attention he gave to a person, and Davan's face was, in the looking, not unkind.

"I don't have a good answer," Ash said.

Davan did not move.

"I have an answer," Ash said. "I don't think it's a good one. The answer is that if I open the pool against the canal quarter this winter, I warm the canal quarter this winter. I do. And next winter the canal quarter waits for me again, because what I did the winter before was the answer, and the answer is me, and as long as the answer is me, no one builds the thing that isn't me. And I will not, the way pools go, last forever. I won't. And when I'm gone the canal quarter will be cold the way it was cold last winter, except that now the city will have learned to expect not to be cold, and the cold will hit them harder."

"Yes," Davan said.

"You agree."

"I agree with the structure."

"But."

"But the people in the windows tonight are not, this winter, the people in the windows in twenty years. They are people. They are tonight. And the argument you just made is the argument every powerful person in this city has ever made when asked to share."

"I know it is."

"You know it is."

"I know it is. I, I, Davan, I lived in that argument. I read it, in my head, at twenty-one, in every essay in the upper-terrace journals. I hated the man who wrote it. I am sitting at this bench and I am the man who wrote it."

He stopped.

He had not, he registered, meant to say it like that. He had meant to be calmer. He had meant to put the answer on the table the way Davan had put the question on the table, palms flat. He had said it with his hands closed around the cup and his eyes on the bench, and the saying had been, the way the saying had been on the night he had told his mother about the job, raw, too loud. It had cracked, and the crack had been in the part of his voice he could not, in the year, scaffold over.

Davan did not, he registered, do anything visible.

He waited.

He waited the way he had waited on the cart for the yard to make its sound. Then he said, quietly, "And."

"And I don't know what to do with it," Ash said. "I don't. I, the math isn't the question. The math, the math is the math. The math says if I spend one percent now, this winter, on the canal quarter, I'm one percent shorter for the next thing. And the next thing is the foot valve in another district and the cistern in another district and the chimney work in another district. And the math says I have to ration. And the rationing, if you do it on a curve, gets the most people warm in twenty years."

"Yes."

"And in twenty years a lot of people will be dead."

"Yes."

"And Davan, I, I cannot, I cannot know which ones."

"No," Davan said. "You can't."

"And the ones I don't pick, I will not, in my life, ever know I didn't pick. They'll just be cold. And they'll die cold. And the math will say I did the right thing."

"Yes."

"And you. You. You're saying."

"I'm saying," Davan said, "that I also can't know. I'm saying that the answer the platform offers is also a guess. I'm saying that we're both, you and I, at this bench, in a winter, with people in the windows, and our arguments are arguments, and the windows are dark."

He picked up the cup.

He drank. He set the cup down. The kettle on the stove, untended now, made the small sound the kettle made when the heat in it had begun, in Haran's absence, to go.

"I don't think you're a bad person, Ash."

"I know."

"I think you're wrong."

"I know."

"I think the prescription is wrong, and you think mine is wrong, and I think that the gap between us is not, actually, a gap about the people. The gap is about which honest mistake we'd rather make. I'd rather make mine. You'd rather make yours."

"Yes."

"Mm."

He stood up.

He stood up the way he had stood up at the basin the first day, in one motion, and he buttoned his coat, which he had not, in the conversation, unbuttoned, and he picked up his cup and rinsed it in the basin in the corner of the workshop, the small cold-water basin Haran kept for cups, and he set the cup upside down on the rack to drain, the way a man set his own cup at a workbench he had eaten at twice.

He went to the door.

He stopped at the door. He turned.

"I'm going to keep coming," Davan said.

"Come."

"And I'm going to keep asking."

"Ask."

"And there's going to be a day I ask in a way you can't answer, and the not-answering will be the answer."

"I know."

"Mm."

He went out. The door closed. The square received him, and the sound of his boots on the stones of the square went away, the way Davan's voice on the cart had carried without channeling and stopped, as voices stopped, against the wall of the yard.

Ash sat at the bench.

He did not move for a count of ten. He counted the ten the way Vell counted the prime sequence on the eastern pump, slow, in his head, with his hand around the cup. The cup was cold.

The back door opened.

Haran came in with his coat on. He took the coat off and hung it on the peg. He came to the stove and put the kettle on, and the kettle began, on the heat, the small sound it had begun to make when Davan had been at the door, and Haran took it off before it boiled and poured for one cup, and he set the cup on the bench, in front of Ash, beside the cold cup.

"Mm," Haran said.

Ash put his hand on the new cup.

The heat came up into his hand. The heat was the heat the heat had been every evening for nine months. He sat with the heat. He did not, for a long minute, look up.

Haran went to the bench.

He took the spring's housing off the under-shelf and set it back in the small clear space at the south end of the bench, the place a thing went when Haran had decided, in his absence from the room, that the thing was not, in fact, finished.

He picked up the file.

The file moved.

Ash drank the tea.

The dark windows of the canal quarter were, he knew without looking, dark. The square outside the workshop window was the square. The basin tap, from where he sat, was not running, and the eastern pump was under its cover, and the lamps in the windows of Block Three were on.

He stayed at the bench until the second cup, also, was cold.

He did not get up.

Behind him, the file moved, and moved, and moved.
