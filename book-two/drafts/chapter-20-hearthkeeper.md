# Chapter 20

The door of the family apartment was on the latch.

Ash stood on the landing with his hand on the iron and felt the small give that meant his mother had left it for him. She did that on the days she expected him and not on the days she didn't, and the difference between the two latches was a thing only the people who lived in the apartment knew, and the small fact of the latch being on, late on a grey afternoon in the middle of the week, with no dinner planned and no occasion on the calendar, was the kind of fact that arrived ahead of the rest of the visit and arranged the chairs.

He pushed the door.

The apartment was quiet. Not empty quiet, the apartment was rarely empty, but the quiet of a room where the people who filled it had stepped out and the room had not yet adjusted. His mother's coat was not on the peg. His father's cap was not on the shelf above the boots. The boots were there, both pairs, the old work pair and the better pair his father had bought in the summer and still hadn't broken in, and the boots in their row at the door were the first thing Ash noticed because his father didn't go anywhere in his slippers.

"They went to the market," Maren said from the kitchen. "She wanted the good flour. He went because she wanted the good flour."

He closed the door behind him.

"Hi," he said.

"Hi."

He hung his coat on the peg next to where their father's coat would have been. The inside pocket was warm against his hand for the half-second he held the lapel; he thought of Maren's note in it, the bench paper, Joren's letter, the cheap paper book. He let the coat hang. He went into the kitchen.

She was at the stove.

She had her back to him, which was not unusual; Maren's back was a thing she gave a room when she wanted to do a thing without being watched while she did it. She had a kettle on the front burner. The kettle was the old one, the one with the dent in the base and the lid that didn't quite seat unless you set it on with a small twist, the one their mother had carried out of the previous apartment in her own arms when the movers had wanted to pack it. The burner was lit. The flame was a real flame, blue at the base, yellow at the tips, the small particular flame of a stove their parents kept because their mother would not, in any apartment, have a kitchen without a flame in it.

Maren was watching the kettle.

The kettle was not yet on.

Ash sat at the table.

He sat in his chair, which was the chair to the left of his father's chair, which had been his chair since before he could remember, and he set his hands flat on the table and he looked at his sister's back at the stove.

She was thinner than she had been at the summer dinner. Not gauntly. The way a person was thinner when the small daily margin of eating-enough had narrowed by a quarter and the body had adjusted, which was the way Maren got thinner; she didn't stop eating, she just stopped being hungry, and her body kept the books and made the small unannounced cuts. Her hair was tied back the way she tied it for work, the practical knot, though she was not, today, at work. The note in his inside pocket said she was on leave. The note had said that for two weeks. The note had said it in a steady hand that was not, in the steadiness, the hand of someone who was at ease.

She turned the burner down a notch.

She did not turn around.

"You're early," she said.

"I left the workshop early."

"Mm."

The kettle began the small sound a kettle made before it began the bigger sound, the low domestic clearing of the throat that meant water was, somewhere in the dented base, deciding to move.

"Tea?" she said.

"Please."

"Mm."

She did not turn from the stove.

He watched her at the stove a count. He watched her hand on the handle of the kettle, the way her fingers sat on it, the small adjustment she made to the placement of the kettle on the burner that was not necessary because the kettle was already where it needed to be. He watched her not channel.

She had, in this kitchen, channeled the kettle a thousand times.

She had channeled it on summer mornings and winter mornings and the morning after their grandmother died and the morning of her first day at the corps and every ordinary morning between. She had channeled it the way she channeled everything, the small precise pulse, the heat going into the water and nowhere else, no waste, no warmth on the kettle's outside, no rise in the kitchen's air, the engineer's draw on a domestic object. He had watched her do it across this table for years. He had, in the years he could not channel, watched her do it with the small careful neutrality of a man not letting his face be a thing his sister had to manage, and she had done it without meeting his eye, and the choreography had been the choreography.

Today the burner was lit.

Today the kettle was on the flame.

He did not say anything.

She did not say anything.

The kettle climbed toward the boil at the speed a kettle climbed toward the boil on a flame, which was the speed of a kettle that was not being asked to hurry. The kitchen made the small sound of the burner. The burner made the small sound of gas. Outside the kitchen window the grey of the late afternoon sat on the building across the courtyard and did not, in the sitting, commit to rain.

Maren took the cups down from the shelf.

She took them down with both hands, which was the way she took cups down, one in each hand, the small steady reach. She set them on the counter beside the stove. She took the tin of tea from the shelf. She opened it. She set two pinches in each cup with the small wooden scoop their mother had had since before Ash was born, and she set the scoop back in the tin, and she set the lid on the tin, and she set the tin back on the shelf.

The kettle boiled.

She lifted it.

She poured the water into the cups, one and then the other, the slow careful pour of a person pouring boiling water from a kettle whose lid did not, on a wrong tilt, stay on. She set the kettle back on the burner. She turned the burner off. She brought the cups to the table.

She set his in front of him.

She sat across from him in their mother's chair.

She wrapped her hands around her cup.

* * *

He looked at her hands on the cup.

He looked at the cup, which was the chipped one with the green band, which had been her cup since she was seven, and he looked at her fingers around it, and he looked at the small thing he had not, in the last six months, let himself look at.

Her hands were shaking.

Not the way a hand shook when a person was cold, which was a shake that sat in the shoulder and the upper arm and came down to the hand at the end of the long rope of the body. Her hands were shaking at the wrist the way a hand shook when the small fine motor of holding a cup had become, in the holding, a thing the body had to attend to. The shake was not large. It was a person who had been a precision instrument all her life noticing, in her own fingers, the small new tremor of an instrument that was no longer the instrument it had been.

She saw him see it.

She did not move her hands.

She did not put the cup down.

She held it the way she had been holding it, and she looked at him across the cup, and her face was the face she had brought to the kitchen, which was the level face, the engineer's face, the face that had held in council meetings and in hallways and at the edges of council chambers when the men twice her age had interrupted her and the face had not, in the interrupting, given them anything.

She held it a count.

Then she said, "Was it like this?"

He did not, at first, answer.

He looked at her across the table. He looked at the chipped cup with the green band and her hands at the wrist. He looked at the kettle on the burner that was no longer lit. He looked at the kitchen that was, in the absence of their parents, the kitchen of two children who had grown up in it.

He knew what she meant.

He had known what she meant when the latch had been on the door.

"Like what," he said.

She breathed in through her nose. She breathed out. The cup did not, in the breath, settle.

"Like the ground is moving," she said. "Like the thing you built your life on is dissolving and you're supposed to keep standing."

"Yes," he said.

She did not, for a count, speak.

She set the cup down.

She set it down with the slow careful set of a person who had decided, in the count, that holding the cup had become more work than the cup was worth, and the cup made the small ceramic sound a cup made on a wooden table, and her hands came back from the cup and sat in her lap, and she did not, in the sitting, hide them.

The performance flickered.

It flickered the way a lamp flickered when a draught came in under a door, the small wavering of a flame that had been steady, the moment before it either steadied again or went out, and Ash watched his sister's face across the table and watched the small unfamiliar thing of his sister not, for a count, knowing where to put the next sentence.

"I don't know how to do this," she said.

"Mm."

"You. You spent twenty-three years learning how to live without it."

He did not, for a count, answer.

"I don't know how. I never learned. I never had to learn. I was so busy being grateful I had it that I never imagined."

She stopped.

She stopped in the middle of the sentence, the way Maren did not stop. Maren finished sentences. Maren had always finished sentences. The half-sentence sat between them on the table with the cups, and she did not, for a count, finish it, and he did not, for a count, finish it for her.

She picked the cup back up.

She picked it back up with both hands.

The shake was there.

"I felt guilty every day," she said.

The voice was the level voice. It was the level voice with a small thing under it that was not the level voice, which was the small thing of a person saying a sentence they had not, in their life, said out loud.

"Every single day. I would wake up and channel the light on and think, he can't do this. My brother can't do this simple thing that I do without thinking. And then I'd work harder, be better, achieve more, because at least if I earned it. If I was the best at what I could do. Then maybe it wasn't just luck that I got it and he didn't."

She looked at him.

She did not look away.

He sat with it.

He sat with the small steady weight of having been told a thing he had, in a deep quiet way under all his other knowings, known, and he sat with the way knowing a thing in the deep quiet way under was different from being told the thing across a kitchen table by the person it had lived in.

His own hands were on the table.

He looked at them.

"I know," he said. "I always knew."

"You never said anything."

"Neither did you."

The kitchen made the small sound of the kitchen. The kettle made the small ticking sound of a kettle cooling on a burner that had been turned off. From the courtyard came the small far sound of a window being shut on a higher floor.

* * *

She let go of the cup.

She let go of it slowly, the way she did things, the deliberate release that was not, in any of her other gestures, a release; it was a placement. She placed her hands on the edge of the table. She looked at them on the edge of the table. She did not look up.

"Tell me," she said.

He did not, for a count, know which thing she meant.

He thought about which thing she meant.

He thought about the kitchen at twelve when he had sat at this table and watched her channel the light on at dusk and had felt the thing he had felt and had not had a name for, and how the name, when he had found it later, had been a name he had not, then or after, said to anyone in this apartment. He thought about the night of his thirteenth birthday when his father had set the small wooden box on the table and had said, in the quiet way he said the things he had decided not to make a thing of, *we'll know in the morning,* and how in the morning they had known. He thought about the years after, the small daily lake of nothing, the walks to the market, the afternoons at the workshop, the careful cultivation of being-fine, the small specific labor of being a person who did not, in any room he walked into, become the problem the room was already managing.

He thought about which of those things to tell her.

He told her the small one.

"The cheerfulness," he said. "It cost me. I don't know what to call it. It cost. I didn't know that, at the time. I knew it later. I knew it the morning of the river."

"Mm."

"Every joke was an apology. Every. Every. I was paying rent. I was paying rent for taking up space."

"Mm."

"It got bad. Not in a way anyone saw. It got bad on the inside. I would come home from the market and sit at this table and. And eat the bread. And tell her about my day. And the day was a constructed thing. I had constructed it in the walk back from the market. So I would have something to say at the table that didn't make her face do the thing her face did."

He stopped.

He looked at the cup in front of him.

"Her face still does the thing her face does," he said. "She just, the things she's looking for have changed. The looking is the same."

Maren's hands were on the edge of the table.

Her knuckles had gone pale.

"Mm," she said.

He drank the tea.

He drank a sip, which was a sip of tea that had been made on a flame by a woman whose hands shook at the wrist, and the tea was the tea. It was good tea. It was tea their mother had bought from the shop on the corner that had always sold the tea their mother bought, and the leaves had been steeped on a stove and the heat had come up through the dented base of the kettle and it was the same tea it had always been, and it was not the same tea at all.

He set the cup down.

"Your turn," he said.

It came out quieter than he meant it.

She looked at him.

She looked at him a count, the steady evaluating look, and then she nodded, the small short engineer's nod, and she closed her eyes for the half-count people closed their eyes for when they were giving themselves the count they needed before they began.

"I don't fail," she said. "I haven't. I haven't failed at a thing since I was small. I haven't been allowed."

"Mm."

"Nobody told me I wasn't allowed. I told me. I told me when I was seven. I told me at this table. You were six. You were six and you couldn't channel the light and Dad's face when. When the man came to test you. I saw his face. I was seven. I saw his face and I decided. I don't know how a child decides a thing, but a child decides, and I decided, that nobody was going to look at me the way they were looking at you. Not because of anything I would. Not because of what I was. Because of what I did. So I did. I did everything. I did all of it. I was the easy one. I was the one nobody worried about. I was the one who got the marks and made the corps and made the corps young and made senior at twenty-one and did not, in any of it, ever ask anyone for anything. Because there wasn't room."

"Mm."

"There wasn't room. You needed all of it. Mom needed all of her, on you. Dad. Dad had the shop. There wasn't room. And I told myself there wasn't room and then I told myself I didn't need room and then I told myself I was a person who didn't need room, and now."

She stopped.

"And now," she said.

She did not finish.

He waited.

"I can't do the morning load," she said. "At the corps. The morning load is the load the senior does at six before the crew comes in. It's the warm-up. It's the load you do to set the bench. I have done it every working morning for three years. Last week I could not do it. I did half of it. I sat in the office for ten minutes and I could not do the second half. And I did the rest of the day on the half-load and I came home and I told no one. And the next morning I went in and I did, on a good draw, three quarters. And then the pulse went. And I stood at the bench and I watched a junior do the rest of the warm-up and I went into the office and I shut the door and I sat there and I did not, in the sitting, know what I was."

She breathed.

"I don't know what I am," she said.

It was the level voice. The level voice held it. The level voice held it the way a level voice held a thing that the holder did not, in the holding, want to put down, because to put it down was to admit the thing existed at the size it existed at, and Maren had not, in her life, put a thing of that size down on a table in front of another person.

She looked at her hands on the edge of the table.

"I took leave," she said. "I haven't told them why. Joren thinks it's exhaustion. He has been, he has been kind. He has been very kind. And I cannot. I cannot tell him."

"Mm."

"I cannot tell anyone."

"You're telling me."

She looked up.

She looked at him across the table.

Her eyes were wet.

The wet was a thing Maren did not let her eyes do. The wet was a thing that, in twenty-one years of being his sister, Ash had seen her do four times that he could count, and he could count them: the day their grandmother died, the night the cat went out and didn't come back, the morning she got the corps appointment and had stood at the stove with her back to him and channeled the kettle on with a pulse that had been, in its preciseness, the small giving away of how much she had wanted it, and now.

She did not wipe her eyes.

She let the wet sit.

"You're telling me," he said again.

"Mm."

"Yeah."

He breathed.

"Okay," he said.

* * *

The conversation did not, after that, run.

It moved in surges and stops, the way a conversation moved when the people in it had not, in their lives, had it, and the muscles of having it were muscles they were finding in themselves for the first time and using badly.

She told him about the first time she had felt the floor move. It had been six months ago, in early spring, on a morning when the morning load had come in heavier than usual and she had set the pulse and it had not, in the setting, behaved. The pulse had behaved a hundred thousand times. It had behaved across her training years and across her early-corps years and across every small precise pulse she had set on a kettle in this kitchen, and that morning it had not. It had set wrong. She had set it again. It had set wrong the second time. The third time she had set it harder than she needed to and it had set, and the bench had warmed, and the warm-up had finished, and she had sat in the office with the door shut for a count she had not, until much later, accounted for.

She had told no one.

She had told no one for six months.

She had told no one until Ash had walked into the kitchen and sat in his chair and looked at her hands at the wrist.

He told her about the cheap paper book.

He had not meant to. He had not, when he had hung the coat on the peg, intended to. But the thing was that the cheap paper book was the thing he had been carrying, in the inside pocket, against her note for two weeks, and the cheap paper book had, on its last page, the line *It's what they built,* and the line had been written the night before, after a basin on a bracket on an upper terrace, and the line was a thing he could not, telling her about the morning load, not also tell her about. So he told her. He told her about Tesha at the bench. He told her about Liesel from the lower quarter with the cross-stitch patch at the elbow of her coat. He told her about the man at the corner of the square in the dark coat the color of wet stone, who had walked across a lower terrace and up a lane to look at a basin, and had not, looking, gone through Tesha's door. He told her how he had stood at the upper terrace afterwards and had thought, the man had built the ground for the thing he had not, at the end, built. He told her how it had felt.

"It felt like watching someone lose," he said. "It felt like watching someone lose a thing he had earned the right to be the one to build."

"Mm."

"And I am not. Going to tell him. Going to tell him I see it. Because. Because that isn't mine to give him. It would be, the giving would be, a thing a man like Davan would. He would not. He would not take it."

"No."

"No."

She turned her cup with the tips of her fingers.

She turned it a quarter, the way she turned a thing on a bench when she was thinking.

"I'm Davan," she said.

He looked at her.

"In what way."

"In the way of the fight. In the way of the. The proving. The doing-the-fight at the size of the doing-the-fight, because the fight is the thing you have, because if the fight isn't the thing then what was. What was all of it for."

She did not look up from the cup.

"I built my life on a pool," she said. "On a draw. On the precise pulse. On the morning load. On the bench at six. On being the youngest senior in the lower-districts corps. On being the Torren who. The Torren who channels."

"Mm."

"And the pool is going. And I do not know who I am if I am not the Torren who channels. I do not know what I am if I cannot set the pulse. I do not. I do not have a thing to be, under the thing I have been."

"Mm."

He sat with it.

He sat with the small heavy familiar thing of having sat with this thing himself for twenty-three years in a different shape, and he sat with the way the thing's shape, in his sister's voice across this table, was a shape he could see all the way around because it was the inside-out of the shape he had carried.

He had the thing he had to say.

He had not said it, in his life, to any person.

He drank the tea.

He set the cup down.

"I have to tell you something," he said.

"Mm."

"I have to tell you the thing I have not."

She looked up.

"Some part of me," he said.

He stopped.

He breathed.

"Some part of me hated you. For having the pool."

She did not, for a count, move.

"Not you," he said. "I want to. I want to be clear. Not you. I have loved you. The whole of my life. I have loved you and there is not a count in my life I have not. I have not. Loved you. The hate was not at you. The hate was at the. At the fact. At the cosmic, at the. Whatever divided us at the beginning and gave the pool to one and not the other. The hate was at the dividing. And the hate sat next to the love in the same room for twenty-three years and I could not, in the room, ever look directly at it. Because to look at it was to. Was to see I was a person who hated my sister. And I was not a person who hated my sister. So I did not look at it. So I sat in the room with it for twenty-three years, and I came to the table, and I ate the bread, and I watched you channel the kettle on and I made my face the face I made, and the. The hate sat under the face. With the love."

He looked at her.

She had not moved.

"I am telling you," he said, "because I think you should know that the. The cheerfulness and the. The thing I performed. Was a performance over a thing that was not, on the inside, only love. There was a thing under it that was not love. And you. You should know that. You should know that I carried it. And that I love you. And that both of those things were, were both true, and both of them sat in this kitchen across this table for twenty-three years, and I did not, in the twenty-three years, say so."

She looked at him.

Her eyes were wet.

She did not, for a count, speak.

Then she said, "Of course you did."

"Mm."

"Of course you did."

"Yes."

"Of course."

She breathed in.

"I would have hated me," she said.

"Mm."

"In your place. I would have hated me. I would have hated me harder than you hated me. I would have. I would have hated me at the bench every morning for twenty years and I would have come home and sat at this table and watched me channel the kettle and I would have. Mm. Yes. Mm."

She nodded.

She nodded the small short engineer's nod she nodded when a brief had landed.

"Thank you," she said.

He had not expected that.

He had expected anything else.

His face did something he did not, at the time, see, because his eyes had begun to do what hers were doing and his hand was on the edge of the table the way her hand had been on the edge of the table, and the thing in his throat had become the thing in his throat, and the kitchen, in the count, was the kitchen of two adults at a wooden table with cups of tea and the small steady weight of having said the things they had not, in their lives, said.

He cried.

He did not cry hard.

He cried the way a person cried when a long held thing in a low place came up and the body did the small unspectacular work of letting the thing pass through. The tears came down his face. He did not wipe them. His hand stayed on the edge of the table. Across from him, his sister, who had not, in his life, cried in front of him for more than the count of three, sat with her hands on the edge of the table and her shoulders moving with the small breaths of a person who had not practiced this, and her face was wet, and she did not, in the wet, look away from him.

They sat with it.

The kitchen made the kitchen's sound.

The kettle ticked one more small tick on the cooling burner.

In the courtyard, two floors down, a door opened and a child was called, in a voice not their mother's, in for the second time.

Ash thought, without meaning to, of two children in this kitchen at this table, divided by a thing neither of them had chosen, dividing themselves into the shapes a household needed in order to bear what could not be fixed. The cheerful one. The competent one. The one who did not bring problems home. The one who did not make problems where there were none. The shapes had been the shapes that fit. The shapes had been the shapes that had got them, the both of them, here, to this afternoon, to this table, to this conversation. The shapes had done their work.

He grieved them.

He grieved them the way you grieved clothes a child had grown out of, which was a grief that was not, at the bottom, a grief at the clothes, but at the child who had had to wear them, and at the smallness of the child, and at the way the child had not, at the time, known that the clothes were clothes, and had thought the clothes were her body.

Maren wiped her face.

She wiped it with the back of her wrist, the way a child wiped its face.

She breathed out.

"Right," she said.

"Mm."

"Mm."

She looked at her cup.

She picked it up.

Her hands were still shaking. The shake was the shake. It had not, in the conversation, gotten better, and it had not, in the conversation, gotten worse, and the cup came up to her mouth, and she drank, and she set the cup down.

"Cold," she said.

"Mine too."

"Mm."

* * *

She got up from the table.

She got up the way she got up, the small economical lift, and she took the cups to the counter and she set them in the basin, and she put a hand on the kettle to test it, and the kettle was warm, and she did not, in the testing, channel anything. She lifted the kettle. She filled it at the tap. She set it back on the burner. She turned the burner on. The flame came up, blue at the base, yellow at the tips, and she stood at the stove a count with her hand on the kettle's handle, watching the flame.

"They'll be back soon," she said.

"Mm."

"Did you eat."

"At the workshop."

"That isn't eating."

He almost laughed.

He did not, in the almost, look up at her at the stove, because the almost-laugh was a thing he didn't want to break, and the not-looking was the small care he had learned across twenty-three years of being in this kitchen with this sister, and he sat with the almost-laugh and the cup-shaped hollow on the table where his cup had been and the small steady warmth of the kitchen with the burner on.

"I'll eat at dinner," he said.

"Mm."

"I'll eat all of it."

"Mm."

She did not, at the stove, turn.

He could see the small set of her shoulders that was not the set she had brought into the kitchen. The set was not lighter, exactly. He could not have said it was lighter. But it was a different set. The shoulders were the shoulders of a person who had set a thing down, and the setting-down was not the same thing as the thing being gone. The thing was not gone. The pool was still going. The morning load was still half. The corps was still the corps. The afternoon was still the afternoon it had been when she had put the kettle on the burner and not, in the putting, channeled it. None of that had changed.

The way she stood at the stove had changed.

He watched her stand at the stove and not channel, and he sat with the watching, and he did not, in the sitting, name the thing.

The downstairs door opened.

It opened with the small pneumatic sound the downstairs door made and then the small wooden sound of the inside door, and from the stairwell came the voices of his parents, the voices that arrived together at the apartment whenever they had been at the market together, his mother's voice setting the agenda and his father's voice agreeing in the small particular way his father agreed, which was the agreement of a man who had, on the walk back, decided which battles to fight at which counters in the next hour.

Maren did not turn from the stove.

"They're early," she said.

"Mm."

She wiped her face one more time with the back of her wrist.

She wiped it the small careful way, and then she picked up the dishcloth from the rail at the side of the stove and dried her hands on it that hadn't been wet, and she set the dishcloth back, and she squared her shoulders the small fractional square, and she did not turn from the stove.

She waited at the stove.

Her face was the face she would, in a count, give to their mother. It was not the face she had had at the table. It was the face she gave a doorway. Her hands at the kettle were the hands she had brought into the kitchen, except for the shake, which she would, in a count, slide out of sight by tucking the left hand at her hip and using the right hand to lift the lid, and Ash watched her, in the count before the door, do all of these things.

He did not say anything about them.

He did not need to.

He sat at the table and arranged his own face the small careful arrangement he had spent twenty-three years arranging it into, and he was aware, in the arranging, of the arranging, and the awareness was new. He had, until this hour, not been aware of the arranging while he arranged. He had been the arranging. Now the arranging was a thing he did with hands. He did it. He set his cup back where his cup went. He moved Maren's chair, which was their mother's chair, back the small fraction it had moved out from the table in the conversation. He turned in his seat to face the door.

Maren caught his eye.

She did it without turning her head.

She did it in the glass of the cabinet over the stove, where the kitchen's light made the glass a small mirror, and in the mirror her eye and his eye met for the count of a single look, and in the look there was the thing that, an hour before, would have been a thing the look did not say.

The look said it.

The look said *I see you arranging.*

The look said *I am arranging.*

The look said *we are doing this together because we have to and we know we are doing it and we know that the other knows.*

The look held a count.

Then it broke, and Maren tipped the lid back onto the kettle the small careful way she tipped it, and the latch on the apartment door clicked, and their mother came in.
