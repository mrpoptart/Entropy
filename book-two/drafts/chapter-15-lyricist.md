# Chapter 15

He came up the curve at the first hour with the question in his pocket like a stone he had been turning since the canal.

Three weeks since Ryn. Three weeks of Tuesdays. Three weeks of watching her finish a coupling and putting his hand to a wrench when his hand was asked for and not before, three weeks of walking up to the wall at the third hour and watching boats that did not need watching, three weeks of *not deciding* while a thing in him decided anyway. The thing had decided this morning. He had woken at the second hour with the shape of it already at the front of his mind, and the shape was not *what to build,* which was the shape he had been carrying down the curve every morning of his life, but a different shape, a colder one, the shape of a question he had not let himself ask because the asking was the harder thing.

How do you make it last.

The workshop's lamp was lit when he came around the bend. It was always lit when he came around the bend. Haran was at his bench under the long window with the wire at his fingers and the fire low at the forge behind him, and the door stood half open the way Haran always left it after the third quarter, and Ash went in.

"Morning."

"Mm."

He hung his coat on the peg. He stood a count at his bench. The geared mechanism sat where he had left it, half assembled, the brass cap clean and the spring loose in its housing, and he did not pick it up. He walked past it. He went to the slate Haran kept on the wall by the forge for figuring, the long grey board with the chalk on a string, and he wiped a corner of it clean with his cuff, and he started writing.

He wrote the reservoir at the top. The number that was a fraction. *Thirty-seven,* he wrote, the percent sign dragged in afterward as if remembering. Beneath it he wrote the district's daily draw, the new draw, the one Tesha had been running her notebook on for two months, and beneath that he wrote the hill country's draw, and beneath that the coastal village's, and beneath that he wrote a question mark and circled it and wrote, beside it, *Ardenmere?* and circled that.

He stood back from the slate.

The numbers did what the numbers did.

He had done this calculation a hundred times in his head in the past three weeks and every time it had come out the same, but seeing it on the slate was a different seeing, and the difference was that on the slate the numbers did not close their eyes when he wanted them to. The reservoir was thirty-seven. The draw, even at the gentlest scaling, the smallest, the stingiest version of a thing that could be called *building,* ate into thirty-seven at a rate his pencil did not want to write down. He wrote it down anyway. He wrote the curve. The curve was the curve.

Months, the curve said. Not years.

He stood at the slate and let the months be months.

"You're at it again," Haran said, without looking up.

"Mm."

"Same answer."

"Same answer."

"What would change it."

He turned. Haran had the wire pinched between thumb and finger and was sighting along its length, and the sighting was the answer-of-the-body Haran had to a question that was not yet a question, and Ash watched him and did not, for a count, speak.

"I don't know," he said.

"Good."

"Good?"

"Good is when you don't know. Bad is when you think you do."

Haran set the wire down. He picked it up again. He bent it a degree and sighted again and set it down again. The forge breathed behind him, low, the small steady breathing of a fire that had been banked since dawn and was waiting to be asked for something.

Ash went back to the slate.

He wiped the corner clean again. He wrote *waste* at the top, and beneath it he wrote, *60%? 80%?*, and he drew a line under it and wrote *if halved,* and the writing of *if halved* was a thing he did with his hand before his mind had caught up with what his hand was doing, and when his hand had finished his mind ran the line out and saw, for the first time, where it went.

The curve, halved, was a different curve.

The months, halved, were not years yet, but they were closer to years than they had been when he'd come up the curve, and the *closer to years* was a thing his chest registered before the rest of him did, the small lift of a number that had been a wall becoming a number that was a slope you could walk.

He stood at the slate.

He stood at the slate and let it be a slope.

\* \* \*

The forge was not loud.

That was the thing he noticed, when he turned away from the slate, when he turned away from the numbers and let his eyes fall on Haran working, that was the thing his ears registered before his mind named it. The forge was not loud. Haran was at the forge now, had moved there while Ash was at the slate, and was working a length of iron rod in the coals, and the coals were doing what the coals did, and the iron was doing what the iron did, but the air around Haran was, Ash realized, *quiet,* in a way that Ash's air, when Ash worked the forge, was not.

Ash's forge was hot all the way out to the wall.

When Ash worked, the heat came off the forge in waves and the waves rolled across the workshop and the wall behind his bench warmed and the air at his collar went thick and his shirt at the small of his back went damp, and that was working, that was the body of working, and he had not, until this morning, thought of it as anything but working.

Haran's forge was hot at the iron.

The iron was orange where the iron needed to be orange. The coals, three inches off the iron, were the dim red of coals that had not been pushed. The air at Haran's shoulder was the air at the workshop's shoulder, ordinary, indifferent, the air that came in under the door in the cold months, and Haran's shirt at his back was dry.

Ash stood at the slate with the chalk in his hand and watched.

Haran took the iron out. He laid it on the anvil. He brought the small hammer down on it three times and the three were the three, each one set at a place a wrist had decided on without telling the wrist, and the iron took a curve. He put the iron back in the coals. He did not, in the putting back, push the coals. He let the coals be the coals. He stood at the forge with one hand resting on the long bar and the other on his hip and waited, and the waiting was the work too, and the air at his shoulder did not change.

Ash crossed the workshop.

He crossed it slow, with the chalk still in his hand, and he stopped two paces from the anvil so he was not in Haran's working space, and he watched. Haran took the iron out again. The orange was a different orange. He set it on the anvil. Three taps. The taps were the taps. Back into the coals.

"How do you do that."

Haran did not look up.

"Do what."

"That."

"You'll have to be more specific, Ash."

"The forge."

"Mm."

"It's not. The heat. It's not coming off it the way it comes off mine."

Haran turned the bar in his hand a quarter and set it back. He looked, finally, at Ash. His face did not do anything in particular. It did the thing it did when he had been waiting for a question and the question had arrived a little later than he'd expected and a little differently than he'd expected and he was, at the looking, deciding whether to give the long answer or the short.

"Thirty years of practice. Give or take."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer."

"Haran."

"Mm."

"Show me."

\* \* \*

Haran took the iron out of the coals and laid it aside on the brick and let it cool a count, because the demonstration was not going to be, Ash understood, a thing about iron. It was going to be a thing about heat. He wiped his hands on his apron. He stood at the forge with the long bar in his hand the way a man stood at a tool that did not, particularly, belong to him, that he was the steward of and not the owner of, and he set the bar down.

"Watch the coals."

Ash watched the coals.

Haran put his hand, palm down, six inches above the coals. He held it there. The coals did not, at first, do anything. Then, slow, slow as the slow of a thing being asked something it would have to think about, the coals at the center under his palm went a degree brighter. A hand-sized circle of brighter. The circle did not spread. The coals at the edge of the circle stayed the dim red they had been. The air at Haran's wrist did not change.

"Feel it."

Ash put his palm out, an inch from Haran's. He felt the heat coming up off the coals where Haran was working, the small pillar of warmth that rose between Haran's palm and the coals, narrow, contained, the width of the brighter circle and not wider. Past the edge of the circle the air was the air. He moved his palm a hand's breadth out and there was nothing. He moved it back and there was the pillar again.

"How."

"You're channeling into the coals."

"Yes."

"You're channeling into the *coals.* Not the air. Not the bar. Not the brick. The coals. And not all of the coals, because most of the coals don't need it. The ones at the center under the work. Those."

"Yes, but."

"You're channeling at the size of the work. Not at the size of the forge."

Ash watched the circle.

"The iron is going to want a circle the size of itself. So you give it a circle the size of itself. The brick around it is happy as it is, the brick was warm an hour ago and will be warm an hour from now and does not, today, need anything from you. The air is air. You leave the air alone."

"That's, "

"That's the thing. Yes."

Haran lifted his palm. The brighter circle held a half count after his palm went and then dimmed back to the dim red of the rest of the coals, and the dimming was the slow dimming of a thing returning to itself.

"Most channelers," Haran said, "channel at the size of the room."

"Mm."

"Because that's what the body wants to do. The body wants to push, and pushing is loud, and loud feels like work, and feeling like work feels like working. So you push, and the coals get hot, and the air gets hot, and the brick gets hot, and your shirt gets hot, and you tell yourself the heat in your shirt is the price of the heat in the iron. It isn't."

"It's waste."

"It's waste."

"How much."

Haran picked the iron back up. He turned it in his hand and frowned at the part where the orange had bled to a duller orange while they had been talking, and he put it back into the coals without looking at Ash.

"On a hot forge with a sloppy channeler? Sixty percent. Two thirds, if the channeler's tired or distracted, which most of us are most of the time. The energy that gets to the iron is the third that found its way there."

"Sixty."

"Rough number. I'm not going to write you a curve. But sixty, yes. Sixty out the back of your shirt, into the brick, into the air, into the rafters. Forty into the work."

Ash thought of his shirt.

He thought of the wall behind his bench going warm. He thought of the day in the second week of Stonecurve when he had rebuilt the heating lattice in Building Four and the room had been a furnace by the third hour and he had been proud of it, of the heat, because the heat had felt like effort, and the effort had felt like care, and the care had felt like the right amount.

The heat in his shirt was the price of the heat in the iron.

It wasn't.

"The traditions," he said. He said it slow. "The way it's taught. The push."

"The traditions were built when the reservoir was full."

"Mm."

"When you have an ocean you don't worry about a leak. You worry about the boat. You channel for the work and you don't care that two thirds of it goes out the back, because two thirds of an ocean is still an ocean. Now."

"Now."

"Now we don't have an ocean."

Haran did not say it the way it had been said in the broadsheets. He did not say it the way Davan said it. He said it the way a man said a thing about the weather that was the case, and the saying was the saying, and the saying did not perform the saying.

"You should know what your choices are," he said.

"Mm."

"That's all. Most channelers don't. Most channelers were taught to push and have pushed all their lives and have not, in their lives, been told there was a choosing. There's a choosing."

He took the iron out. He laid it on the anvil. The orange was a clean orange, the kind of orange that was the right orange for the work and not a degree past it, and he brought the small hammer down on it once, twice, three times, and the iron took the curve he wanted, and the air at his shoulder was the air at the workshop's shoulder, and his shirt, at the small of his back, was dry.

\* \* \*

Ash went back to his forge.

His forge was at the other end of the workshop, the smaller of the two, the one he had been using since Haran had cleared a corner for him in his second year, and he stood at it now and looked at it the way a man looked at a tool he had used for years and had not, until this morning, thought to look at.

The coals were banked. He drew them up. He let them breathe. He did not, in the drawing-up, push.

He took a length of scrap rod off the rack. It was a piece he had used before for figuring, a piece that did not matter, the kind of piece a man kept for the practicing of a hand on a thing he did not have to be careful with. He set it on the brick. He took up the long bar and set it under the rod and lifted the rod into the coals.

He put his palm above the coals.

He thought *small.*

He thought *the size of the rod, not the size of the forge.* He thought it the way Haran had done it, narrow, contained, a circle the width of a palm and not wider, and he reached his attention down through his hand into the coals at the center under the rod, and he asked the coals, quietly, gently, for the heat the rod was going to want, and he tried to ask only the coals at the center.

The coals did nothing.

He waited a count. He asked again. The coals at the center went a degree brighter, less than a degree, the smallest brightness, and the brightness held a half count and then dimmed, and the rod in the coals did not, in any way that he could see, change.

He frowned.

He pulled his hand back. He shook it out. He put his palm above the coals again.

This time he asked harder.

The asking-harder was a thing his body did before his mind had instructed his body to do it. He felt the asking go down through his palm and into the coals and he felt it spread, because the asking-harder was wider than the asking-quieter, and the wider asking caught the coals at the edge of his palm too, and the coals at the edge went bright with the coals at the center, and the bright was a wider bright than Haran's had been, and it held longer, and the air at Ash's wrist warmed, and his shoulder warmed, and the workshop's air, in the small bowl of it that surrounded the forge, took on the soft pressure of heat that wanted out.

The rod heated.

It heated too much. It went past orange. It went to the white of a piece of iron that was past where the smith wanted it to be, and Ash, seeing the white, jerked his palm back, and the jerking was the jerking of a man whose body had told him *that was too much* before his eyes had told him, and the rod sat in the coals at the wrong color and the brick under the coals took the heat the coals were not using and the brick warmed and the air at the bench beside the forge warmed and the wood of the bench, the bench that had been there since Haran's father had set it, the long bench that had taken thirty years of forge-work and not, in those thirty years, been scorched, the bench took, in one neat circle the size of Ash's outstretched fingers, a black mark.

He stood with his hand out.

He looked at the mark.

The mark was the dark of a thing wood went when wood had been asked to be hotter than wood was meant to be, and it was small, smaller than a coin, and it would never come off, and the bench, which had not been scorched in thirty years, was now scorched, and the scorching was Ash's.

He looked at Haran.

Haran had not, throughout, looked up.

Haran was at his own bench, working a different piece, with the air at his shoulder ordinary and his shirt dry, and the not-looking-up was the not-looking-up of a man who had heard everything, who had heard the first quiet asking and the harder asking and the rod going past orange and the small breath Ash had taken when the bench had taken the mark, and who had, in the not-looking-up, decided not to make any of it a thing.

Ash put the rod aside.

He took up another piece. He set it in the coals. He stood at the forge again.

Smaller, he thought. Smaller than smaller.

He put his palm above the coals. He reached his attention down. He found the coals at the center under the new rod. He asked, careful, the way a man asked a thing of a child he did not want to startle. He asked the coals at the center for *just enough.*

The coals at the center went a degree brighter. The brightness was a hand's-width brightness, not a palm's-width, and his pulse jumped a half count because the hand's-width was the right shape, was Haran's shape, was the shape of the work and not the shape of the forge.

The rod did not heat.

The brightness held. The hand's-width was steady. He kept his palm where it was. He asked the brightness to give the rod what the rod needed. The brightness held. The rod, in the brightness, did not heat. He had narrowed too far, narrowed past the rod, and the brightness, however neat, was a brightness in the coals and not in the iron, and the iron sat in the brightness and was the iron the iron had been when he'd put it in.

He let his hand fall.

"It went small," he said, to no one, to himself, to the bench.

"It did," Haran said, from across the workshop.

"It didn't reach the rod."

"No."

"I asked it for the rod."

"You asked it for *small.* That isn't the same thing."

Ash let his hands hang. He stood at the forge a count. He looked at the scorch on the bench and at the cold rod in the coals and at his own forearm, where, under the sleeve, the skin felt the way skin felt after a thing had been asked of it that had not, in the asking, been clean. The skin felt like a shirt that was a degree damp. Not soaked. Not dry.

He went back to the rod.

He tried again.

This time he tried to think of the rod and not the coals, of the iron at the center of the brightness and not the brightness itself, and he asked the coals to give the iron a particular orange, and he tried to hold the asking at the iron and not at the air and not at the brick and not at the rafters, and what came out of him was a thing that was neither of the things he had done. The brightness was a middling brightness. The iron warmed. The iron warmed unevenly, brighter on one face and duller on the other, because he had been holding the asking at the iron but had been holding it at half the iron, the half he was looking at, and the half he was not looking at had gone without. The air at his collar warmed a degree. Not the wave. A degree. The bench did not scorch. He turned the iron in the coals so the dull face came up, and he asked again, more carefully, and the dull face caught up a degree, and the iron was, for a count, the orange he had wanted.

He stood at the forge with his hand out and held the asking and watched.

It was barely different.

That was the thing. It was barely different from how he had always done it. The air at his collar still warmed. His shirt at the small of his back still went, by a small amount, damp. The bench did not scorch but the brick under the rod warmed past where the brick under the rod had needed to be. He had not done what Haran had done. He had done a worse version of what Haran had done. He had, in the trying, narrowed and overcompensated and adjusted, and the adjusting had landed somewhere that was not, in any meaningful sense, a place. It was the road between two places. He was on the road.

He pulled his palm back.

"Thirty years," Haran said, without looking up.

"Mm."

"Give or take."

Ash laughed.

He laughed once, short, the laugh of a man who had been told a thing that was both an answer and not an answer and who had, in the telling, been given the only answer he was going to be given. He set the rod aside on the brick. He stood at the forge with his hands at his sides and let the forge be the forge. The coals settled into themselves. The small brightness he had drawn from them dimmed. The air at his collar cooled.

He went back to the slate.

\* \* \*

He stood at the slate a long count.

The slate had the curve on it. The curve had not changed. The numbers had not changed. The reservoir was thirty-seven, would, by tonight, be thirty-six, the small daily slip the math had been making since the autumn, the slip that was now a fact of waking up.

He picked up the chalk.

He drew a second curve.

The second curve was the first curve with the slope halved. He drew it slow, with the chalk dragging on the slate's grain, and the second curve, being half the slope of the first, ran out across the slate at a different angle, an angle that did not, before the slate ran out of room, hit zero. It hit zero somewhere off the edge of the slate, somewhere that, on the slate of his life, was a different country.

He stood at the slate and looked at the two curves.

The first curve was *months.*

The second curve, the curve where he wasted only thirty percent of every channel instead of sixty, the curve where he asked the size of the work and not the size of the forge, the curve where he was *halfway* to Haran, not all the way, not even close to all the way, just *halfway,* the second curve was *more than months.* The second curve was, by the math, a number of times the first curve. He did not write the number. He could see the number without writing it. The number was the number.

His hand, on the chalk, went still.

He had, three weeks ago, walked away from the canal with Ryn's words in him. He had, three weeks ago, decided that he was not going to disappear and was not going to decide and build alone, and he had, in the deciding, narrowed his thinking to the question of *what,* the question of what to build, the question of whose mouth the answer was in. He had been working on *what.*

This was not *what.*

This was *how.*

This was the thing that sat under *what,* the thing the *what* did not get at, the thing Haran had been doing for thirty years and had not, until this morning, been doing in front of him in a way Ash had been able to see. This was the discipline that turned a curve into a different curve. This was the thing that made a *what* into a *what that lasted.*

He set the chalk down.

He turned from the slate.

\* \* \*

The forge had gone dark.

Haran had banked it again. He was at his bench with the wire back in his hand, the small fine work of the resonance fitting that he had been at when Ash had come in, and he was not, particularly, looking at Ash. He had been not looking at Ash for an hour. The not-looking-at-Ash was a kindness Ash recognized only because he had been raised by Maren, who not-looked the same way, and had been loved by Ryn, who not-looked the same way, and had been worked by Haran, who not-looked the same way, and the not-looking, in the workshop, was a room a man could stand in.

Ash stood in the room.

He stood in it and he did not, for a long count, do anything.

Outside the long window the morning had moved to the third hour. The light on the brick across the lane was the pale light that the brick took before the sun got to it proper, and a pigeon went along the gutter and stopped and went, and the pigeon did what the pigeon did. The lamp by the door was still lit because Haran had not, in the hour, gone over to it, and the lamp burned the small steady burn of a lamp that was, by the third hour, no longer needed.

Ash thought of the slate.

He thought of the second curve.

He thought, slow, of what the second curve meant if he let it mean what it meant. The reservoir was thirty-seven percent. At his current rate of waste, at the rate he had been at since the earthquake, at the rate he had been at when he had built the pump for Branner and the heating retrofit for Building Four and had walked Tesha through the rebuild of the eastern row, at sixty percent waste, the curve ran out in months. The reservoir reached zero in months. He had counted them, in the small hours, in the back of the workshop, when his counting had been the thing he did to not think about Ryn or Dorenne or Sera at the laundry or the boy in Stonecurve who had asked him what his name was. Months.

If he halved the waste.

If he learned, slowly, painfully, over years, to channel the way Haran channeled, to ask the size of the work and not the size of the room, to leave the air alone, to let the brick be the brick, to be dry at the small of his back at the end of a working day, the curve ran out in *years.*

The years could be many years.

The years could be, depending on the discipline, a lifetime.

He thought of a lifetime.

He thought of the reservoir, reduced by a third, holding for the rest of his life. Holding for things he had not yet imagined. Holding for the moment, ten years out, when the hill country settlements that Haran had drawn for had matured and a child in one of them had broken a leg and there was no elder near, and Ash, being Ash, walked up the hill, and put his hands on the leg, and gave the leg, *cleanly,* the energy the leg needed, and walked home, and the reservoir, at the end of it, was almost the same reservoir it had been at the start of the day. Holding for the moment, twenty years out, when the coastal village's seawall failed in a storm and Ash, being Ash, with the discipline of two more decades in his hands, channeled through his palms into the stone and held the stone in its place for the half hour the storm asked it to be held, and walked home, and the reservoir, at the end of it, was not, in any meaningful sense, smaller. Holding for the moment, thirty years out, when he was an old man on the long bench under the window, with wire at his fingers, and a young person came through the door, and the door was half open the way Haran always left it after the third quarter, and the young person came to him with a question, and he, Ash, did not have to spend his reservoir on the question, because the question was a question of *how,* and he had, by then, the *how* in his hands, and the reservoir, the reservoir, *was still there.*

He stood in the workshop.

The forge was dark.

He stood at the dark forge with his hands hanging at his sides and let the picture be the picture, and the picture was warm, and the warmth of the picture was the warmth that comes off a thing a man wants and has only, in the wanting, just begun to allow himself to want.

He thought of the second curve.

The second curve was a slope you could walk.

If he walked it, *carefully,* if he chose his work, *carefully,* if he built only what could be built in a way that did not eat the reservoir, if he stopped, mostly, building, if he did the small clean things and not the large hot things, the reservoir was a thing that *stayed.* He could be the man who, at the end of his life, handed his student a thing that still had something in it. He could be the man whose great work was, in the end, the *not-using* of the great work. He could be extraordinary the way a held breath was extraordinary, the way a saved coin was extraordinary, the way a thing was extraordinary that, having been preserved, became precious to the people who came after.

He just had to stop building.

The thought came down slow.

It came down the way a thing came down that had been turning at the top of a tower for an hour and had finally fallen, and the falling was a long falling, with the thing turning end over end through the air, slow, and Ash watched it fall and saw, in the falling, the shape of it.

He just had to stop building.

He had to take the work he had not yet started and not start it. He had to take the next district, the second district, the third, the hill country, Ardenmere, the request from the coastal village that had come in the resonance fitting last week, the broadsheet in his coat pocket about the upper terraces and the lattice that Esten's apprentice had been reassigned away from, the cold flat where Hira sat with her baby and timed the feeding to the brick across the lane, all of it, all of the *what* he had been turning over in his head for three weeks while he learned, slowly, to ask the right questions, all of it, and he had to set it down. He had to use the *how* he was beginning to find on the work that was already done, on the maintenance, on the small fixings and the small holdings, and not on the new building. He had to be the man who held a reservoir for a long time and who, in the holding, stayed extraordinary.

Stay extraordinary.

The phrase was the phrase. It was warm. It was warm the way a coat was warm in a doorway, the way a kettle was warm in a room, the way a thing was warm that a person had pulled in from the cold and had not yet let cool.

He let it be warm.

He let himself, for a count, want it.

He wanted it.

He wanted it the way a man wanted a long quiet evening at the end of a year of bad nights. He wanted it the way a man wanted to sit down. He wanted it the way Haran, perhaps, had wanted it twenty years ago when he had begun to see how the discipline could be a discipline of preservation and not a discipline of work, the way a man wanted the small clean lasting thing instead of the large hot ending one. The wanting was a true wanting. The wanting had no edge. It was warm and it was clear and it was, in the workshop at the third hour with the forge dark and the lamp by the door still burning, the most reasonable thing he had wanted in a year.

He stood at the forge and let himself want it.

He let the picture run.

He let the picture run all the way out, to the long bench under the window, to the young person at the door, to the reservoir at the end of his life that was still, somehow, a reservoir, and the picture, in running, was a picture he could see the inside of, could feel the inside of, a picture warm with the warmth of *enough,* of *kept,* of *not lost,* and the inside of the picture was a place he could live.

He could live there.

He could.

The forge was dark.

The lamp by the door burned its small steady burn.

Haran's wire at the bench made the small fine sound that the wire made when it was being bent a degree at a time, and the sound was the sound, and the workshop was the workshop, and outside on the lane a cart went past with a wheel that sang on the cobble, and the singing was the singing, and the cart went on.

Ash stood at the forge.

He just had to stop building.

\* \* \*

He did not move for a long count.

He stood at the dark forge and let the want sit in him and did not, in the letting, do anything with it. He did not refuse it. He did not accept it. He let it be the thing it was, which was a warm thing, a true thing, a thing that did not, in the warmth of it, lie, and he watched it.

He watched it the way a man watched a fire he was thinking of warming his hands at.

After a count he turned.

He went, slow, back to the slate.

The slate had the two curves. The first curve, *months.* The second curve, *years.* He took the chalk in his hand. He stood at the slate. He did not, for a half count, do anything with the chalk.

Then he drew a third line.

The third line was not a curve. It was a flat line. It started at thirty-seven and ran straight across the slate at thirty-seven, level, the line of a reservoir that was not, in any way, being spent, the line of a man who had stopped building. He drew it carefully. He drew it from the left edge of the slate to the right edge. It ran past where the second curve hit zero. It ran past where the second curve was still falling. It did not hit zero. It went off the slate at thirty-seven, where it had started.

He stood at the slate.

He looked at the three lines.

The first line was *months.* The second line was *years.* The third line was *forever, if I do nothing.*

He looked at the third line a long count.

He thought of Hira at the window with the baby in her coat, timing the feeding to the brick across the lane.

He thought of Margit at her stoop with the lattice cold by the third hour and her hands hurting at seventy-one.

He thought of the lattice running cold under his boots in the upper terraces and the long stretch of cold being longer than the stretch of warm and his counting it because his counting had been a thing his attention had done when his attention had not wanted to be doing the thing it had been doing.

He thought of Ryn at the canal. *I'm not asking for it anyway.*

He thought of the boy in Stonecurve who had asked him what his name was and then, on being told, had nodded the small nod of a child who had taken the answer and put it somewhere it would, in him, outlast the giving.

He stood at the slate.

He did not erase the third line.

He did not erase any of the lines.

He set the chalk down on the ledge of the slate. He stood at the slate a count more. He turned, and he went back across the workshop to the forge.

The forge was dark.

He drew the coals up.

He did not push. He let the coals breathe. He took up a fresh length of scrap rod from the rack and set it in the coals on the long bar. He put his palm above the coals. He thought *the size of the work.* He asked the coals at the center under the rod for the heat the rod was going to want, narrow, contained, the width of the rod and not wider, and his hand was unsteady, and the asking was unsteady, and the brightness that came up was a wider brightness than he wanted and a narrower brightness than he had been getting, and it was, exactly, on the road between two places, and he was, exactly, on the road.

He stood at the forge with his palm out and asked again.

The brightness held a half count steadier this time. He thought, the second curve.

He thought, *years.*

He thought, *not stopped.*

He kept his palm where it was.

Behind him, at the bench, Haran's wire made the small fine sound that the wire made.

The lamp by the door burned its small steady burn.

The brightness, under his palm, was the brightness of a thing he was learning, the brightness of a thirty-year discipline at the first hour of the first day, and the brightness was, by every measure, a wasteful brightness, and the brightness was, by every measure, less wasteful than the brightness he had, an hour ago, been making.

He held it.

The rod, in the brightness, took a degree of heat.

A degree.

Not the orange. Not the white. A degree.

He let the brightness go. He pulled his palm back. He stood at the forge with his hand at his side and looked at the rod in the coals, the rod that had, in the asking, taken a degree of heat, and he stood there a count, and then another count, and then he reached his palm out and asked again.

A degree.

He held it.

He let it go.

He asked again.

A degree.

He stood at the forge in the small slow rhythm of a man learning a new asking, and the asking was a degree at a time, and the rod was a degree warmer at a time, and the bench did not scorch, and the brick under the coals warmed only the small warm a brick under coals was meant to warm, and his shirt at the small of his back was, by a small amount, damp, but less damp than it would have been an hour ago, and his shoulder was, by a small amount, warm, but less warm than it would have been an hour ago, and the air at his collar was the air, and the workshop's air was the workshop's air.

Behind him Haran said nothing.

The not-saying was the not-saying.

Ash stood at the forge and asked the coals for a degree, and held it, and let it go, and asked again, and the slate behind him on the wall had three lines on it, and the third line was not erased, and the want was still in him, warm, present, the want of a man who had seen a country he could go to and was, for the moment, choosing not to.

He kept asking for a degree.

The rod, slow, took the heat.

The forge was quiet.

*He just has to stop building,* he thought. The thought came one more time, soft, the soft of a thing that was not, yet, gone.

He held his palm above the coals.

He asked for a degree.
