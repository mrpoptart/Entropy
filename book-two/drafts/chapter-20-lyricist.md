# Chapter 20

The apartment door was not locked.

Ash stood at the landing a count with his hand on the iron of the rail, and he listened, the way a man listened at a door he had been listening at since he was a boy, and the apartment did not, on the other side of the door, give back the sound it gave back when the apartment was full. There was no kettle. There was no scrape of a chair. There was no low murmur of his mother in the front room reading a thing aloud to his father at the table, or his father at the table answering the thing she read with the small unsurprised noise he made when she read him a thing he had already read.

There was the thin tick of the radiator on the wall under the window of the stairwell.

There was the door.

He pushed it open.

The apartment was the apartment in its empty hour. The light that came in at the front window was the colored grey of the late afternoon, the same grey that had been at the high window of the workshop all day, and it lay across the boards of the front room in the long flat way it lay when nothing in the room moved, and nothing in the room moved. His father's chair at the table sat pushed in. His mother's basket sat on the chair by the door, the small darning basket she had carried in and out of rooms his entire life, and the basket was closed, the way she closed it when she had finished a thing, and she had gone out of the apartment with the small clean having-finished she went out with when a thing had been finished.

A note on the table. Her hand. *Market with your father. Back by dark. Tea is in the tin.*

He read it twice.

He set it down where she had left it.

He stood at the threshold a count.

The kitchen was at the back of the apartment. The kitchen door was not closed. The kitchen door was not open, either. The kitchen door sat in the small not-open not-closed position it sat in when somebody had passed through it and had not, in the passing, bothered with it, and through the gap, in the colored grey of the afternoon, he heard, very faintly, the sound of water running into a pan.

He took off his coat.

He hung it on the peg by the door, the way he had hung it on the peg by the door for twenty-three years, and the inside pocket where Maren's note sat against the cheap paper book and the bench paper and Joren's letter went against the wood of the wall, and the outside pocket on the other side hung empty, and he stood at the peg a count with his hand on the wool of the sleeve.

Then he went down the hall.

\* \* \*

She was at the stove.

She was at the stove with her back to the door and a copper pan on the front ring and her hand at the tap, and the tap was running into the pan, and the pan was filling, and she had not heard him at the door because the tap was running, or she had heard him at the door and had not turned because she was not, in that count, ready to turn, and he stood at the doorway of the kitchen and watched her at the stove a count before she heard him.

She was thinner.

It was the first thing he saw. It was the thing the eye saw when the eye had been away a count and came back, the way a child's height was a thing you did not see if you saw the child every day and saw if you saw the child after a month, and Maren was thinner at the shoulders under the dark blue of the work coat, and at the wrist where the cuff of the work coat sat up over the bone, and the small change in the weight of her was the change of a person who had been forgetting the second meal a count of weeks running.

She turned the tap off.

She set the pan on the ring.

She did not, in the setting, channel.

He saw her hand at the small iron knob of the stove, and he saw her turn the knob, and he saw the small puff of the gas catch under the pan, and he saw her draw her hand back from the heat the way a person drew her hand back from a heat she had not, in years, had to draw her hand back from, and she stood at the stove a count and watched the small blue ring of flame settle under the copper, and then she turned.

"Ash."

"Hey."

"You're early."

"A little."

"Mm."

She wiped her hands on a cloth at her hip. She wiped them the small precise way she wiped a thing, the way her hands moved in the kitchen the way her hands moved at a bench, and she folded the cloth and she set it on the rail of the stove, and she looked at him.

"They went to market."

"I read the note."

"She'll be back by dark."

"Mm."

"You want tea."

"I want tea."

"Sit."

He sat.

The kitchen was the kitchen. The table at the wall was the table at the wall. The two chairs at the table were the two chairs at the table, the one with the small chip out of the back rail where he had put a chair leg through it at twelve, and the one without. The window over the small counter at the side was the window over the small counter, and through it, in the colored grey, the back of the building across the alley was the back of the building across the alley, with the run of laundry at the third floor and the closed shutter at the fourth, and the tile on the wall above the counter was the tile that had been there when their mother had been a girl in this kitchen. The tile had a small chip at the corner of one square. He had known the chip his entire life.

She got the tin down from the shelf.

She got the tin down by hand. She lifted it. She set it on the counter. She opened the lid. She took the small wooden scoop out of the tin and she scooped the leaf into the iron pot at the side of the stove, the way their mother scooped it, three flat scoops and a half, and she set the scoop back in the tin and she put the lid on and she put the tin back on the shelf.

The water in the pan was beginning to ring at the bottom.

She did not, watching it, do the thing she did. She did not put her palm at the side of the pan and pulse the heat the small economical way she pulsed it. She did not narrow at the ring and set the heat to the bottom. She watched the water at the bottom of the pan. She watched it ring, and start to lift, and she took the pan by the handle when it lifted, and she poured the water into the iron pot, and the leaf turned the water dark.

She set the pan back on the ring.

She turned the gas off.

She brought the pot to the table.

She brought two cups. She set one in front of him. She set the other in front of the chair across the table. She sat. She did not, sitting, look at him.

He did not, for a count, speak.

The pot sat on the iron tile at the middle of the table the way it had sat on the iron tile at the middle of the table when they were children, and the steam came off the spout in the small slow climb steam climbed in a kitchen with no draft, and the kitchen was quiet in the way a kitchen was quiet when the people in it were not, in the quiet, looking at one another.

He poured.

He poured hers first. He poured his second. He set the pot back.

She picked up her cup.

Her hand shook.

It was a small shake. It was the shake of a hand that had been steady all its life and was, in the late afternoon of a grey day in the autumn of the year, not steady, and the cup chimed, very faintly, against the saucer when she set it back down, and she heard the chime, and she did not look at him, and she set her hand flat on the table beside the cup the way a person set a hand flat on a table when the hand had betrayed her and the table was where you put a hand that had betrayed you.

He did not, for a count, look at the hand.

He looked at the cup.

He looked at the steam.

He drank a swallow.

The tea was strong. The tea was the tea their mother made, the same leaf, the same three scoops and a half, and the tea was hot, and the cup was warm in his hand, and across the table his sister sat with her hand flat on the wood beside a cup she had poured by hand from a pot she had filled at the tap from a pan she had heated on a ring.

"It's good," he said.

"Mm."

"Strong."

"It's her leaf."

"I know."

"Mm."

She did not pick the cup up again.

\* \* \*

The silence at the table was a silence with shape.

It was not the silence of two people who had nothing to say. It was the other silence, the silence with the furniture in it, the silence of a room in which a thing too large to walk around had been sitting between two people for so long that they had, both of them, learned the small habits of walking around it, the small turn at the hip when you came past the thing, the small tilt of the shoulder that kept you from grazing it, the small careful eye that did not, in the carefulness, ever quite land on it, and they sat at the table on either side of it and drank, and did not, for a count of breaths he did not measure, speak.

He looked at the tile with the chip.

He looked at the back of his sister's hand on the table, the flat of it against the wood, the small bone at the wrist where the cuff sat up.

He looked at the steam.

She picked up her cup.

She picked it up the second time the way a person picked up a cup the second time when the first time had gone the way it had gone, with her other hand against the side of the cup as well, both hands at the cup, the way a person held a cup when the person needed the cup to be steady more than the person needed to look as if the cup were steady, and she drank, and she set the cup down, with both hands, and she put both hands flat on the table either side of it, and she looked at the cup, and she said, in a voice he had not, in his life, heard her use:

"Was it like this?"

He did not, for a count, answer.

He did not pretend, for the count, that he did not know what she meant. The pretending was not a thing he had, at this table, on this afternoon, in his hands. The pretending had been in his hands his whole life and it was not, in this count, in his hands.

He set his cup down.

"Like what?"

She breathed in.

She breathed out.

"Like the ground is moving," she said. "Like the thing you built your life on is dissolving and you're supposed to keep standing."

"Yes."

The word was small. It came out small. It came out without any of the things he had, his whole life, put around words, the small lift at the end that turned a yes into a yes-of-course-it's-fine, the small follow-on clause that made the yes into a yes-but, the small breath of cheerfulness that took the weight off the word and put it somewhere it could not, in the having of it, be a weight. The word came out as a yes. It sat in the kitchen between them. It did not get up.

She did not look at him.

She looked at her cup.

Her hands, flat on the table, did not lift.

After a count she said, "All right."

He did not answer.

"All right," she said again, smaller.

The kitchen sat.

\* \* \*

She moved her hand.

She moved it the way a person moved a hand that had been sitting too long flat on a thing, the small unfolding of the fingers, the small turning of the palm, and she lifted her hand from the table and she put it in her lap, and her other hand came off the table after it the way the second of two birds came off a wire when the first had gone, and she sat at the table with her hands in her lap and the cup between her elbows and she looked at the cup.

"My pool," she said.

She stopped.

"I know," he said.

"How?"

"Mother."

"What did she say."

"Not much. She said you were tired. She said you were working too hard. She said the corps had given you a bigger run."

"They didn't."

"No."

"They gave me less."

"Mm."

"They gave me less in the spring. They gave me less in the summer. In the autumn the senior on my floor, who has been a kind man to me and who is not, by anything you would call evidence, a kind man, sat me down at the small table behind the dispatch and told me, in the small careful sentences a man like that uses when he is doing a kindness he does not, in himself, want to be doing, that they were taking the night runs off my list."

"Mm."

"He said it was for my health."

"Mm."

"He said it the way a man says a thing he has been told to say. He did not look at me when he said it. He looked at the small ledger on the table, and he turned the page back, and he showed me the run from the spring, and the run from the summer, and the run for the autumn that wasn't, in the autumn ledger, on the page anymore."

"Mm."

"I said all right."

"You would."

"Mm."

She looked at the cup.

"It's not that I can't channel," she said. "It's that I can't channel the way I could in the spring. And the way I could in the spring was the way I'd channeled all my life. And the small extra I had over the median, the small careful margin that meant I could take a run a man with a stronger pool couldn't take because I could draw the line clean and he couldn't, the small thing that was the thing, that thing is gone."

She turned her hand in her lap.

"I am, this autumn, an average channeler."

She said it flat.

"I have spent my life," she said, "being not an average channeler. I do not know how to be one."

He did not, for a count, speak.

He looked at her hand in her lap. The hand was the hand he had watched, his entire life, do the small precise pulses she did, the small economy he had watched at every kitchen table and every bench and every doorway he had stood with her at, and the hand sat in the lap of her work coat with the bone at the wrist a little sharper than it had been.

"It came on slow," she said. "I want to say that. It didn't come at me. It came at me the way a season comes at you. You don't notice the day the wind turns. You notice that you've been wearing the heavier coat a count of weeks and you can't, when you try to remember, remember the day you stopped wearing the lighter one."

"Yes."

"I noticed in the spring. I told myself it was the winter. I told myself I'd been overworking."

"Mm."

"In the summer I told myself it was the heat."

"Mm."

"In the autumn the senior on my floor sat me down at the small table behind the dispatch."

"Yes."

She looked at him.

She looked at him for the first time since he had sat down, and her eyes were dry, and they were not, in the dryness, calm, and she said, "I haven't told them."

"I know."

"I haven't told anyone."

"I know."

"I came home for a count of weeks because the apartment is the apartment and the kitchen is the kitchen and at the apartment I do not have to be the senior whose night runs have been taken off the list. I have, at the apartment, been making tea by hand because I do not, in the kitchen, want to feel my pool in my hands and feel that it is not what it was. I have been, at the apartment, behaving as if I were a person with no pool."

She breathed.

"I have been behaving as if I were you."

He set his cup down.

He did not, in the setting, do it carefully. The cup chimed, very faintly, against the saucer, the way her cup had chimed, and he heard the chime, and he did not lift his hand from the cup.

"Yes," he said.

\* \* \*

She looked at the table.

Her hand, in her lap, came up.

It came up and rested at the edge of the table the way a thing that had been deciding came to rest at the place it had been deciding to come to, and her fingers spread on the wood, and she looked at the back of her own hand, and she said, in a voice that was not the voice she had been using:

"I don't know how to do this."

He did not speak.

"You. You spent twenty-three years learning how to live without it. I don't know how. I never learned. I never had to learn. I was so busy being grateful I had it that I never imagined ..."

She did not finish.

She did not finish because the sentence did not, in the kitchen of their childhood, have a place to finish, and she sat at the table and looked at the back of her own hand on the wood, and she breathed, and she breathed again, and her shoulders, under the dark blue of the work coat, which had been holding the small held shape they had held since he had sat down, came down, a count of breaths, all the way down, the way a thing came down when the thing had been carried a long way and had been set, in a kitchen, at last, on a floor.

"I felt guilty every day," she said.

He went still.

"Every single day. I would wake up and channel the light on. I would wake up before the rest of the house and channel the light on at the small lamp by my desk and start the morning's work, and I would think, while I was doing it, while my hand was at the lamp and the pulse was going and the lamp was coming up, *he can't do this*. I would think it that clean. *My brother can't do this simple thing that I do without thinking.* And then I would work harder. I would work harder than the work needed me to work. I would be better at the work than the work needed me to be, because at least, if I earned it, if I was the best at what I could do, then maybe ..."

She stopped.

She stopped and her hand on the table was not steady, and she did not, in the stopping, lift it.

"... then maybe it wasn't just luck that I got it and you didn't."

The word *luck* sat in the kitchen.

He had heard the word in his life. He had said the word in his life. He had said it in the small careful way you said it about a thing it cost something to say, and he had heard it from his father and his mother and the woman at the corps office at twelve who had looked at the chart and at him and at the chart, and he had not, in the hearing, ever heard it said in the voice his sister had just said it in, which was the voice of a person who had been carrying the word in her mouth for twenty-three years and had not, in the carrying, ever set it down.

She set her hand flat on the table again.

She sat.

He looked at her.

He looked at her a count.

"I know," he said.

"What?"

"I know. I always knew."

She lifted her eyes.

"You never said anything."

"Neither did you."

\* \* \*

It was very quiet in the kitchen.

It was the kind of quiet a kitchen had after a thing had been said. It was not, in the quiet, the silence with the furniture in it. The furniture in the silence had been moved. The furniture had been moved, and the room had not, in the moving, become a different room, but a place in the room that had not been walked through in twenty-three years was, in the count, walked through, and the wall on the other side of the place was the wall, and the floor was the floor, and the place was a place, and they sat in the kitchen with the place open between them and they breathed, and neither of them, for the count, spoke.

He picked up his cup.

He picked it up because his hand was on it and because the cup was warm and because to not pick up the cup, in the count, would have been to make the not-picking-up a thing, and he did not, in the count, want to make a thing of any small thing, and he drank.

The tea was the tea.

She picked up her cup.

She picked it up with one hand. The shake was there, smaller. She drank. She set the cup down. She did not put her hand flat on the table after the setting. She set her hand around the cup. She kept it there.

"You were always cheerful," she said.

"Mm."

"You were the cheerful one. You were the one in the doorway. You were the one with the small thing to say at the table when the table was not, in itself, easy. You were the one who, when mother was tired, made the joke that lifted the room. You were the one I would tell, in school, about the small bad day at the bench, and you would do the small dry thing you did, and the day would be the day, and I would feel less tired."

"I tried."

"You tried."

"Mm."

"It cost you."

"Yes."

"I never asked."

"No."

"I'm asking."

He looked at her.

He breathed.

"It cost me," he said, "everything I had to give in a day, every day. The cheerful was not free. The cheerful was the rent. I was renting the room. I was renting the chair at the table. I was renting the small space in the family that a person who could not channel was, in our family, renting. I paid the rent in cheerful. I paid it because it was the only currency I had. And the rent was, in the years, due every morning."

She did not, for a count, answer.

"The jokes were apologies," he said. "I have not, until now, said this. I have not said it to Ryn. I have not said it to Haran. I have not said it. The jokes were apologies. The small things at the table were apologies. The being-easy-in-the-doorway was an apology. I was apologizing, every day of my life, for the chair I was sitting in."

She looked at him.

Her eyes were not dry.

"I'm sorry," she said.

"No."

"Yes."

"It wasn't yours."

"It was somebody's."

"Yes."

She breathed.

"I owe you an apology I cannot find the shape of," she said. "I have, this autumn, been looking for the shape of it, and I cannot find it, because the shape of it is the whole of my life, and you cannot apologize for the whole of a life."

"No."

"I'm sorry anyway."

"All right."

"All right."

\* \* \*

She drank.

She drank the small functional drink she drank when a drink was a thing you did to keep going, and she set the cup down, and she said, "I don't know what I was going to say. I had a thing I was going to say."

"You said it."

"Did I."

"Yes."

"Mm."

"You said the thing I never said."

"Which."

"That the cheerful was not free."

"You said that."

"Yes."

"All right."

She looked at her cup.

"The competence," she said, "was not free either."

He did not speak.

"I will not, at this table, perform it. I will, at this table, name it. I want to name it. The competence cost me ... I do not have the small clean sentence for it. I will not have, in this kitchen, the small clean sentence. I will have what I have."

"Have it."

She breathed.

"I did not, in my life, have a day in which I was permitted to fail. I did not, in my life, have a day in which I was permitted to be tired. I did not, in my life, have a day in which I was permitted to be small. The other children at the school had bad days. The other children at the corps had bad mornings. I did not have them. I did not have them because, if I had had them, the magic that I had been given would have been a thing I had been given that was, in the having, wasted, and the wasted was not, in our house, a thing a person could be."

"It was not."

"It was not in our house. It was not in me. I do not know, when I look at it, which one it was first."

"No."

"And the loneliness."

She stopped.

"The loneliness was the loneliness of being the easy child. The one who did not need. The one whose troubles were invisible, because I had, by the age of seven, taught everyone in the house, and after the house everyone in the school, and after the school everyone at the corps, not to see them. I taught them that. I trained them. And then I lived in the house I had trained them to make, which was a house in which no one, on any day, ever asked me how I was, because I had taught them that the asking was not, in my case, required."

"Maren."

"Mm."

"I asked you."

"You asked me cheerfully."

"Yes."

"It was the cheerful version of asking."

"Yes."

"Mm."

"I didn't know how to ask the other way."

"No."

"I am sorry."

"You are not the one. Stop."

"All right."

She looked at her cup.

She drank.

\* \* \*

He set his cup down.

He set it down and he put his hands on the table either side of it, and he looked at the tile with the chip, and he looked at the back of the building across the alley through the small window over the counter, and he looked at his sister's face across the table from him, and he said, quietly, the thing he had not, in his life, said.

"I hated you for it."

She did not, for a count, breathe.

"Not you," he said. "Not you. I want you to hear the word in the right place. I hated you for it. I hated the it. I hated that there was a thing in the world that divided us at birth and gave you the thing the world said was worth and gave me nothing and I hated the world for it and I hated the it for sitting between us and I hated, in the count of years, the small fact that you were the it's vessel and I was the not-it's vessel and we had not, either of us, asked to be either, and I loved you, and I hated the it, and I lived in the small impossible tension between the two for twenty-three years, and I have not, in my life, said it."

He stopped.

He looked at her.

"I'm saying it," he said. "I am saying it because we are saying it. I am saying it because if we are at this table we are at this table. I am not asking you to absolve me of it. I am not asking you for anything. I am putting it down on the table because if I do not put it down on the table now I will carry it for the rest of my life and you will carry the not-knowing-I-carried-it for the rest of yours and we will go on being these two children in this kitchen for the rest of the time we have, and I do not, in the count, want to."

She put her hand on the table.

She put it across the table. She did not put it on his hand. She put it on the wood, halfway across, the way a person put a hand halfway across a table when the hand was a hand that was offering itself to be reached for or not, as the reached-for-or-not was the thing the other person needed, and she looked at him and she said, "All right."

"All right?"

"All right."

"All right."

"You thought I would not, in the kitchen, say all right."

"I did not know what you would say."

"All right."

He looked at her hand on the wood.

He put his hand on the table.

He did not put it on her hand. He put it on the wood, halfway across, the way she had put hers, and the two hands sat halfway across the table, with the cups between them, and the steam off the pot.

After a count she moved her hand the small extra count of inches.

She put her hand on his.

It was not a holding. It was a setting. She set her hand on the back of his hand the way she had, all his life, set her hand on his collar, the small precise functional gesture that was, in her, the gesture love took, and she left it there, and she looked at the cup.

He did not move his hand.

\* \* \*

She cried first.

She cried the way she did everything, quietly, without the performance of crying, and the tears came down her face and she did not, in the coming, lift her hand from his to wipe at them, and they ran down to her jaw and stopped there and dried in the small tracks tears dried in on a face that had not, in the crying, moved, and she breathed, and she did not make a sound.

He cried after.

He cried after because he had been, in the count, holding it, and the holding had been the holding of a man who had been holding it for twenty-three years, and the holding broke at the small precise weight of his sister's hand on the back of his, and the weight was a weight he had not, in the holding, prepared for, because the holding had been prepared for the absence of the weight, and the weight, when it came, undid him in a single breath.

He did not, in the crying, make a sound either.

They sat at the table.

They sat at the table in the kitchen of their childhood and they grieved, and the grief was not for each other, because the each other was, in the kitchen, sitting across the table with a hand on the back of a hand, and the each other was, in the kitchen, the last thing the grief was for. The grief was for the children they had been. The grief was for the boy in the doorway with the joke ready and the rent due, and the girl at the small lamp at the desk before the rest of the house was up, channeling the light with the guilt at the back of the channeling, and the two of them, at seven and at five, dividing themselves into the complementary shapes the family they loved had needed them to be, because the family they loved had not, in itself, been able to be any other shape, and the dividing had been the small accidental honest thing two children had done to keep a house standing, and the house had stood.

The house had stood.

The children were not, after twenty-three years, children.

They sat at the table and the steam came off the pot in the small slow climb and the tile with the chip sat in its square on the wall and the back of the building across the alley sat in its grey, and after a count Maren said, "I'm going to need a cloth."

"Mm."

"For my face."

"Mm."

"Get me a cloth."

"Mm."

He got up.

He went to the rail of the stove and he took the cloth she had folded there at the start of the afternoon, and he came back, and he gave it to her, and she took it, and she wiped her face the small precise functional way she wiped a thing, and she folded the cloth, and she held it in her lap.

He sat.

He picked up his cup.

The tea was cooler.

He drank.

\* \* \*

Nothing in the world outside the kitchen was different.

The light at the back of the building across the alley was the light at the back of the building across the alley. The shutter at the fourth floor was still closed. The run of laundry at the third was still running. The grey at the small window over the counter was the grey of an afternoon in late autumn that had not, in the hours of its day, committed to weather, and the radiator in the front room ticked the small thin tick it ticked, and the tin on the shelf was the tin, and the tile with the chip was the tile with the chip.

Her pool was still fading.

His reservoir was still finite.

The decline at the rural network and the eleven districts on the broadsheet's list and the man in the dark coat the color of wet stone walking out through a gate without looking back and the seven motions and the thing the doing had not been, all of it, sat outside the kitchen in the city the kitchen sat in, and would, when he went out of the apartment, be there.

The kitchen, on the inside, was different.

He sat at the table and he could not, looking at the kitchen, say what was different. The chairs were the chairs. The table was the table. The cups on the table were the cups. The pot on the iron tile was the pot. The window over the counter was the window over the counter and the tile with the chip was the tile.

The thing that was different was a thing not in the kitchen.

It was the thing that had been in the kitchen.

The thing that had been in the kitchen for twenty-three years was, in the count, not in the kitchen, and the not-being of it was a space, and the space was the difference, and it sat between them at the table and it was not, in itself, a presence, but the absence had a shape, and the shape was the shape of the room with the furniture moved.

He breathed.

His breath went down further than he had, in his life, sent it.

He had not noticed, in the years, that he had not been sending it that far.

They sat for a long count without speaking.

After a long count Maren said, "She'll be back soon."
