# Chapter 5

Haran moved in on a Tuesday morning, with a handcart and no announcement.

Ash came down the workshop stairs at the western edge of Stonecurve and found the old man unloading a wooden crate of files onto the swept-clean floor of what had been, three weeks ago, an abandoned ground-floor grain store. The doors stood open. The smell of old chaff still hung under the rafters, mixed now with the iron smell of Haran's tools. A bench had been hauled in from somewhere, set against the long wall. A vise was bolted at one end. A second crate sat beside the first, lid pried, the dull glint of calipers and hand-drills laid out in the foam Haran cut himself, every tool in its slot.

"You're moving in," Ash said.

"I'm here." Haran lifted a coil of brass wire from the crate, hung it on a peg above the bench. "Same thing."

"Permanently."

"For now."

He didn't look up. The peg was a finger's width too low for the coil to clear the bench; Haran considered it, took a small mallet from the bench, and tapped the peg out and in again two centimeters higher. The coil swung clear. He moved to the next tool.

Ash watched him for a while. The morning light came in low through the open doors and lit the dust hanging above the floor, and Haran's hands moved through the dust without disturbing it, the way they always moved. Tool. Slot. Peg. Tap. Tool. He had been waiting decades for this room. He didn't say so. He didn't need to.

"Where are you sleeping?" Ash asked.

"Upstairs." Haran nodded at the ceiling. "Two rooms. Felden found them."

"You told my mother?"

"I told Felden. Felden tells whoever needs telling."

A pause. Haran lifted a long brass rule, sighted along it, set it on the bench.

"So." He looked up for the first time. "What's broken today."

* * *

The water system worked.

That was the strange thing, the thing Ash kept rediscovering each morning, the thing he wanted to set down and pick up again the way a child picks up a stone to feel its weight: the system worked. Three buildings on the south face of the square, all served from the rooftop cistern by a single gravity line that branched twice and ended at three street-level taps. The pump on the roof filled the cistern once a day. The cistern fed the taps for the rest of it. No channeling. No pool. No mid-afternoon failure when the assigned channelers thinned out.

The first week, the line ran dry twice. Once because nobody had cranked the pump. Once because somebody had cranked it but the cistern's overflow valve had stuck and the water had bypassed the storage entirely and run down the back wall into the alley. Haran fixed the valve in seven minutes with a file and a thumbnail's worth of grease. Nobody had cranked the pump because nobody had agreed whose job it was.

That was the second problem.

They held a meeting in the square on a cold morning, Ash and Haran and Corren and maybe twenty residents, and Corren ran it because Ash had learned by then that when Ash ran a meeting people waited for him to tell them what to do. Corren made them argue. They argued for an hour. They came out with a roster: twelve people, two-hour shifts, the crank manned from sixth bell to fourteenth.

The roster broke in three days.

It got rebuilt by a girl.

Her name was Iri. She was thirteen, maybe fourteen, narrow-shouldered, with hair cut short the way the apprentice channelers wore theirs, except she wasn't an apprentice channeler because her pool was nothing, smaller than her father's and her father's was already small. She showed up on the morning of the fourth day and asked Corren if she could take a shift. Corren said sure. She took the shift. She took the next one too. By the end of the first week she'd taken eleven shifts and the roster was a polite fiction draped over the fact that Iri ran the pump.

Ash came down to the square one afternoon and watched her work.

She had her sleeves rolled. Her stance was wide, knees soft. The crank was a heavy iron arm a meter long, and the early operators had wrestled it, hauling on the down-stroke and lifting hard on the up. Iri didn't haul. She'd found the rhythm where the crank's own weight carried it through three-quarters of the rotation and her arms only had to add the missing quarter. Her breath came in even fours. The crank turned. The pipe hummed. The cistern filled.

He'd seen channelers move like that. The good ones. The ones who'd stopped fighting the pool and started riding it.

"How long does it take you?" he asked, when she stopped to drink from a clay cup someone had set on the parapet.

"Twenty-three minutes."

"That's fast."

"I know." She didn't smile. She wiped her mouth on the back of her hand. "Old man Cavren takes forty-eight."

"Cavren's seventy."

"I know that too." Now she smiled, a small private flicker. "I'm not bragging. I just timed it."

She went back to the crank. Ash watched a moment longer. The down-stroke. The lift. The down-stroke. The lift. Water moving up through copper because a girl with no pool had decided this was her job.

He went back to work.

* * *

Heating was harder.

Heating was a different animal entirely, and he understood that the first week and underestimated it anyway. Water had one problem: get it from up there to down here. Heating had a thousand. Air leaked. Stone wicked cold. Window frames had been built sixty years ago to fit a city that channeled warmth into every room every morning, which meant nobody had bothered to make them seal. You could put your palm flat on a closed window in any of these buildings and feel the draft come through the joinery like the window wasn't there.

Haran's solution was three things at once.

Compressed fiber, packed tight into wall cavities, cut to fit by hand. Mechanical window frames with copper weather-stripping and a brass cam that drew the sash hard against the seal when you closed it. Small stoves in iron, squat, ugly, efficient, burning the processed brick-fuel that Haran had sourced through a contact in The Splits whose name he wouldn't say out loud.

All three required craftsmanship. The fiber needed to be packed at a specific density, not so loose it settled, not so tight it bridged. The window frames needed cuts true to a quarter-millimeter or the cam wouldn't draw. The stoves needed flue installation, and the flues ran through old chimneys that the earthquake had cracked from cap to throat.

The district had craftsmen. The district had carpenters and stonemasons and a tinsmith. They were skilled in their own work and had no idea how to do this work, because this work didn't exist yet in this city. There was nothing to compare it to. Nothing to copy.

Ash channeled the difficult parts.

He stood in a room on the third floor of the building on the square's north corner, where a young couple lived with two children and a mother-in-law, and he laid his palms against the inside wall and pushed reservoir into the compressed fiber until it bonded to the lath behind it. The fiber wanted to settle. He didn't let it. He warmed it just enough to soften the binder and pressed it into the cavity with the long flat board Haran had cut, and the fiber set, and the wall was full, and the wall would stay full. Two percent. Maybe two and a half.

Window frame next. He fused the brass weather-stripping into the wood at a temperature that would have warped the frame if he'd held it three seconds longer. Held it three seconds less. Pulled away. The frame closed; the cam drew; the seal whispered shut. The draft died.

Chimney flue. He climbed to the roof and reinforced the cracked masonry from the outside, the same threading technique he'd used on the cistern, energy laid along each fault. Half a percent. He came back down and the family's grandmother stood in the doorway with her shawl pulled close and watched him and said nothing.

He walked through the building when he was done. The stairwell was still cold; they hadn't reached the stairwell yet. The hallway was warmer than it should have been at this hour, with no one channeling. He put his palm on the wall outside the family's door.

Warm.

Not the channeled warmth he knew, not the pulse that lived under a heated wall like a small contained sun. A different warmth. The warmth of insulation, which wasn't really warmth at all, which was just the absence of cold leaving. The wall was holding what the family had made themselves. The stove inside was burning. The fiber in the cavity was keeping the heat in. The window was sealed. The flue was drawing.

Something he'd built, that didn't need him.

He stood there with his hand on the wall a long time.

* * *

He kept the count.

It lived in his head the way Dorenne had taught him to keep counts, a running ledger that updated whenever he drew. Cistern reinforcement at the Bren building: one and a quarter. Window frames on the north corner: three across two days. Flue on the same building: a half. Insulation at the cooper's house: two. He totaled it on the walk home each night and the totals hovered around 1 to 2 percent in any given week, and the reservoir behind his ribs kept its slow even ebb.

Forty-one. Then forty and a half. Then forty.

He could feel it. Not as a number, but as a wall that had moved a finger's width closer to him since last week. A boundary that had been theoretical, in the days when he was channeling boulders out of streets and lifting transit cars off children, and was now no longer theoretical. He could reach the wall. He hadn't reached it yet. But he knew where it was.

One percent. Two percent. The math sat behind his eyes when he closed them at night.

One district.

The city had thirty-seven.

He didn't say it to Haran. He didn't say it to Corren. He didn't say it to himself, in any deliberate way, because the moment he said it the work for tomorrow would feel different, and he needed the work for tomorrow to feel possible.

He let it sit. A shadow on a wall he wasn't yet ready to turn and look at.

* * *

Haran taught.

He taught the way he repaired things, which was the way he did most things: by doing them in front of you, slowly enough that you could see, and then handing you the tool and watching what you did with it. He didn't lecture. He asked questions. He made you find the answer. If you found the wrong one, he let you find out it was wrong by watching it not work, and then he asked another question.

A few of them caught.

Corren caught. Ash had expected this; Corren had been a crew lead for six years and had the construction-mind, the sense of how loads transferred, where weight wanted to go. He took to Haran's mechanical thinking the way a swimmer takes to a river. By the third week he was diagnosing pump problems before Haran had to point them out.

A man named Tovin caught. Forty, scarred along one forearm from a kiln accident, formerly a fuel-handler in the southern works. Quiet. Slow with words. His hands knew metal in a way that surprised Ash the first time he watched him work; you handed Tovin a brass cam and he turned it once in his palm and knew where it wanted to wear.

Iri caught, in her own way, though she'd appointed herself to the pump and refused most other lessons; what she did with the crank she did by feel and she didn't want to be told why it worked.

Most of the others were adequate. Tesha worked steadily and got steadily better. Two of the new arrivals from the third week, brothers, learned to install weather-stripping correctly after four tries each and then could do it forever. Dael, who'd been a skeptic at the start, had quieted into a kind of grudging competence. He installed flues. He didn't enjoy installing flues. But he installed them, and they drew.

A few never took.

Haran didn't push them. There was a man named Pell who tried for two weeks to learn pipe-fitting and produced joints that leaked every time, and one afternoon Haran took the wrench from his hand and said, "Try the carting." Pell nodded. Pell now hauled fiber from the supply yard to the buildings, a job he was good at, and the leaks stopped happening. Nobody mentioned the reassignment. Pell's pride sat where it was.

"You can't train everyone," Haran said to Ash one evening, when they were closing up the workshop at the end of a long day. "You train enough. The enough train more."

"That's the theory."

"That's the only theory there is." Haran set the last file in its slot. Closed the lid. "You teach ten. Two of those ten teach ten. You're not building workers. You're building teachers."

"And the ones who never take?"

"They cart fiber. They cook. They watch the children of the ones who teach." He looked at Ash. "It isn't a failure. It's a district."

Ash thought about that on the walk home.

* * *

The valve broke on a Thursday.

Ash was on the roof when Corren came up the stairs two at a time, breathing hard, and said the south-line junction had blown a seal and water was running into the alley behind the cooper's. They went down. The pipe was warm to the touch where the seal had given and the joint had separated by a hair, enough to spit water in a fine mist that had been spitting for an hour before anyone noticed because the alley was narrow and nobody walked through it.

"Shut the cistern," Haran said. He was already there. He must have come up from the workshop the moment he'd heard. "Shut it now."

Ash shut it. Ran up to the roof, closed the cistern's outlet valve, ran back down. The mist died to a drip. The drip died to nothing.

Haran was crouched by the joint with his hand on the pipe.

He didn't move for a while. His eyes had the slight unfocus Ash had learned to read, the inward look of a channeler reading structure. His hand rested on the brass collar where the seal had failed. His other hand held a small file. He was looking, not at the pipe, but through it.

Then he moved.

His left hand stayed flat on the collar. His right took the file and worked the inside of the seal-housing, three short strokes, then a fourth at a different angle, then a pause. He set the file down. He took a small twist of fiber-packing from his belt pouch, threaded it into the housing with his thumb. Pressed.

His left hand pulsed.

That was the only word for it. Ash watched. He'd been trying to watch for weeks now, trying to see the channeling itself the way Haran saw structure, and he'd been failing because there was almost nothing to see. This time he was close enough and watching hard enough and he caught it: a flicker, a breath, a thing so small that if he hadn't known to look for it he'd have missed it entirely. The brass under Haran's left hand warmed by a fraction. The fiber-packing seated. The seal closed.

That was the channeling.

That was all of it.

Ash kept his face still. Inside his ribs the reservoir did the small involuntary thing it did when he saw something that pulled his attention, the slight forward-lean that was almost a question.

What had Haran spent.

He couldn't measure another channeler's draw, not from outside, not exactly. But he had a sense of it now, after months of tracking his own. He knew what one percent felt like coming out of him. He knew what a tenth of one percent felt like. And what Haran had just done, in that little pulse that had set the fiber and warmed the brass to grip, had been smaller than that. Smaller than a tenth. A whisper. A breath against a candle. The repair was permanent. The cost had been almost nothing.

Ash had sealed the cistern with a one-and-a-quarter-percent draw. Haran had sealed this seal with what, a hundredth? A two-hundredth?

He filed it away. He didn't have a frame for it yet. Not technique, exactly; he'd been studying Haran's technique for months and he had pieces of it, and his own work was getting cleaner. This wasn't technique. This was something underneath technique. Something about the relation between intent and expenditure that Haran had figured out and Ash hadn't, that maybe Ash didn't even have the right word for yet.

"Done," Haran said.

He stood. Brushed his hands on his trousers. Stepped back.

"Test it," he said.

Ash went up to the roof and opened the cistern valve. Water moved through the pipe. Ash came back down. The seal held. The alley stayed dry.

"What did it?" Ash asked.

"Burr in the housing. Original machining. Should have been filed at the foundry." Haran rolled the file in his fingers, dropped it back in his pouch. "Not your fault. Not anyone's. It was always going to fail."

"And now?"

"Now it won't."

Corren had gone to find Tovin. He came back with him five minutes later, and Haran walked Tovin through the repair from the start. Tovin watched. Haran took the seal apart again. Tovin reassembled it.

It took an hour.

The first time Tovin's seal didn't seat right. He took it apart, did it again. The second time it seated but the file-work on the housing was uneven and the joint sat a half-degree off true. He took it apart, did it again. The third time it was rough but it held.

"Works," Haran said.

Tovin looked at the joint. Looked at his own hands. He didn't say anything but his shoulders had moved, fractionally, the way shoulders move when something heavy comes off them.

"Works," he said.

Ash watched both of them. Haran's seven-minute repair. Tovin's hour. The same outcome, more or less, in the way that mattered to the alley behind the cooper's. Different outcomes entirely in the way that mattered to the city of thirty-seven districts. Haran had decades. Tovin had three weeks. The gap between them was a gap that no amount of teaching would close in any timeline that helped.

He thought: *enough. Enough is all you get.*

He thought: *Haran's right.*

He thought, underneath both of those, the thing he'd been not-thinking for weeks: *Haran's whisper. My shout. There's something there.*

He filed it away again. The shape of it sat in him the way a stone sits in a pocket. He'd come back to it.

* * *

The liaison came on a clear afternoon in the fifth week.

He arrived at the workshop a little after noon. Mid-thirties, neat, his Infrastructure-office coat brushed clean, a leather satchel under one arm and a pencil already in his hand. He introduced himself as Ennis Korr, junior administrator under Director Vaska. Ash had never heard of Vaska. He'd heard of the office. He understood the chain.

"Director Vaska reports to Senior Director Talven, who reports to Dorenne," Korr said, anticipating the question Ash hadn't asked. "I'm here at her instruction. To observe and document."

"Tour, then."

"Please."

Ash took him through the district.

He did it the way Corren ran meetings, which was to say he tried not to do too much of it himself. He showed Korr the workshop. He introduced him to Haran, who said "Korr" in greeting and went back to a piece of brass-work without further comment. He walked him to the square and showed him the pump, where Iri was cranking out the day's fill. Korr watched her for a full minute, which surprised Ash, and made a note.

"How old is she?"

"Fourteen, I think."

"Trained by?"

"Herself."

Korr made another note.

They walked the south line. Korr asked about pipe diameter, about pressure tolerance, about the maintenance schedule. He asked who was on the maintenance schedule. Ash named the people. Korr wrote them all down. He asked where the spares came from. Ash said The Splits, and Korr's pencil paused, and resumed.

They walked the north corner. Korr put his palm on the wall outside the family's door, the way Ash had, the first time. He held it there. He took it away. He wrote something down.

"This is warm."

"Insulation. Stove inside."

"What's the stove burning?"

"Processed brick-fuel. Sourced through Haran's contacts."

"Volume?"

"Per household, maybe two kilos a day in this weather. We're working on a cooperative supply line."

Korr wrote.

They went up to the roof. Korr looked at the cistern. He looked at the pump. He looked out over the district, the way Ash had looked out over it on the first morning, before anything had been built. Smoke rose from the stoves now, in five or six places, where there had been no smoke before. The square below was quiet. Iri had finished her shift. Cavren had taken over. The crank turned at its slower rhythm.

"It works," Korr said.

"It works."

"Director Vaska will want to know the reservoir cost."

"One to two percent per week of construction. Maintenance is negligible. Mostly Haran's."

"Sustainable."

"For one district."

"How many in the city?"

"Thirty-seven."

Korr's pencil paused again. He didn't write that down. He didn't need to. He looked at Ash, and his face had the careful neutrality of an administrator who had been trained, at length, not to let his face do anything that wasn't authorized.

"And the next district." He said it carefully. "When it's time to build the next one. What happens then."

"We build it."

"You build it."

"I help. The people there install most of it. With training. The way it happened here."

A pause. Korr looked down at his notebook. Tapped the pencil twice against the page.

"And when the Wellspring isn't here." His voice had stayed neutral. "When you're not the one installing the next system. When you're, for any reason, not available. What happens then."

Ash felt the question land.

It landed the way a stone lands in still water. Not loud. Not dramatic. A small clean drop, and then the rings going out from it, slow and wide, touching everything.

"The people here install it," he said.

Korr nodded.

He nodded the way administrators nod when they've received an answer and don't intend to dispute it and also don't intend to believe it. His face stayed neutral. His pencil moved across the page in small even strokes, recording. He didn't look up.

When he did look up, what was in his face wasn't doubt, exactly. It was a gentler thing than doubt. It was the look of a man who'd toured a lot of pilot programs in his career, who had written a lot of optimistic reports, who had watched a lot of hopeful answers age into the more complicated answers that came after. He had nothing against the answer Ash had given. He'd just heard a lot of answers like it.

*They're not ready for that.*

He didn't say it. He didn't need to.

"Thank you," Korr said. "I'll have a report to Director Vaska by week's end. She'll forward to Senior Director Talven. Any follow-up questions will come through me."

"Tell Dorenne it works."

"I'll tell Director Talven, who will tell Dorenne." A small precise smile. "But yes. The substance of the message will be that it works."

He closed his notebook. He shook Ash's hand. The handshake was firm, brief, professional. He went back down the stairs.

Ash stayed on the roof a while longer.

Smoke rose from the stoves. Iri's replacement worked the crank, slower, but working. The cistern filled. The system did what it did, with him standing there, with him not standing there, with him gone tomorrow or in a year or in ten years. That was the design. That was the whole point of it. *Something he'd built that didn't need him.* That was the line he'd been carrying in his head for weeks and it was true.

It was true.

The wind moved across the roof. He turned and went down.

* * *

He worked late.

The cooper's house had a stretch of north wall that needed insulation and the family had timed it for evening because the grandfather slept during the day. Ash channeled. The reservoir gave. He set the fiber, fused the lath, sealed the small cracks where the wall met the floor. The grandfather slept. The grandmother thanked him quietly and gave him a piece of bread. He ate it walking out.

It was past tenth bell when he came down the stairs of the cooper's into the alley.

Haran was on the workshop step. The lamp inside was lit. The door was open behind him, light spilling out across the cobbles. He had a cup of something hot in his hands and another beside him on the step.

"Sit," he said.

Ash sat.

The cup was tea. Bitter, strong, the leaf Haran kept in a tin he wouldn't share the source of. Ash drank.

"Korr came," Haran said.

"He did."

"He asked the question."

"He did."

Haran nodded slowly. He didn't drink. He held the cup the way he held tools, loose, ready, his fingers settled into a grip that had taken decades to learn.

"What did you tell him."

"That the people here install the next one."

A long pause. The street was quiet. Somewhere a dog barked once and stopped.

"Do they," Haran said.

It wasn't a challenge. It was a question. Ash sat with it.

"Some of them could," he said, eventually. "Corren. Tovin. In a year, maybe. Not yet."

"In a year."

"Maybe two."

Haran drank. The cup steamed a little against his face.

"How many districts in two years," he said.

"At one to two percent a week." Ash did the math he'd been not-doing. "If the reservoir holds. If I keep this rate. Maybe. Two more."

"Two more."

"Maybe three."

"Out of."

"Thirty-seven."

Haran nodded. He didn't say anything for a while. He drank his tea. Ash drank his.

"So," Haran said.

The word sat in the air between them. The way it always did. The pause after it as long as it needed to be.

"So we keep going," he said.

"That's it?"

"That's it." Haran turned his head to look at him. The lamplight caught the side of his face, the lines of it, the eyes that had watched a city decline for sixty years and had not stopped working through any of it. "You think I didn't know the math. I knew the math when I drew the first plan, forty years ago. The math hasn't been the question."

"Then what is."

"Whether anything works at all." Haran looked back out at the street. "Whether one district is possible. We didn't know that until last month. Now we know."

"And the other thirty-six."

"Are not your problem tonight."

He stood. Slow, careful, the knee that had been bothering him all winter taking the weight last. He picked up his empty cup.

"Tomorrow," he said. "The east line needs a third tap."

"I'll be here."

"I know."

He went inside. The lamp moved with him, deeper into the workshop, the light pulling in toward the far wall and leaving the doorway dim. Ash sat on the step a while longer with the bitter tea cooling in his hands.

The reservoir sat behind his ribs at forty percent. He could feel the wall of it now without reaching, the way you can feel a room's far corner with your eyes closed. Forty. Down from forty-two five weeks ago. The math was the math. Two more districts, maybe three, before the wall got close.

Korr's face. The neutral pencil moving across the page. The look that wasn't doubt and was the thing that lived next to doubt, the thing that had toured a lot of pilot programs.

*The people here install it.*

He'd said it cleanly. He'd meant it.

He hadn't tested it.

A girl named Iri was the fastest pump operator in the district because she'd decided to be. A man named Tovin had repaired a seal in an hour because Haran had shown him once. Corren ran meetings now without being asked. These were facts. These had happened. He had watched them happen and they hadn't required him; Iri had appointed herself, Tovin had earned it, Corren had been Corren.

But the pump had been built, and the pipe had been fused, and the seals had been forged, and the insulation had been bonded, by him. By a draw from a pool that nobody else had. The work that didn't need him had been built by him. The maintenance might hold without him. The construction wouldn't.

He set the cup down on the step beside him.

The street was quiet. The smoke from one of the stoves on the square drifted overhead, thin and pale against the night sky, going somewhere he couldn't see. Inside the workshop Haran was moving slowly, putting things away, the small clean sounds of a man closing his day.

Ash stood.

He looked once across the district, at the buildings he could see from this angle, at the windows with their new mechanical frames, at the chimneys with their reinforced flues, at the dark line of the south pipe running along the wall where moonlight caught it. All of it built. All of it working. All of it sitting on the answer he had given Korr and not yet earned.

He went home in the dark. The reservoir lighter than it had been. The wall a finger's width closer than a month ago. The work for tomorrow already arranging itself in his head, the way work did now, the way it would tomorrow and the day after.

Forty percent.

A start.

But only a start.
