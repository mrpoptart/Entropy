# Chapter 19

The broadsheet was on the table when Ash came in, folded once down the middle, the ink still smelling faintly of the press. Haran had set it under the pepper jar to keep it from curling, the way he set anything he meant for Ash to read without saying read this.

Ash hung his coat. He poured the tea that had been left for him. He sat down with the cup in both hands and let the warmth go through the porcelain into his fingers before he reached for the paper.

"Page two," Haran said, without looking up from the small clock he was, for the third week running, refusing to give up on. "Bottom column."

"Mm."

"Don't read the editorial. The editorial is a man yelling at a wall. Read the column."

The column was a list. Ash had read enough Leveler broadsheets by now to know the shape of a list when it ran in the bottom corner of page two, the small dense print of names and numbers. This one was contributions, district by district, the autumn tally against the spring. Spring on the left. Autumn on the right. The arrow between them, when the printer had bothered with an arrow, pointed the wrong way every time.

Ash ran his finger down the column. Anside, down. Wells Lane, down. The mill ward, down. The Crescent, which had been the Levelers' showpiece in the spring, the grid they brought reporters to see, down by nearly a fifth.

"They did the math honest," he said.

"They did the math honest." Haran set the small screwdriver against the rim of the clock and turned it a quarter turn and listened, and turned it back. "That's what's killing them. A dishonest movement could have buried this. They printed it on page two."

"Page two is a kind of burying."

"Page two is the most a proud man can manage." Haran set the screwdriver down. He looked at the clock the way a man looked at a thing that had defeated him for the morning, and he picked up his cup, and he drank. "There's a meeting at the Founders' Hall tonight. There won't be a meeting at the Founders' Hall by the end of the week. Whatever comes out of it, three things come out of it. Mark my words."

"Three."

"The ones who want more. The ones who want less. And the ones who want to go home."

Ash folded the broadsheet back along its crease and slid it under the pepper jar where Haran had had it. He drank his tea. Outside, the lane was already the color of late autumn, that flat grey light that made the cobbles look wet whether they were or not, and a cart was going by with the slow deliberate sound of a wheel that had a flat spot on its rim and a driver who had stopped caring.

"You going down there?" Haran said.

"To the hall?"

"To the hall."

"No."

Haran nodded. He did not say good and he did not say why not. He picked the screwdriver up again and bent over the clock, and his shoulder, where it caught the lamp, had the small comfortable hunch of a man who had decided some time ago that other people's quarrels were other people's.

"Tesha said come by when you've a minute," he said, after a while.

"Mm."

"She's got two new faces this morning. Wanted you to know before you walked in on it."

"What kind of faces."

Haran looked up over the top of his glasses. He had a way of looking over the top of his glasses that meant he was deciding how much of a thing to tell, and how much to let a man find out for himself. He decided, this morning, on a small portion.

"Leveler faces," he said. "The good kind."

* * *

The proof district at this hour was a working district. The pump in Building Six was still where Tesha had moved it, which is to say at the head of the slope and not the foot, and the woman who had carried twelve buckets a day in September was carrying none, because the water came down to her now instead of up to her, and that was the whole of it. Ash had stopped marvelling at it. Tesha had never marvelled at it. It was the kind of fact that, once seen, refused to be impressive.

He came up the lane with his hands in his pockets and the small paper book in his coat, and he stopped at the doorway of the workshop and stood there a moment before he went in, because the room had voices in it that were not, as they had been a month ago, only Tesha's voice and Pell's voice and the careful slow voice of Mira asking what a brass fitting was for.

"Ash," Tesha said, without turning. She knew his step too. Half the south block had begun to know his step, which was a thing that ought to have pleased him and that he had not yet, this autumn, found a way of being pleased about. "Come here. Tell this man he's wrong."

"I haven't been told what he said yet."

"He said you can't run a maintenance roster without a charter. Tell him."

The man at the bench looked up. He was perhaps forty, perhaps a little less, and he had the long bony hands of a man who did fine work with them, and he had the close-cropped beard the Leveler organizers wore in the southern wards, more habit by now than statement. He had a pencil behind his ear and a printed sheet folded in front of him, and the printed sheet had the small block-letter heading of the Levelers' contribution office, and someone, recently, had crossed the heading out with a single line of pencil and written above it, in a careful hand, *south block, week of.*

"I'm Joren," he said. He put his hand out. "I've been at Wells Lane four years. They sent me, well, they didn't send me. I came."

"Ash."

"I know who you are."

"Most people do, lately."

"Mm." Joren's mouth went up at one corner, the small dry smile of a man who had spent four years in rooms where being known was not the asset it sounded like. "Tesha says you don't run a charter."

"We don't run a charter."

"How do people know whose week it is."

"Tesha tells them."

Joren looked at Tesha. Tesha was bent over the bench again, doing a thing to a valve that Ash could not, from where he stood, quite see, and she had the steady unhurried movement of a person who had stopped having to think about her hands an hour into the morning. She did not look up.

"Tesha can't tell them forever," Joren said.

"No," Ash agreed.

"That's what I'm saying. That's what a charter is. It's the not-Tesha. It's the thing that runs when she's got the flu."

Tesha did look up then. She looked at Joren the way she had, a month ago, looked at Ash the morning he came down without the rolls of paper, the steady evaluating look of a woman who was, on balance, willing to be persuaded but not in any hurry to perform the persuading.

"Show me," she said.

"Show you the charter?"

"Show me the not-Tesha. On a piece of paper. With the names and the weeks. Then I'll tell you whether we want it."

"Fair."

"And don't print it. Write it. Printing is what we don't want. Printing is what makes a thing a rule."

Joren took the pencil from behind his ear. He sat down at the corner of the bench where the light from the high window came down in a soft column across the wood, and he turned the printed sheet over to its blank side, and he began to draw, in a slow careful hand, the small grid of weeks and names that, in another part of the city, in a building Ash had never been inside, had once been the contribution roster for the Crescent. He drew it without looking up. He had drawn it many times.

Ash watched him for a moment. Then he went over to the bench where Mira was, this morning, taking apart a pressure gauge with the patient tongue-out concentration of a person twelve years old who had decided a thing was within her reach, and he sat down on the stool beside her, and he did not, for a while, say anything.

"That's the Bourdon tube," Mira said, after some minutes.

"Yes it is."

"Tesha said you wouldn't know its name."

"Tesha is right that I learned its name about six weeks ago."

"Why is it called that."

"A man named Bourdon."

"Is he dead."

"I expect he is."

"Mm."

She went back to the gauge. Ash sat on the stool and let his eyes go around the room. There was Joren at the bench with the pencil. There was the second face Haran had warned him of, a woman in her fifties at the back, in the long grey coat the Levelers had worn at the Crescent rallies in the spring, and she was sitting on an upturned crate with two of the Building Four women either side of her, and the three of them had a piece of paper between them and the paper was, from the way they were leaning, a list of households. The Leveler woman had a finger under one of the names and was saying something, low, to the woman on her left, and the woman on her left was nodding the slow nod of a person who was not, this morning, surprised to find that she had been thought of.

It was the rosters. Ash understood it, sitting on the stool. The Levelers had been keeping rosters of households for years, who needed what when, who could carry and who could not, who lived alone and who did not. They had built a whole movement on the back of those rosters. And in the proof district, until this morning, the rosters had been Tesha and the half-dozen women who had begun to organize around her and a piece of paper Pell kept in his coat pocket, and the seams between them had shown.

The Leveler woman had brought the rosters. She had not announced them. She had sat down on a crate and opened the paper and started naming names, and three women in the room had recognized the shape of what she was doing, and that was the whole of it.

He did not say anything. He sat on the stool beside Mira and watched the Bourdon tube come apart in her hands, and after a while he took the paper book out of his coat and wrote, in the small careful hand he wrote in lately, *Joren. roster. not from me. not from Tesha. from him.*

He underlined *not from me* once, and then, after a moment, underlined it again.

* * *

He stayed an hour and went out the back way, because the front of the workshop had two more people in it by the time he stood up, and one of them had the look of someone who wanted to be introduced, and he had decided some weeks ago that being introduced was a thing he was going to do less of.

The lane behind the workshop ran along a wall and came out at the small square where the second heating grid was. The grid had been a pure Leveler grid in the spring. It had been the kind they had brought reporters to. There were, Ash counted as he came up to it, seven people working on it at the moment, and four of them were in Leveler grey and three of them were in the patched canvas the south block women wore, and they were doing two different things at once that had, this morning, become one thing.

The Levelers were going household to household with a clipboard, asking who had contributed and who had not and who could not. The south block women were going valve to valve with a wrench and a cloth, doing the small unromantic work of keeping the mechanical side from freezing, because the autumn had begun to bite at night and the valves at the corner of Wells Lane had gone in October. The two groups passed each other in the square without remarking on each other. They were not, yet, working together. They were working in the same place, on the same grid, for the same households, and that was, this morning, sufficient.

Ash stopped at the edge of the square and watched. He stood with his hands in his pockets and the small paper book in his coat, and he did not, for a while, do anything but watch.

He had thought, when he had thought about this at all, that the merger would come at a meeting. That somebody would stand up at a meeting and say a word, and the word would mean something, and the broadsheets would print the word the next morning. He saw, standing at the edge of the square, that he had been wrong about that in the way a man was usually wrong about a thing, which was thoroughly. The meeting at the Founders' Hall would happen tonight and three factions would come out of it and the broadsheets would print the three factions in the morning, and none of that would be the merger. The merger had been happening, here, in the square, for two weeks, and nobody in the square had noticed it was happening because, for the people in the square, it was simply Tuesday.

He took the paper book out. He did not write anything. He held it open against his palm and stood there with the pencil in his other hand, and after a minute he put it away again, because the thing he had wanted to write would not have been true in any way that writing it down would not have made small.

A woman in Leveler grey came past him with the clipboard. She nodded at him the small nod that did not commit to recognition. He nodded back.

"You're the one with the workshop," she said, not quite stopping.

"Tesha's the one with the workshop."

"Mm. That's what they said you'd say."

She went on. She was at the next door before he understood she had paid him a compliment, and by the time he understood it she was inside the doorway, and he was alone at the edge of the square again, with the wind coming down the lane and lifting the corner of his coat.

* * *

He was crossing the small market on Anside when he saw Davan.

The market on Anside was a market in name only at this hour, the stalls mostly down, two or three women still selling the late root vegetables and the small hard apples that came in from the western hills in the autumn. The light was the grey late-afternoon light that came in flat under a low sky, and the cobbles were wet in the patches where the stalls had been washing down, and Davan was standing in front of one of the new heating posts at the corner of the square, in the long grey coat he had worn through the rallies in the summer, and he was looking at the post the way a man looked at a thing that had been built without him.

The post was a hybrid. Ash recognized the housing from twenty paces. The lower casing was the cast Leveler model, the one with the contribution port and the small embossed mark of the contribution office, and the upper casing was the mechanical backup Tesha had begun fitting to the south block grid in October, the small brass valve and the resistive coil that took over when the contributed pool ran thin. Somebody, recently, had painted the seam between the two casings in a flat rust-red, the way a person painted a seam they did not want to pretend was not there.

Davan stood in front of it with his hands in his coat pockets. He had lost weight since the summer. The coat hung on him the way a coat hung on a man who had stopped, some weeks ago, eating with attention. His hair was longer than he had used to wear it. His shoulders were the shoulders of a man who had carried a thing a long way and was, at the moment, neither carrying it nor putting it down.

Ash slowed.

He could have crossed the square at the other diagonal. He had time to make the choice. He went on at the same pace, and Davan saw him coming, and Davan did not move.

They came up to the post at the same time, from opposite sides, which was a thing Ash had not planned and which Davan, from the small steady look of him, had not planned either. They stood at the post. The post was warm. Ash could feel the warmth coming off the upper casing, the small mechanical warmth of the backup, the kind of warmth that had nothing of channeling in it and nothing of contribution either, the kind a kettle gave off, which was to say honest.

Davan looked at the post. He did not look at Ash.

"This isn't what I built," Davan said.

"No," Ash said. "It's what they built."

Davan stood a moment longer with his hands in his pockets. He nodded once, the small private nod a man gave when he had been told a thing he already knew and had needed, for reasons of his own, to hear from someone else. He did not look up.

He turned, after that, and walked away across the square, in the long unhurried walk he had, and he did not look back. Ash watched him go as far as the corner. Davan did not put his hand on the post on the way past. He did not touch it. He went around it, at the small careful distance a man kept from a thing he had decided not to argue with, and the corner of his coat caught on the edge of the next stall and he did not stop to free it, and the stall woman called after him and he did not turn, and then he was around the corner and gone.

Ash stood at the post for some time after that.

He put his hand on the upper casing once, briefly, the way a man checked a thing he already knew the temperature of, and he took it off again, and he went on across the square in the direction he had been going, which was, he realized halfway down the next lane, no direction at all.

* * *

He went home the long way, by the river.

The river at this hour was the colorless river of late autumn, low between its walls, the water moving with the slow deliberate weight it had when the upstream pools had begun to thin. There were two barges at the far quay and one of them was the small grain barge that came down from the western wards on Tuesdays, and the other one was a barge Ash did not recognize, and he walked along the embankment and watched the water and did not think, for a while, about anything at all.

He thought, when he began to think again, about Davan.

He thought about Davan in the spring, on the platform at the Crescent, with his sleeves rolled to the elbow and his voice carrying across two thousand people without any effort at all, saying the thing he had been right about, which was that the people who had power had been hoarding it, and the thing he had been wrong about, which was that taking the hoard apart would refill the well. He thought about Davan in the summer, at the small back room meeting where Ash had gone, once, uninvited, and stood at the back, and Davan had seen him at the back and had not, that night, made anything of it, the small courtesy of a man who had decided his quarrel with Ash was not, on that particular evening, worth the room's attention.

He thought about Davan in front of the post.

He took his hand out of his coat pocket and looked at it. There was nothing on his palm. He had not held anything that day that left a mark. He put his hand back in his pocket.

*The success and the loss are the same thing,* he thought, and then, because the thought was the kind of thought a man wrote down and then was embarrassed by in the morning, he did not write it down. He walked along the embankment with the river on his left and the wall on his right, and he let the thought go down the river the way he had been learning, this autumn, to let things go.

He was not, he found, sorry that he had been right.

He was not, he found, glad either.

He walked a long way along the river before he turned back toward the workshop, and when he turned back the light had gone out of the sky and the lamps along the embankment had begun to come up one by one, the small steady mechanical lamps the city had begun installing in October, the ones that did not flicker because they did not depend on anyone's pool, and he walked home under them, and the wind off the river came up the back of his coat, and he put his hand inside the coat and felt for the folded paper in the inside pocket, the small brief letter Maren had sent him in October that he had not, since he had moved it from the outside pocket, taken out and read again.

It was where he had put it. The paper was warm against his palm from his own body. He left it there.

* * *

Haran had news at supper.

Haran had news the way Haran had news, which was to say he had it laid out on the table in three pieces between the bread and the small pot of butter, and he did not start with it, and he did not announce it, and he waited for the soup to be in front of them both before he reached over and turned the first piece around so Ash could read it.

The first piece was a notice from the Founders' Hall. The meeting had broken up at the second hour. There would be no resolution. There would be, the notice said in the small careful language of a movement that was trying to look composed, *continued discussions among the affiliated councils.* Haran had underlined *continued discussions* in pencil.

"Three pieces," Ash said.

"Three pieces."

The second piece was a printed sheet from the Wells Lane council. The Wells Lane council had voted, by a margin of one, to suspend further mandatory contributions until the spring. The vote had been close. The minutes, which somebody had passed to Haran by the small route Haran's minutes came by, recorded that two of the elder channelers had stood up before the vote and asked that the suspension be made permanent for them personally, and that the chair had not, for some minutes, been able to bring the room back to order.

"Mm," Ash said.

"Read the third."

The third piece was a single line in Haran's hand on the back of an envelope. *D. has gone north,* the line said. *To the family farm. Two weeks at least. Possibly the winter.*

Ash read the line twice. He set the envelope down on the table beside the soup.

"Who told you," he said.

"Liss."

"Liss is in the city?"

"Liss is in the city. Liss came by the shop this afternoon. Liss did not stay long. Liss said to tell you, if it came up, that he was not asking anything."

"Mm."

"He didn't say that the way he says a thing he isn't asking for. He said it the way a man says a thing he isn't asking for and means it."

Ash looked at the line on the envelope. *D. has gone north.* The two weeks at least. The possibly the winter. The small dignified retreat of a man who had decided, in a square that afternoon, that he did not have, this autumn, the room in him for what was happening in the square, and who had chosen to be elsewhere while it happened.

"He saw me at the post," Ash said.

"Mm."

"We spoke."

"What did you say."

"I said it wasn't what he built."

"Did you."

"He said it first. I said it wasn't what he built. I said it was what they built."

Haran considered him. He had the small considering look he had over the top of his glasses, the look that decided how much to say. He decided, this evening, on a portion.

"That was a kind thing," he said.

"Was it."

"It was. It was also true. The two are not always the same. This evening, on this thing, they were."

Ash did not answer. He picked up the spoon. The soup was the soup Haran made on Tuesdays, the one with the small white beans and the bit of pork at the bottom, and the steam from the bowl went up into his face and the smell of it was the smell of every Tuesday he had ever, from the time he was a child, eaten in this room, and he took a spoonful and held it on his tongue and let it cool before he swallowed.

"There's a fourth thing," Haran said, after a while.

"You said three."

"There's a fourth. I wasn't going to give it to you tonight. I'll give you the shape of it and not the rest."

"All right."

"Dorenne has put in for a private audience with the council on Thursday. The agenda is unstated. The audience is not, by the smell of it, the kind a director who is going to keep her job asks for."

Ash held the spoon over the bowl.

"She knows," he said.

"She knows."

"What does she want."

"That," Haran said, and he picked up his own spoon, and he tipped it once in the soup, "is the part of the fourth thing I am not, tonight, going to give you. Eat your soup."

Ash ate his soup.

He thought, while he ate, about Dorenne in the alley behind the workshop, with her bare hand on the brick and the dust print left on her palm and not brushed off, and the small steady look of a woman who had said *I hate that* and meant the hate and the saying both. He thought about the way she had walked out of the alley at the end without asking for anything, which was the only thing, in his memory of her, she had ever done that he had not been able to predict.

He did not know what she would ask for on Thursday. He suspected, eating his soup, that she did not entirely know either. The merger in the square had taken the structure she had managed for thirty years and made it, in a single autumn, a thing that ran without her, and a person who had managed a thing for thirty years did not, in his experience, walk easily into a room and tell a council that the thing now ran without her. She would walk into the room with something. He did not know what.

He thought, then, that it was not, this evening, his to know.

He put the spoon down. The soup was half gone. He picked up the bread and tore off a piece and put the piece in the soup and let it sit, the way he had been doing since he was six, which was a thing Haran had stopped commenting on at about the same age.

"Joren," he said, after a while.

"What about him."

"He's good."

"I thought he might be."

"He drew a roster on the back of a Crescent contribution sheet. Tesha told him to write it instead of print it. He wrote it."

"Mm."

"She was right to tell him."

"Tesha is, more often than not, right to tell people things."

Ash smiled. He had not, he realized as it happened, smiled all afternoon. The smile went up at one corner and then the other and stayed for a moment and then went, and Haran, across the table, did not remark on it, which was the courtesy of a man who knew that a smile remarked on was a smile that did not come back.

They ate. The lamp on the table was the small steady lamp Haran had had since before Ash was born, the one with the brass collar and the wick he trimmed on Sundays, and the light from it went up the wall behind Haran and made the small soft shape it had always made, and outside the window the wind had come up off the river and was moving in the lane, and somewhere down the lane a door opened and a voice called for a child to come in, and the child, in the way of children, did not, for some moments, come.

* * *

He went up to his room afterward and sat on the edge of the bed for a while without doing anything in particular.

He took the letter out of the inside pocket. He did not open it. He held it in his palm, the small folded square of it, the paper softer at the creases now from having been carried in the inside pocket for a month, and he turned it over once and turned it back, and he set it on the small table beside the bed where he kept the cup of water and the book he was, this autumn, not really reading.

He had thought, walking home, that he would write something tonight. A letter. To Davan, possibly, although he would not have known where to send it and would not have sent it if he had. To Maren, more likely, although he had not yet found the form of a letter to Maren that did not feel, when he read it back, like a man trying to write his way out of going to see her. He had not, in the end, written either. He sat on the edge of the bed and looked at the letter on the table and the cup beside the letter and the book beside the cup, and he did not, for some time, do anything but look.

The small paper book was in his coat. He had not, since the square, written in it. He thought about getting up and getting it out of the coat and writing in it, the small note about Joren and the roster, the small note about the woman with the clipboard and the south block women working at the same grid, the small note about Davan walking around the post at a careful distance and not touching it. He did not get up.

The notes, he thought, would still be there in the morning. The things they were notes about would also still be there in the morning. The merger in the square had been happening for two weeks without his writing it down, and would go on happening, and his writing it down or not writing it down was not, this autumn, the thing it had been a year ago.

He thought, after a while, that this was what being right felt like. It did not feel like being right. It felt like a low room and a lamp and a folded letter on a table and a square in another part of the city where two groups of people who did not, individually, like each other very much had begun, without asking anyone, to do the same work in the same place at the same hour, because the work needed doing and they were the people who were there.

He lay down on the bed without taking his coat off.

He listened to the wind in the lane. He listened to the small ticking of Haran's clock downstairs, the one that Haran had, this morning, not given up on, and that was, this evening, running again, in the slow uneven way of a clock that had been talked back into running by a man who knew it well enough to know when to stop turning the screw.

He closed his eyes.

The success and the loss, he thought, were not the same thing. He had been wrong about that on the embankment. The success was the square. The loss was Davan walking around the post. The two had happened on the same afternoon, in the same city, to the same man watching, and they were not the same thing. They were two things. They had come together this autumn and would come apart again, in their own time, and the work he had to do was not to make them one thing and not to pretend they were not both true.

He slept after a while.

He slept the way he had been sleeping lately, which was lightly, and badly, and through.
