# Chapter 18

He came into the south block in the morning without a bag.

He had carried the bag for two months. The small canvas one with the small tools, the spool of wire, the file with the wood handle, the two drivers, the bit of beeswax for stuck threads. He had carried it the way a man carried a bag he might need, and the carrying had been the carrying it was, which was the carrying of a man who came into a place expecting to be the answer to the place's question.

He had, this morning, left the bag on the peg.

He had left it on the peg without deciding to leave it. He had stood at the peg with his coat in his hand and the bag at his foot, and he had picked up the coat and not the bag, and he had not, picking up the coat, said to himself the words for what he was doing. He had said them later, halfway down the curve, when his hand at his side had felt the lightness it felt without the strap, and the lightness had been a thing he had not, until that count, named.

He came in at the press corner.

The press was running. Pell was on the lever. Mira was at the feed. There was a young man at the cradle Ash did not know, and the young man was working the cradle slow, in the slow way a man worked a cradle when he was learning, and Pell did not stop the lever to call across, because Pell had, in the autumn, stopped stopping the lever to call across, and Pell only nodded, and Ash nodded, and the press kept running.

He went past the press.

He went up the lane to the heating row. The heating row was the row it had been the day before, which was the row it had been the day before that, which was the small run of dwellings on the north edge with the wind catch on the roof and the elements in the wall plates and the wires along the eaves, and the row this morning had a thin smoke at one chimney that was the smoke of a stove and not of a fire, and the row was the row.

Tesha was at the third dwelling.

She was on the doorstep with a cup in her hand and a woman Ash had seen but not spoken to, who was the woman whose dwelling the third dwelling was, and the two of them were standing the way two women stood at a doorstep in the morning, which was at an angle to each other, with the cups between them, and the talk Ash heard as he came up was not talk for him.

He stopped at the corner of the lane.

He did not go to the doorstep. He waited the count it took for Tesha to see him, and Tesha saw him, and she did not, in the seeing, wave him over. She finished what she was saying to the woman. The woman went in. Tesha came down the step with the cup in her hand and came to the corner of the lane.

"Ash."

"Tesha."

"You don't have the bag."

"No."

She looked at him a count.

She did not, in the count, ask why. She drank from the cup. She tipped it to him in the small offering Tesha made when she was offering tea, and Ash shook his head, and Tesha drank.

"I came," he said, "to ask."

"Mm."

"What doesn't work."

She looked at him.

She looked at him the way she had looked at him in the small dwelling the afternoon before, which was without pressure and with the small careful attention. She did not, in the looking, smile. She drank from the cup again.

"That's a long question," she said.

"I know."

"You want it short."

"I want it true."

She set the cup down on the low wall at the corner of the lane. She wiped her hands on the front of her apron. She looked up the row.

"All right," she said.

* * *

They walked.

Tesha walked the way Tesha walked, which was with her hands in the pockets of the apron and her eyes on the lane and the small steady pace of a woman who had work to be at later and was not, for the count of a walk, hurrying. Ash walked beside her. He did not, walking, take out a piece of paper. He had, in the inside pocket of the coat, a folded sheet and the stub of pencil he had brought from the bench. He did not take them out.

He had been told, in the autumn, to listen first.

He had been trying, since the autumn, to learn what listening first looked like, and the learning had been slow, and the slowness had been the slowness it was. This morning he was trying again.

"Building Six," Tesha said.

"Mm."

"The pump."

"The pump works."

"The pump works."

She stopped at the lane corner. She looked at him.

"Come and see where it works," she said.

She turned up the slope. He went with her. The slope was the small rise behind the heating row that ran up to the cistern at the second terrace, where Ash had set the pump in the spring on the foundation he had channeled, with the line running down through the wall at the back of Building Six and out at the spigot in the inner court. He had set the pump where the line ran cleanest. He had set it on the high side, because pumps wanted the high side, and the line wanted the gradient, and the engineering had been the engineering it was, which had been, in the spring, a thing he had been pleased with.

Tesha walked him up the slope.

She walked him to the spigot at the inner court of Building Six, which was at the bottom of the line where the line came out of the wall. Two women were at the spigot with buckets. One of them was an older woman in a grey shawl. The other was younger and had a child at her hip. They drew water. They set the buckets on the cobbles. They picked the buckets up.

They went up the stair.

They went up the stair on the inside of the court, which ran three flights up to the dwellings at the upper terrace, and they went up with the buckets in their hands, and the buckets were the buckets of women going up a stair with water.

Ash watched them go.

"The dwellings are up there," Tesha said.

"Yes."

"The pump's down here."

"Yes."

"They carry it up."

"Yes."

"They carry it up three flights, in the morning, twice. They carry it up at noon, once. They carry it up at evening, twice. Fifty-eight buckets a day, in the building. I counted."

He looked at the stair.

He looked at the stair the way a man looked at a stair he had not, in the spring when he set the pump, looked at, because in the spring he had been looking at the cistern and the line and the gradient and the foundation and the place where the pump wanted to sit, and the stair had not been a thing he had looked at, because the stair had not been a thing he had been thinking about.

"The pump," he said.

"Works."

"Yes."

"In the wrong place."

He took a count.

The instinct came up at the back of him. The instinct said, *it's the gradient, the gradient is the gradient, you can't move the pump without moving the line, and you can't move the line without moving the foundation, and the foundation is the foundation it is, and the engineering is the engineering*. The instinct said this in the quick clean voice of a man who had set the pump in the spring and known what he was doing, and the voice was the voice it was.

He did not, this morning, speak in the voice.

"Where does it want to be," he said.

Tesha looked at him.

"Up at the upper terrace," she said. "By the kitchen door of the third dwelling on the north side. Where the women come out with the buckets."

"The line runs the wrong way for that."

"I know."

"The line runs down."

"I know."

She did not, in the saying, soften it. She said it the way she had said the screw was being slow the afternoon before, which was as the thing it was, and the thing it was sat between them in the lane the way a thing sat between two people who were not arguing about it.

"It would mean a new line," he said.

"Yes."

"It would mean breaking the foundation."

"Yes."

"It would mean a worse pump."

"Maybe."

He stood with his hands at his sides.

The instinct said, *the engineering*. The instinct said, *the elegance*. The instinct said, *you set this up in the spring, you set it up right, the line is clean, the gradient is clean, the foundation is sound, and the women carrying the buckets are the women, and they can carry the buckets, and the pump is the pump it is*.

He did not, this morning, speak in the voice.

He thought about the upper post on the second plate.

He thought about Tesha's hand at the file.

He thought about the women going up the stair with the buckets.

"All right," he said.

She looked at him.

"All right," he said again.

She did not, in the looking, do anything with her face. She picked up her cup off the low wall, where she had set it when she was not at the low wall, and Ash did not, for a count, understand how the cup had moved with her, and then he did, because Tesha had picked it up in passing in the way Tesha did things, and he had not seen her do it.

"I'll show you the other things," she said.

"Yes."

"This afternoon."

"Yes."

"Bring paper."

"I have paper."

"Bring more."

* * *

He brought more.

He went back to the workshop in the middle of the day and took, from the drawer under the long bench, the bound book Haran kept for the answer-when-time letters and which had, in the bottom third of it, a stack of blank sheets the size of a man's hand. He took ten of the sheets. He folded them once. He put them in the inside pocket of the coat, where the bench paper sat with Joren's letter, and he put the folded sheaf in beside them, and the inside pocket had, now, a small thickness to it.

Haran looked at him over the long bench.

Haran did not, looking, ask.

"Tesha," Ash said.

"Mm."

"She's showing me what doesn't work."

"Mm."

"In the south block."

"Mm."

Haran picked up a piece of stock. He set it on the cradle. He set the small hammer beside it. He did not, for a count, work. He looked at Ash.

"Bring back what she says," he said.

"I will."

"Not what you'd do about it."

"No."

"What she says."

"Yes."

Haran nodded the small nod he nodded when a thing had been said that did not need to be said again, and he turned to the cradle, and he picked up the hammer, and Ash went out the door.

* * *

He came back at the press corner in the afternoon.

Tesha was at the heating row. She had a cloth at her hip and the small driver in her apron pocket and Mira at her shoulder, and the woman from the doorstep in the morning was inside the third dwelling with the door open, and Tesha was on the step with the wall plate cover in her hand, looking at the plate.

"Ash."

"Tesha."

"This is the second one."

"The second."

"That fails."

"Mm."

She turned the cover in her hand.

"The first one was the upper post," she said. "Yesterday. You saw."

"Yes."

"This one's the lower terminal. The screw's stripped. Mira pulled the cover this morning. The screw turns and doesn't bite."

He looked at the plate. The lower terminal was a plate he had set in the spring, with a brass post and a brass screw, and the screw was a screw he had threaded with the tap he had brought from the workshop, and the tap had been a clean tap. He looked at the screw.

He did not put his hand on it.

"How long has it been turning."

"Two weeks."

"The household didn't say."

"The household didn't know it was turning. The household knew the heat was uneven. The heat was warm in the morning and cold by the second hour, because the screw was working loose under the warm, and the warm was the load, and the load was loosening it."

"And the maintenance."

"Wasn't due until next month."

"And next month."

"Is the maintenance window."

She said *the maintenance window* the way a man said a phrase he had taken from another man's mouth, and Ash heard, in the saying, the small sour edge that Tesha put on phrases that had come from his own mouth, and which had stopped, at some point in the autumn, sounding to Tesha the way they had sounded to him.

"Tell me," he said.

"The window's at the second hour after the bell. To the fourth."

"In the morning."

"In the morning."

"And."

"And the household isn't home. The man's at the warehouse. The wife's at the laundry. The boy's at the school. The grandmother's at the market. There's nobody to open the door. The crew comes. The crew knocks. The crew goes."

"And next time."

"Next time the crew comes back at the second hour at the second day, and the household isn't home, and the crew goes again. By the third week we get a key from the woman, and we go in without her, and we work in her dwelling without her there, and she comes home and the chairs are moved and the cover's on the wall and a strange man's tea is on the windowsill."

"And."

"And she doesn't like it."

"No."

"And the next household won't give us a key."

"No."

"And the maintenance window is the window."

He took the folded sheaf out of his inside pocket.

He took the stub of pencil from the corner where he had put it. He stood at the wall plate with the sheaf in his hand and the pencil in the other hand, and he opened the sheaf to the first sheet, and he wrote, in the careful hand he had been writing in for Joren, the words *maintenance window: wrong*.

He looked at Tesha.

"Tell me when the household is home."

"Evening. After the seventh bell."

"All of them."

"Most of them. The children before. The women between sixth and seventh. The men by seventh."

"And the crew."

"Goes home at sixth."

"Mm."

He wrote *crew at sixth, household at seventh*. He wrote *gap*. He wrote *crew works in cold dwelling, household lives in warm one*. He looked at what he had written. He did not, looking, know yet what to do with it. He folded the sheaf. He put it back in the inside pocket.

"What else," he said.

"The training."

"The training."

"You did a training in the spring. I came. Mira came. Pell came. Sera came. We learned the operation. We learned to read the gauges. We learned the cleaning. We learned the wire wrap."

"Yes."

"We didn't learn what to do when it failed."

He looked at her.

"It didn't fail in the spring," he said.

"No. It didn't."

"And."

"And when it fails now in a way that isn't on the manual, we freeze. Yesterday I froze. I sat at the plate for an afternoon because the manual said *coil, wire, plate* and the answer was *post*. The post wasn't on the manual. The post was on the plate, but it wasn't on the manual, and I didn't, for an afternoon, look at the post."

"You did look."

"Eventually."

"Mm."

"The next person won't. The next person, when something fails in a way that isn't on the manual, will go and get me. And I'll come. And next time it'll be a thing I haven't seen, and I'll sit at it for an afternoon. And the woman whose plate it is will be cold for two days, and she'll go and find a man with a wide loose press to come and warm the room, and that's the thing we were trying not to do."

He stood with his hands at his sides.

The instinct said, *write a better manual*. The instinct said this fast and clean and the instinct was the instinct it was. He felt the instinct come up at the back of him the way he had felt it at the pump, and he did not, this afternoon, speak in the voice.

He took the sheaf out again.

He wrote *training: operation, not improvisation*. He wrote, under it, *what would teach improvisation*. He looked at it.

"Tesha."

"Mm."

"What would teach improvisation."

She looked at him.

She looked at him a long count. She did not, in the count, answer. She put her hands in the pockets of her apron. She turned and looked up the heating row, which was empty in the afternoon, with the wind catch on the roof turning slowly and the chimneys of the dwellings going up into the grey.

"I don't know," she said.

"Mm."

"That's the right answer."

"Yes."

"I don't know."

He wrote *don't know yet*. He folded the sheaf.

* * *

They walked the row.

She showed him the third thing, which was a vent in Building Four that had been set in the kitchen wall and which the household had, in the winter, blocked with a folded blanket because the vent in the kitchen wall opened above the place where the grandmother slept, and the grandmother was old, and the cold off the vent was a cold the grandmother could not sleep through. The vent was meant to draw the warm from the heating element through the dwelling. The blanket meant the warm did not draw, and the kitchen was warm and the back room was cold, and the grandmother was cold.

She showed him the fourth thing, which was a wire in Building Two that ran along the outside of the eave and which the rooks had, in the autumn, started landing on, and the rooks had not broken the wire, but the rooks had loosened the staple at the third bracket, and the wire was sagging, and the sagging would, in a wind, take the wire down.

She showed him the fifth thing, which was a small thing, and which was the way the heating elements clicked in the night when they came up to working temperature, and which was the small clean tick a man heard at the edge of hearing, and which was a tick that, in three of the dwellings, woke the children. The children had been waking for a month. The mothers had not said. The mothers had not said because the mothers had not, in the dwellings before, had heat at all, and the heat was the heat, and a tick that woke a child was a thing a mother bore.

He wrote.

He wrote *vent: location wrong*. He wrote *staple: wind*. He wrote *tick: nights*. He folded the sheaf. He put it in the inside pocket. The inside pocket had, by the end of the row, a thickness it had not had in the morning, and the thickness was not the thickness of the bench paper or of Joren's letter, and was the thickness of a thing he had not, before this afternoon, written down.

They came back to the press corner.

Tesha leaned on the low wall.

"That's the row," she said.

"That's the row."

"There's more."

"I know."

"There's a hundred more."

"I know."

She looked at him.

"What are you going to do," she said.

He took a count.

The instinct said, *write the plan*. The instinct said, *take the sheaf back to the bench, lay it out, look at it whole, sequence the work, allocate the days, assign the crew*. The instinct said this in the clean voice that had, in the spring, set the pump on the high side of the slope.

He did not, this afternoon, speak in the voice.

"I'm going to bring the sheaf back tomorrow," he said.

"To do what."

"To ask you which one."

"Which one."

"To start with."

She looked at him.

She looked at him for a count, and she did not, in the count, smile. She picked up her cup off the low wall, where she had set it again when he had not been watching, and she drank.

"All right," she said.

* * *

He went back the next day.

He went back the day after that.

He did not, in the going back, bring a plan. He brought the sheaf. He brought the stub of pencil. He brought the question. The question was the same question he had asked at the lane corner, which was the question of what didn't work, and the answers came in the rhythm Tesha gave them, which was the rhythm of a woman who had, for a year and a half, been keeping a small ledger in her head of the things that did not work, and which she had not, in the year and a half, been asked.

She gave him the ledger.

She gave it to him over three afternoons, in pieces, in the lanes and at the doorsteps and at the press corner with the cup of tea Pell made, and Ash wrote it down. He filled the sheaf. He took out the bound book from the workshop and started a second sheaf. He filled half of the second.

On the fourth afternoon, Tesha said, "The pump."

"The pump."

"You said all right."

"I did."

"And."

He took a count.

The instinct said, *the engineering*. The instinct said, *the line, the gradient, the foundation*. The instinct said this and Ash listened to it and let it say what it was going to say, and at the end of the saying he heard, under the saying, the smaller voice that said, *the women carrying the buckets up three flights, twice in the morning, once at noon, twice at evening*.

"We move it," he said.

"Where."

"Where you said. Upper terrace. Kitchen door."

"The line."

"New line."

"The foundation."

"I'll do the foundation."

She looked at him.

"It'll be ugly," she said.

"Yes."

"There'll be a stub of the old line in the wall."

"Yes."

"It'll look like we made a mistake."

"We did make a mistake."

She did not, in the count, answer.

She drank from the cup. She set it down. She picked it up again.

"All right," she said.

* * *

He did the foundation in the morning of the next day.

He did it the way he had been trying to do things since the morning at the offcut, which was with the line, narrowed, held for the count of the work. He stood at the place on the upper terrace where the new pump would sit, and the place was a small square at the corner of the wall by the kitchen door of the third dwelling on the north side, where two women had been standing the morning before with buckets in their hands, and where the cobbles came up to the wall in the small uneven way cobbles came up to a wall. He had, in the day between, marked the square with chalk. He had laid two iron rods at the corners. He had set, in the night before, the cradle the new pump would sit on, in the small careful work of a man setting a cradle without channeling.

This morning he was going to set the foundation under the cradle.

He stood at the square. He set his palm above the cobble at the inner corner. He breathed.

He narrowed.

He narrowed slowly. He found, under the narrowing, the thin line. The thin line was in his palm. He held it. He did not, in the holding, open the wide loose press. He put the line at the cobble. He held the cobble against the line. The cobble warmed. The cobble warmed in the way a cobble warmed under the line, which was not the fog-warming of the wide loose press but the small clean warming of a thing being asked, exactly, to do a thing. He held the line a count. He let the cobble take the warming. He let the warming go down through the cobble into the bedding under it, and he let the bedding take it, and he let the bedding fuse to the cobble in the small slow fusing of a foundation coming up, and he held the line, and he held it, and he held it, and at the count he had measured he pulled back.

The cobble was set.

It was set in the small way a cobble was set when the bedding under it had been brought up to the working temperature for the count of a working, and the cobble was, now, a thing a man could not, with his hand, lift.

He went to the next cobble.

He set his palm above it. He narrowed. He found the line. He held the line at the cobble. The cobble warmed. He held it the count. He pulled back.

He went around the square.

He went around the square in the small steady rhythm of a man who had a working to do and the time to do it, and the working took him twenty counts, and the spending was a spending he did not feel as the spending of the wide loose press, and the spending was, in the back of him, a thing he could feel as a quantity, and the quantity was a small quantity, and it was, by the math at the back of his hand, perhaps a third of what the wide loose press would have spent.

He stood at the square.

He looked at the foundation.

The foundation was a foundation. It was not a foundation any man would have looked at and said, *this man channeled the cobbles down*. It was a foundation that a careful man might have said, *the cobbles here are tight*, and a less careful man would not have said anything about, because the foundation was a foundation, and foundations were not things men remarked on.

That was the point.

He had, in the spring, channeled foundations that men had remarked on. He had channeled them with the wide loose press, which had set the cobbles deep and the bedding hard and the foundation visible from a lane, and he had been pleased, in the spring, with the visibility, because the visibility had been, to him then, the proof of the working.

This foundation was not visible.

This foundation would hold the pump.

He went to the next square.

* * *

Tesha came up at the eighth hour with two men he did not know.

The two men were carpenters from the corner shed. They had a length of the new line on their shoulders, in two pieces, with a coupling between, and they had the cradle, and they had the small iron pump that Pell's brother had brought down from the upper warehouse the day before, and they set the pump on the cradle on the foundation, and they coupled the line, and they ran the line down the inside of the wall in the channel Tesha had cut the day before, and Tesha was at the channel with the small driver in her hand, and Tesha tightened the bracket.

Ash watched.

He did not, in the watching, channel. He did not put his hand on the pump. He stood at the corner of the wall with his hands in his pockets and watched two carpenters and Tesha set a pump that, in the spring, he would have set alone. The setting took an hour. The setting was done in the small clean rhythm of three people who had each, in the doing, a thing they were good at, and Ash watching at the corner was a fourth thing that did not, in the rhythm, need to be there.

When the line was on, Tesha pumped.

She pumped the handle three times. The line ran. Water came out of the spigot at the kitchen door. The water ran down the gutter. The water went where water went on the upper terrace, which was down the gutter and out the small drain at the corner.

Tesha looked at it.

"Huh," she said.

"Huh," one of the carpenters said.

"Runs downhill."

"Runs downhill."

She turned to Ash.

"Runs downhill," she said.

"Yes."

"Was running uphill."

"Yes."

He stood with his hands in his pockets.

He stood at the corner of the wall and watched the water run out of the spigot at the kitchen door of the third dwelling on the north side, and the water ran the way water ran when it was running the way water ran, which was downhill, and a woman in the doorway of the dwelling was watching the water, and the woman did not, in the watching, say anything to him. She did not, in the watching, look at him. She was looking at the water.

* * *

The heating element in the second dwelling came back the next week.

It did not come back the way it had come back the time before. It did not, this time, fail at the upper post. It failed at the lower terminal, where the screw had been working loose under the load, and it failed in the morning before the household left for the day, and the woman of the household opened the door for the crew at the seventh bell because the crew had, in the week between, started coming at the seventh bell instead of the second.

Tesha had moved the maintenance window.

She had moved it without asking. She had told the crew that the window was wrong, and that the window would, going forward, be at the seventh bell, and the crew had said yes, because Tesha was the one who had been at the third dwelling on the morning the woman had not given them a key, and the crew had not, since, been to a dwelling where they had not been let in.

Ash went to the second dwelling at the seventh bell.

He went without the bag.

He went with the sheaf, and he went with the stub of pencil, and he went with, in his palm, the thin line at the back of the morning's working, which he had been holding, this last week, on the offcut at dawn and on the cobbles at the upper terrace and on the small iron bracket he had reset on the wire of Building Two where the rooks had loosened the staple. He went into the second dwelling at the seventh bell, and the woman of the household let them in, and she gave them tea, and she sat at her own table with her own cup and she did not, while they worked, leave the room.

The crew worked.

Tesha was at the plate with the cover off. Mira was at her shoulder. A young man Ash did not know was at the door with the basket. They were looking at the lower terminal. They were looking at the screw.

"Stripped," Tesha said.

"Stripped," Mira said.

"New screw."

"New screw."

The young man at the door brought a new screw. Tesha set the new screw at the post. She tightened it. She tested it with the back of the driver. She nodded.

She looked at Ash.

"The post," she said.

"The post."

"Same post as last week."

"Same family of post."

"Yes."

"It'll burn at the wrap if we don't do something."

She looked at him. She did not, in the looking, say what the something was. She had been, for a week now, not saying what the something was, because she had learned, in the week, that if she did not say it, Ash would. And if Ash said it, the saying was a thing Ash had said, and not a thing Tesha had said, and the not-saying was a small careful thing Tesha did with him now.

He took a count.

He thought about the post. He thought about the wire at the wrap, and the contact, and the small place where two materials met and one of them gave. He thought about Joren's letter in his inside pocket, which had a question in it about the contact, and about the hard side and the soft side and the giving. He thought about the answer Joren had not, in the second letter, given him, because the answer was not a thing Joren had figured out yet, and the figuring was a thing Joren and Ash were, between them, figuring.

"I can catalyze it," he said.

"Catalyze."

"Heat the post. Once. Hot enough to set the wrap into the post. So the contact stops being a contact and starts being a fusion."

"And."

"And it won't burn. The contact won't burn because there won't be a contact."

"Cost."

He took a count.

"Small," he said. "Much smaller than the wide loose."

"How small."

"A breath. Maybe two."

She looked at him.

She looked at him the way she had looked at him at the lane corner on the first morning, which was the look of a woman taking a thing in. She did not, in the look, ask him to do it. She was not, in the look, telling him to do it. She was looking at him.

"Show me," she said.

He set his palm above the post.

He breathed.

He narrowed.

He narrowed slowly. He found the line. He held the line. He brought the line to the post, and the post warmed, and he held the line at the post until the wrap and the post were, in the warm, the same warm, and at the count he held them at the working temperature he let the line let go, and the wrap and the post settled into each other in the small fusing of a thing being made one thing out of two, and he pulled back.

He stood with his hand at his side.

The post was the post.

It was not a post anyone in the dwelling would have looked at and said anything about.
