# Chapter 6 — Home

He left the workshop before sundown for the first time in nine days.

Haran didn't look up from the bench. The file moved across a length of brass in even strokes. A second lamp burned on the back shelf where Haran had been sleeping, the bedroll folded against the wall with a precision that suggested it had not been unfolded in a week.

"Going home," Ash said.

"Mm."

"Dinner."

"Mm."

Ash stood in the doorway. The square outside had cooled. Vell was at her pump, finishing the late fill, the crank's pulse steady through the flagstones. A boy crossed the square with a bucket. The bucket clanked empty against his knee, then full against his shin on the return.

"I'll be back in the morning," Ash said.

Haran turned the brass piece over. Set it down. Picked up a smaller file.

"Tell your mother," he said, "that the kettle she sent works."

Ash hadn't known about a kettle.

"I will."

He took the long way out of Stonecurve. Past the basin, past the eastern flue stack where the brick was still pale against the older wall, past the open door of Block Three where a woman was stirring a pot on a stove that drew its own air and held its own heat. The smell of onions reached him at the threshold and stopped him for the half-step it took to identify it. Onions in cooking fat. Not channeled warmth. A fire in a stove in a kitchen, and a woman cooking on it, and a child somewhere behind her saying something about the bread.

He walked on.

The climb out of Stonecurve was not steep, but he felt it in his calves by the time he reached the second terrace. He had been on his feet most days for three weeks. He had not eaten a sit-down meal since. The reservoir behind his ribs sat where it had sat that morning, the boundary close, the wall present when he reached without reaching.

The new apartment was on the third terrace, two streets above the line where the earthquake's worst damage had stopped. Dorenne had arranged it. He had been there twice. He had not, either time, stayed past the second hour.

He climbed the stairs.

* * *

His mother opened the door before he knocked.

She had been waiting at the window. He saw it in the angle of her shoulders, the half-turn that said she'd crossed the room as his foot hit the landing. She didn't say so. She held the door open and looked at him for a second longer than the greeting required, and then stepped back.

"You're thin," she said.

"I'm fine."

"You're thin."

He came in. The apartment smelled of stew and of the soap his mother had been using since he was a child, the cheap astringent kind from the lower markets, which she had brought with her into the new kitchen and had not replaced. The new kitchen had a tap. The tap worked. She had set the dented kettle on the back of the stove anyway, and the cracked bowl sat on the open shelf above the basin where she had always kept the cracked bowl.

His father was in the front room with the door propped half-open to the kitchen. He sat at a small table with a length of copper pipe across it, a hacksaw, and a tin of paste flux. He looked up.

"Boy," his father said.

"Father."

His father didn't stand. His father didn't, generally, stand for greetings. He gestured with the hacksaw at the chair across from him, and Ash sat.

"What's the pipe," Ash said.

"Drain run. The one under the basin's set wrong. Comes back into the trap on the high side." His father lifted the length, sighted along it. "Whoever fitted it didn't think about it draining."

"You're cutting a new piece."

"I'm cutting two. The angle's off enough that I can't ream it true."

"You have a vise?"

"In the kitchen. On a board. Your mother lets me."

His mother passed behind Ash with a stack of plates. Set them on the table by the window. Her hand brushed the back of his neck on the return, the quickest possible touch, and was gone before he'd registered it.

"Wash," she said. "Both of you. Maren said an hour."

"Maren's coming?" Ash said.

"Maren said she'd come."

His father set the pipe down. The pipe's cut end was clean. The flux tin sat with its lid half-on, the way a man kept a tin when he meant to use it again before the evening ended.

* * *

His mother served the stew before Maren came.

"She'll eat hers warm when she gets here," his mother said. She said it to the pot, not to the table. "She'll eat it warm or she'll eat it cold. She's not keeping us."

His father reached for the bread. His mother set the loaf nearer to him without looking. They had been doing this for thirty years. The choreography did not require their attention.

"Tell me about the district," his mother said.

She sat. She took up her spoon. She did not eat. She watched Ash across the table with the attention she had given him at twelve, when he had come home from a day at the schoolyard and she had needed to know, without asking, whether the day had cost him anything.

"It's working," Ash said.

"That's not telling me."

"The water runs. The buildings hold heat. The crew is learning the work."

"Ash."

He stopped. He set the spoon down.

She had asked him about his life, when he was magicless, with a fierceness he had read at the time as protection and had understood, by sixteen, as something more complicated. She had wanted to know what every adult in his day had said and how they had said it, and what the other boys had done in the corner of his eye, and which teachers had let him sit in the back. She had been mapping a terrain she could not walk on his behalf. The mapping had been her way of walking it anyway.

She was doing the same thing now.

"There's a girl," he said. "Vell. She runs the east square pump. Fifteen, perhaps. She learned the crank in twenty minutes and the priming in three days. She fills the cistern faster than a man twice her weight."

"A girl."

"A girl."

His mother absorbed this. She did not ask the obvious questions. She turned the information once in her hand, the way she turned a coin she meant to spend later.

"And the woman with the soldering," she said. "The one your father heard about."

"Tesha. She's lapping flue seams. She's slow. She's getting it."

"Slow how."

"An hour for a job Haran does in fifteen minutes. But the seam holds. The smoke doesn't come."

His mother nodded once.

"The mother of that girl," she said. "Vell. Does she eat."

He looked up.

"What."

"Does she eat. Vell's mother. The household. Are they fed."

He didn't have an answer. He had not asked. He had not thought to ask.

His mother saw him not have the answer. She did not press it. She lifted her spoon and ate a slow first bite, and the kitchen was quiet for a moment, and his father, across the table, was looking at his stew with the particular blankness that meant he had been listening and had decided not to enter.

"I could come," his mother said.

"Come where."

"To the district. Once a week. Twice. I could cook for the crew. Or for the mothers. I can carry a pot."

She said it without looking at him. She said it to the rim of her bowl. She had her hands folded around the spoon the way she folded them around any object she'd decided to ask for and was preparing to be told no.

"Yes," Ash said.

It came out before he'd thought about it. He said yes the way a son says yes to a mother who has asked, finally, for the thing she could give. He said it because the offer was good and because she had wanted to make it. He said it before he had asked himself what she would actually do, with whom, on what schedule, in a district that did not yet have a kitchen larger than the one she was sitting in.

She heard him say it. She heard the speed of it.

She did not lift her eyes from the bowl. The corner of her mouth moved a quarter-inch in a direction that was not a smile.

"All right," she said.

She ate her bite.

His father reached for the bread again, as if the bread were the point, and tore a piece in half and handed half across the table to Ash without looking, and the half came across the table the way it had come across tables for twenty years, and Ash took it.

* * *

His father waited until the second helping to ask.

"The pipe joints," his father said. "On the water lift."

"What about them."

"Thermal stress. You said in your letter the lift runs warm in the morning shifts. Cold at night. Cycle's hard on a joint."

"It is."

"What're you using. Lead-tin?"

"On the small bore. Brazed brass at the cylinder."

His father chewed. His father had stew on the side of his thumb and had not noticed. He was looking at the table the way he looked at a piece of work he had laid out and was ordering in his head.

"Brass'll outlast you," his father said. "If the brazer was sober. The lead-tin won't. Six years on a thermal cycle. Eight if the joint doesn't see a hammer."

"Haran has us flux every joint twice."

"Haran knows."

His father said it as a man says a thing he knows because another man had taught him the same thing, in another shop, a long time ago. He had never met Haran. He had heard the name from Ash for two months. He had decided, on the strength of the name and the work it produced, that Haran was a man whose practices required no defense.

"Pressure tolerance," his father said. "On the crank."

"Six bars at the cylinder. We've never been near it. Three is the working pressure."

"What's the relief."

"Spring valve at four and a half. Haran's design."

"What's the spring."

"Steel coil. Wound in The Splits."

His father made a sound that was not a word. It was the sound he made when a piece of information slotted into a frame he'd already built. He nodded slowly.

"Won't fail for a while," his father said.

"No."

"You'll want a spare anyway."

"We have two."

"Three. The third one's the one that's there when the second one's in the pump."

It was a thing Ash had heard him say before. About a wrench. About a hinge. About a pair of work boots. The third one is the one that's there when the second one is in the pump. His father had kept three of everything for as long as Ash could remember, and the apartment they had lost had been arranged around the rule, and the new apartment was, slowly, becoming arranged around it again. Three kettles. Three good knives. Three lengths of cord under the kitchen sink in the basket his mother thought he didn't know she emptied weekly.

"I'll tell Haran," Ash said.

"Tell him I said so. He'll know who I am from it."

"He will."

His father reached for more bread.

For a minute or two there was no sound but the spoons. The Wellspring did not exist in the kitchen. The reservoir behind his ribs did not exist. His mother sat across from him eating her stew and his father sat across from him eating his and a length of copper pipe waited on the front-room table for a man to finish his dinner and cut it.

The door opened.

* * *

Maren came in with her coat still buttoned to the throat.

She had walked fast. He saw it in her color. Her cheeks were lit with the climb, the cold of the upper streets still on her shoulders, and she stood in the doorway for the half-second it took to find them at the table and then closed the door behind her with her heel.

"I'm late."

"You're not," his mother said. "Sit. The bowl's warm."

"It's cold."

"It's warm enough."

Maren unbuttoned the coat. She hung it on the hook by the door. The motion was the motion he remembered, brisk, efficient, no half-measures. She crossed to the table and put her hand on the back of his chair and bent and kissed the top of his head, and he caught the smell of paper and ink and the cold of the street on her hair.

He looked up at her as she straightened.

She was thinner.

Not dramatically. Not in a way a stranger would have read. Her face was the face he had known for twenty-six years, the same dark eyes, the same straight set of the jaw she used when she was about to deliver bad news to a client. But the line from her cheek to her jaw was a quarter-inch sharper. The collar of her shirt sat differently against her throat. The wrist that emerged from her sleeve as she pulled out her chair was the wrist of a person who had skipped two meals a week for some weeks.

She sat. His mother set the bowl in front of her.

"Eat," his mother said.

"I am."

"Eat first. Talk after."

Maren ate. She ate with the discipline she brought to any task she had assigned herself, six even spoonfuls, a bite of bread, water. She did not look up. His mother did not look at her. His mother had the kettle off the stove and was pouring tea into the cracked cup she kept for Maren, the one with the chip on the rim that Maren had made when she was nine.

"Where were you," Ash said.

"The office. A client wanted three hours."

"At this hour."

"At this hour."

"What kind of client."

"The kind who pays for three hours."

She said it the way she said most things about her work, which was the way she had been saying them since she had become a channeler-for-hire at seventeen. The work had a shape. The shape was none of his business. The shape was none of anyone's business but the shape's. She had been protecting the shape from his curiosity, and from their mother's, since the year she had moved out.

He let it go. He had let it go for eight years. He let it go now.

"How's the project," she said.

"Good."

"That's not telling me."

She said it with the same inflection their mother had used. It was not deliberate. The two of them did not, generally, agree on the angle of approach to anything. But in the sentences they used to cut through Ash's evasions, they had been the same person for as long as he could remember.

"It's working," he said. "Water's running. Heat's holding. The crew is learning."

"I want to see."

"Tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow."

His mother set the cup of tea at Maren's elbow. Maren glanced at it, a small acknowledgment, and went on eating.

"Did you see Ryn," his mother said.

She said it to Ash. She did not look up from her own bowl.

"Not this week."

"Last week."

"No."

His mother nodded once. She did not say anything else. The not-saying was the thing.

He looked at his bowl. He did not look at his mother. He had been looking at his bowl the same way at sixteen, the year he had stopped going to his cousins' house on the eighth-day, and he had stood at this same kind of pause, with this same kind of silence on the other side of the table, and he had eaten his bowl the same way then.

His mother lifted her tea. Drank.

"Ryn asked after you," she said. "Through Aunt Pera. Last week."

"I'll go."

"Mm."

"I will."

"Mm."

His father, across the table, did not look up. His father had been watching the bread and the pipe and the kettle for forty years, and he knew, the way a man knows the weather from the hinge of his knee, when his wife had finished a sentence with the part that wasn't said.

"More stew," his father said. To the pot. To no one.

His mother stood. She ladled more into his father's bowl. She did not, on the way, touch Ash's shoulder, though she passed close enough to.

* * *

Maren stayed an hour after the table was cleared.

She and his mother sat in the kitchen with the tea cooling between them. Ash and his father went into the front room with the copper pipe. His father set the hacksaw in his hand and pointed at the mark.

"Cut," his father said.

He cut. The hacksaw bit, then ran. The pipe gave a clean section. His father lifted it, sighted along it, set it on the table next to the first piece.

"You've still got the wrist."

"I've got more wrist now than I had."

"Mm."

His father did not, generally, give compliments. He did not give one now. He set the second mark.

"The other thing," his father said.

"What."

"The girl. The pump girl."

"Vell."

"She paid for the pump."

"What."

"You said she paid for it. In your letter. You said something about *her* pump."

"She said it. She said *this one's mine.* The first morning."

His father considered. He set the hacksaw on the second mark and didn't cut.

"That's a thing," his father said.

"It is."

"That's a thing you don't engineer for. You engineer for it not happening, and then it happens, and then you have to decide whether to count it."

"Count it how."

"In the design. In what the thing's for. You build a pump because people need water. You don't build it because a girl decides it's hers. But she did. So now what's the pump for."

Ash sat with the question. The hacksaw lay across his father's mark. The flux tin sat with its lid half-on.

"Both," Ash said.

"Mm."

"It has to be both."

"Mm."

His father cut. The blade went through the second pipe. He set the piece next to the first and the second, and three lengths of copper lay on the table in a row, the cuts clean, the edges bright.

"You'll ream those," his father said.

"I will."

"Tomorrow."

"Tomorrow."

His father set the saw down. He wiped his thumb on his trouser leg. He looked across the front room at the kitchen door, where Maren and his mother were still sitting, the murmur of their voices crossing the threshold without arriving as words.

"Your sister's tired," his father said.

"She works too hard."

"Mm."

He didn't say anything else. He looked at the pipe. He picked up the flux tin and put the lid on properly and set it on the shelf above the table, and the conversation closed.

* * *

Maren left without finishing her tea.

She kissed Ash on the head again at the door. She held his shoulder for a second. She told her mother she'd come Twelfth-day.

"Tomorrow morning," Ash said. "Stonecurve. The east square."

"I'll find it."

"There's a baker's. Old baker's. The shop with no oven."

"I'll find it."

She went.

His mother closed the door. She stood with her hand on the latch a moment longer than the closing required. Then she went back into the kitchen, and the cracked cup with Maren's tea half-drunk was on the table, and his mother lifted it and drank the rest herself, standing, before she set it in the basin to wash.

Ash stayed an hour more.

He helped his father ream the three pipes. He helped his mother dry the bowls. He took the kettle from the back of the stove and felt the dent on its side with his thumb, and put it back where she kept it, and did not channel anything into it though it had cooled.

He walked back to Stonecurve in the dark. The reservoir sat. The wall was there when he reached. He didn't reach.

The workshop window was lit when he came down into the square. He went past it and up the stairs to the small room above the shop where Haran had set him a bedroll. He lay down. He slept.

* * *

Maren came at the fourth hour the next day.

She had walked, again. Her boots were dusty to the ankle the way Felden's had been. She came across the square with the brisk step she used in any place she was new, the step that said *I am noticing the layout and I will not need to ask.* She stopped at the basin. She looked at the tap. She turned the tap. The water ran.

She held her hand under it for the time it took to feel the pressure. Closed it. Looked at Ash.

"Gravity?"

"Gravity from the cistern. Cistern fills from the pump. Pump runs on the crank."

"How tall's the cistern."

"Roof of that building. Six meters above the basin."

"Fall's enough."

"Fall's enough."

She nodded once. She walked the length of the square. She walked to the pump. She put her hand on the crank, did not turn it, looked at the linkage, looked at the cylinder, looked at the priming valve. She knelt. She looked up at the underside of the platform.

"What's the stroke."

"Twenty-two centimeters."

"What's the bore."

"Eight."

She did the arithmetic in her head. She nodded again. She stood.

"Show me the heating."

He showed her Block Three. They climbed the steps to the second floor of the second building and stood in a front room with the stove burning low against the inner wall. Maren did not speak for two minutes. She walked the perimeter of the room. She put her palm flat on the inner wall. She held it.

He watched her hand. Her face. The discipline-mask he had known since she was fourteen, the one she put on for any task she was assessing.

"Sealed."

"Sealed."

"With?"

"Reservoir fusion. Plaster's reknit."

"How many seams."

"Sixty-eight in this building."

She did not say anything to that. She moved her palm. She held it on a different spot. Her eyes lost focus the small way a channeler's did when she was checking with the pool, and Ash was watching for what he was watching for now, and he saw the focus go out and he saw it come back, and he saw the half-second longer than it would have taken her a year ago.

He saw it. He did not, in the moment, name what he had seen.

"It's clean work," Maren said.

"Haran said the same."

"Haran's right."

She moved on.

* * *

The training session was at the sixth hour.

Tesha was at the bench with three workers around her. She was showing them the lap. She had the form on the bench, the metal against the form, the mallet in her hand. She tapped the metal over the form's edge in the rhythm Ash had heard from across the square the day before. Three taps and a pause. Three taps and a pause.

"The pause is for looking," Tesha said. "Don't skip the pause. The pause is when you find the buckle before it's a buckle."

The workers watched. One of them, a man Ash didn't know by name yet, made a note on a slip of paper with a stub of pencil. Another, a woman named Reska, nodded once, the way a person nods who is committing a thing to muscle and not to memory.

Maren watched from the doorway. She watched for the length of two demonstrations. She did not interrupt. She did not ask a question.

When Tesha finished and the workers dispersed to the practice forms, Maren said, quietly, to Ash, "She didn't channel anything."

"She doesn't channel."

"She's a channeler?"

"Modest pool. She uses it for her own household. Not here."

"Why not here."

"Because the work has to be doable without her pool. Or the next neighborhood doesn't get it."

Maren absorbed this. He did not see her face change. He saw her, instead, take the information and set it down somewhere it could be retrieved.

"That's the design," she said. "That's the whole design."

"That's the design."

She stood with her hands in her coat pockets. She looked at Tesha across the workshop, at the form, at the mallet, at the lap that was straightening under a worker's hand on the third try.

"Show me the pump again," she said.

* * *

She tried it herself.

He had not asked her to. She walked back across the square with him, stopped at Vell's pump, and stood with her hand on the crank.

Vell was at the basin with a bucket. She looked up.

"Can I," Maren said.

"You can," Vell said.

She didn't move from the basin. She watched.

Maren took the crank in her right hand, set her left against the platform brace, and pulled. The crank moved. She turned it. The cylinder caught water on the second stroke, the way it did, and the third pulled clean. She turned a fourth. A fifth.

By the eighth her shoulder had set into the rhythm. By the tenth her elbow was drifting. She corrected it. By the fifteenth her breath was coming through her teeth.

He watched.

She was strong. She had always been stronger than him. She rode the high terraces twice a week and walked everywhere she could not ride and her arms were the arms of a person who carried her own bags. She should not have been breathing through her teeth at fifteen strokes.

She turned a sixteenth. A seventeenth.

She stopped.

She didn't lock the brake. She let the crank settle on the downstroke and stepped back from the platform. She wiped her palms on her coat. She looked at the cistern indicator, which had not moved as far as her face had expected.

"It's heavier than it looks," she said.

"It is."

"How long's the fill."

"Forty-one minutes."

"Twice a day."

"Twice a day."

She looked at Vell, who was still at the basin with the bucket, who had not moved. Vell's face did not change. Vell's eyes moved from Maren's hands to Maren's shoulders to Maren's face, the same assessment Ash had watched Vell make of every adult who came into the square, and the assessment ended where it ended.

"Twice a day," Maren said again. To herself, perhaps. Or to Vell.

The flicker came across Maren's face.

It was small. It was not for him. It was the discipline-mask coming down for the half-instant before she put it back, and what was under it was not embarrassment and not effort and not the flush of the work. It was something closer to the look she had worn at fourteen, when a problem in arithmetic had come out wrong twice in a row and she had not been able to find where her hand had failed.

She put the mask back. She put it back fast. She turned to Ash with the smile she used for clients.

"Heavier than it looks," she said. "That's a design feature."

"It is."

"You want them to feel it."

"We do."

She nodded. She stepped further back from the pump.

"Tea," Maren said. "Is there tea in this district, Ash, or do I have to go up the hill."

"Haran has tea."

"Haran's tea."

"Haran's tea is fine."

* * *

Haran's tea was not fine.

It was old leaf and harder water than the upper terraces brewed with, and it was hot, which was the thing Haran asked of it. He did not lift his head from the bench when they came in. He looked once at Maren over the rim of his glasses. He said, "Sister."

"Haran."

"Cup's on the shelf. Kettle's on the stove."

He went back to the file.

Maren took down the cup. She poured. She sat on the upturned crate by the door. She did not, immediately, drink. She held the cup between her palms and let it warm her hands. Ash sat across from her on the second crate.

The shop was quiet. The lamp burned. Haran's file moved.

"He doesn't talk," Maren said.

"He talks."

"He doesn't talk to me."

"He'll talk if you ask him a thing he wants to answer."

"What does he want to answer."

"Try one and see."

Maren considered. She looked at Haran's back. She lifted the cup. She drank, made a face, set the cup down on the crate beside her thigh.

"It's gone cool," she said.

"It hasn't."

"It has. I want it warm."

She lifted the cup again. She held it in both hands. Her eyes went the small way out of focus, the channeler's look.

He saw it.

He saw the look go out, and he saw, between the look going out and the cup beginning to steam, a half-beat that wasn't there. A pause. A micro-pause. The space between her intention and the cup's response was longer than the space had ever been when she had warmed his tea on the kitchen table when he was twelve, when she had warmed his tea every morning for the year of the broken arm, when she had warmed his tea in the doctor's waiting room the day they had told him about the magicless test and she had not known what else to do with her hands.

Then the cup steamed. She drank.

The look came back. Her face composed. She drank again, deeper, and set the cup on the crate.

"There," she said. "Better."

"Better."

She did not look at him as she said it. She looked at Haran's back. She looked at the bench. She looked at the cup. The cup steamed on the crate, the small ordinary steam of a cup of tea a channeler had warmed without ceremony, the kind of channeling Ash had watched his sister do a thousand times since his pool had failed when he was eight and hers had not.

He had not, in any of those thousand times, seen the half-beat.

He saw it now. He saw it. He did not, in the moment, name what he had seen.

He filed it.

He filed it under *Maren's working too hard.* He filed it under *the long hours, the late client, the dust on her boots.* He filed it under everything the day had shown him about a sister who had walked the district at his side and had asked good questions and had been, in her assessments, generous. He filed it under the thinness at her wrist and the discipline-mask that had come down at the pump and gone back up.

He filed it because the file had a drawer for it. He filed it because the other drawer was not one he was prepared to open.

"Tell me about scaling," Maren said. "If this works in Stonecurve. What's the next district."

He told her. He told her about the inquiries. He told her about the rural liaison Haran had spent two hours with the week before. He told her about the maintenance chief from the middle districts, the journalist's piece, the letter from the young channelers' collective Dorenne had forwarded with no comment. Maren listened the way she listened to clients, with her hand around the warmed cup, her eyes on his face, her questions placed at the seams.

"And the reservoir," she said.

"What about it."

"Yours. How much per district."

He didn't answer for a moment.

"Three percent a month at construction phase," he said. "More or less. We're working the more-or-less down."

"That's a lot of districts."

"It is."

"How many."

"At three percent. About thirty months of work to take the reservoir to the wall."

She did not say *and then.* She did not say *what if.* She did not say *the math doesn't.*

She drank her tea.

"You'll get it down," she said. "You'll find a way. You always have."

"Maren."

"You always have."

She said it with the smile she used for clients. She said it for him, the way she had said things for him for twenty-six years, the older sister who had taken his fights at school and his first drink at sixteen and his magicless test result and his Wellspring and now this, his third reinvention, with the same generous decisive yes she had given to all of them.

She said it because she loved him and because she could see he was tired and because she wanted him to keep going. She said it and he heard it as a gift and he took it as a gift, and he did not, for the second time in an hour, look closer.

Haran's file moved across the bench. Even strokes.

* * *

She left at the eighth hour.

He walked her to the edge of the district. They stopped at the lower stair where the climb to the second terrace began. The sun was low. The light caught the upper buildings of Stonecurve and turned the new brick of the chimney stacks the color of a brick that had been there longer than it had.

Maren turned. She put her hands on his shoulders. She looked at him.

"It's good," she said. "Ash. It's good work. I want you to know I see that."

"Thanks."

"I see it."

She hugged him.

He felt how thin her shoulders were under the coat. He felt the bone where there had been less bone. He felt the line of her shoulder blade under his palm, the angle of the scapula sharp where the muscle had thinned around it, and he held her a half-second longer than he had meant to, and she, perhaps, held him a half-second longer than she had meant to.

She stepped back.

"Twelfth-day," she said. "Mother's. Don't make me eat alone with them."

"I'll come."

"You'd better."

She turned. She climbed.

He watched her up the first flight. The brisk step was still brisk. The coat hung from her shoulders the way a coat hung from a hanger, and the hanger was thinner than the coat had been cut for, and the second-flight landing took her around the corner and out of his sight before he had finished the thought.

He stood at the lower stair a minute.

He thought *she's working too hard.*

He thought *I'll tell her to eat. Mother will tell her to eat. Mother will feed her on Twelfth-day.*

He thought *the half-beat at the cup.*

He set the half-beat down. He set it on a shelf in the back of the room and he put a cloth over it and he walked away from the shelf, the way a man walks away from a thing he has decided not to look at on a day that has had enough looking in it.

He turned back into Stonecurve.

* * *

The workshop window was lit.

He came across the square in the dusk. The basin tap ran. Vell's pump was still under its cover, the cloth tied at the corners against the night. Tesha had gone home. The square was empty but for an old woman crossing it with a covered pot, slowly, the pot held against her hip the way a woman held a pot she had carried this distance many times.

He stopped at the workshop door.

Haran was at the bench. The same length of brass. The file had reduced it by perhaps a finger's width over the day. The lamp burned. The crooked wick had not been trimmed.

"She came," Haran said. He did not look up.

"She came."

"She liked it."

"She did."

"Sharp."

"Sharp."

Haran filed.

Ash sat on the crate by the door. He did not pick up the cup. The cup was still on the second crate where Maren had left it, half-drunk, the tea cold, the steam long gone.

He looked at the cup.

He looked at the lamp.

"She channels easy," Haran said.

He said it without inflection. He said it the way he had said *cleaner than the Velden job.* He said it the way he said most things he meant, without weight, like a measurement read off a rule.

Ash looked at him.

Haran was filing. Haran had not lifted his head.

"What," Ash said.

"Mm?"

"You said she channels easy."

"She does."

The file moved. Even strokes. The brass shaving curled off in a thin bright ribbon.

"She does," Ash said.

"Mm."

He did not say more. Ash did not ask. The shop was quiet a long while, the file's even sound, the lamp's small sound, the sound of the tap in the square below the door.

Ash sat with his hands open on his knees. The reservoir sat behind his ribs. The wall was there when he reached. He didn't reach.

After a while he stood. He took the cup from the second crate. He carried it to the basin in the back corner where Haran washed his cups, and he poured the cold tea down the basin and rinsed the cup with the tap.

The water ran clean. The cup rinsed clean. He set the cup on the shelf upside down to dry.

He turned back to the bench.

"My father said to tell you," he said, "the third one's the one that's there when the second one's in the pump."

The file paused.

Haran lifted his head. He looked at Ash over the rim of his glasses. The corner of his mouth wasn't a smile. It was something in the same family.

"Tell your father," Haran said, "I keep four."

He went back to the file.

Ash sat on the crate by the door and watched the lamp until the workshop window, from outside, would have looked the way it had looked from the lower stair, lit, steady, a small warmth in the dusk holding what it had been built to hold.

The reservoir sat. The wall held. He did not, that evening, reach for either.

Outside in the square a child laughed once and was hushed. The old woman with the covered pot reached the far side and was gone. The tap in the basin ran.

He went up to the small room above the shop. He lay down. He did not sleep, for a while. He listened to the file moving in the shop below, even strokes, the sound of an old man working a piece of brass into the shape it would need to be by morning.

He thought about Vell's mother. He had not asked his mother's question. He had not asked it of Vell, either, in the day his sister had been in the district. He set the question on the same shelf where he had set the half-beat at the cup, and the shelf, when he set it down, was fuller than it had been that morning.

He slept.
