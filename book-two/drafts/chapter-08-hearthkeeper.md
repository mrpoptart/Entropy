# Chapter 8

The transcripts came in on the second cart of the morning.

Haran set them on the workbench beside the brass shavings without comment, the way he had set the broadsheet down the week before, the way he set most things down. Three sheets, folded once, the printer's ink still faintly fragrant under the workshop's older smells of oil and rosin. A length of twine had bound them. Haran had cut the twine and laid the twine in the small tin where he kept twine, and he had laid the transcripts on the bench, and he had gone back to the file.

Ash came in from the basin with wet hands.

He saw the sheets. He saw Haran's back. He did not, immediately, ask.

He dried his hands on the rag by the door, the way he had learned in the first week to dry his hands on the rag by the door because Haran kept the bench dry, and he sat on the crate with the transcripts on his knee.

The header ran in the upper-tier broadsheet's narrow column type. *COUNCIL HEARING ON EQUITABLE MAGICAL RESOURCE ALLOCATION. Twelfth-day session. Gallery filled to standing.* He read the first paragraph. He read the second. He turned the page.

"Filled," he said.

"Filled."

"To standing."

"Mm."

Haran filed. The shaving curled. Ash read.

The transcripts were not, at first, what he had expected. He had expected anger. He had read, in the past two months, a great deal of anger, in a great many shapes, by a great many hands. Anger was the standing tone of the broadsheets in the lower-tier columns and a recurring tone in the upper-tier columns and a tone, increasingly, in the conversation overheard at any basin in any quarter where two people stopped to fill a kettle. He had become familiar with it. He had become, in a small way, fluent in its variations.

The transcripts were not angry.

They were structured.

The first speaker was named. *Hela Korrin, organizer, the Open Hand.* Different Korrin than the journalist; a third Korrin, perhaps. She had spoken for eleven minutes. Her statement opened with a count: *In the past year, channeled heating in the lower three districts has decreased by an estimated thirty-one percent. Channeled heating in the upper terraces, by available record, has decreased by six.* She gave the source. She named the inspector who had taken the upper-terrace measurements. She named the broadsheet that had aggregated the lower-district reports. She did not raise her voice; the transcript was a transcript, and any raising would have been noted, and there were no notes.

Ash read her statement to the end.

The second speaker was named. *Davan, of the Levelers.* No surname. Eight minutes. The transcriber had marked, in a small note in the margin, *speaker declined channeled amplification, voice carried unaided.* Ash registered the marginal note before he registered the words, and registered the words after, and read them through once and then again.

"They have a platform," he said.

"They have a platform."

"It's published."

"Mm."

"It has a name."

Haran turned. He did not, in turning, set the file down. He held it loose at his side, the way a man held a tool he was about to put back into use.

"The Hearing Platform," Haran said. "The Open Hand and the Levelers, jointly. Six points. The third point is the one to read."

Ash found the third point.

*The decline in available channeling is not a natural disaster. It is a political one. The pools of the powerful have been preserved by precedence, by inheritance, by institutional protection, and by the unwillingness of those holding them to acknowledge the cost their preservation imposes on those who hold less. Above all, the largest single reservoir in the known city, held by one private person, remains undirected by any public framework. The decline did not choose; the choices that follow it must.*

He read it twice.

He set the transcripts down on his knee and looked at the wall above the bench, where Haran had nailed, in the second week, a small tin lid as a place to hang the soft brush, and the brush hung there now, and the lid was the lid, and he looked at the lid until the lid was a thing he could keep looking at.

"They didn't name me," he said.

"They named the largest single reservoir in the known city held by one private person."

"That's not a name."

"It's a name."

Haran went back to filing.

Ash sat with the transcripts on his knee. He read the third point a third time. He read the fourth. The fourth concerned the Council's reconstruction allocations; he skimmed it. The fifth concerned a proposed tariff; he skimmed it. He came back to the third.

*Above all, the largest single reservoir.*

He had, in his life, written sentences like this one.

He had written them at twenty-two, walking back from the upper terraces in winter with his hands red at the knuckles and a notebook in his coat pocket. He had written them about people whose faces he had not, then, known. He had written them in the kitchen at his mother's table, in the margins of pages he had torn out of cheaper broadsheets, in his head walking past the houses on the second crescent where the windows had been warm and his coat had not been warm enough. He had not, at twenty-two, used the word *hoarding.* He had used worse words. He had used the words a person used when the person had been cold for a long time and had stopped, at the cold's particular thickness, expecting to be warm.

He set the transcripts on the under-bench shelf.

The shelf was the shelf with the broadsheet on it and the two letters on it from the week before, and the third item, the small folded napkin he had brought back from the family apartment without being able to remember bringing it back, and now the transcripts, and the transcripts' twine in the tin beside them where Haran had put it.

The shelf was, as he had registered the night before, getting full.

"Sixth-day," Ash said.

"Sixth-day."

"You said read them on Sixth-day."

"I said read the letters on Sixth-day. The transcripts you read today. The letters you read on Sixth-day. The rally you go to or you don't go to."

Ash looked at him.

"Rally."

"Tomorrow. Foundry Yard. The notice is the second sheet."

He had not turned to the second sheet. He turned to it.

A printed broadside, plain, no ornament, the type the lower-tier broadsheets sold at a third the price of the upper-tier sheets and the type that any printer in the lower districts could lay out in a morning. *Public Address. Foundry Yard. Eleventh hour. Davan of the Levelers. All welcome who wish to listen.* No exclamation. No image. The only flourish was the small printer's mark in the lower corner, an open hand, and the open hand was the same open hand that had sealed the blue-wax letter the week before, drawn with the same line.

"All welcome," Ash said.

"All welcome who wish to listen."

"That's a difference."

"That's a difference."

Haran filed.

* * *

He went the next morning in a coat that wasn't his.

The coat was Haran's older one, the brown wool with the lining gone at one elbow, the coat Haran wore when he went down to the basin in the rain and didn't want to ruin the better one. Haran had not said *take it.* Haran had laid it across the crate by the door before he went out, and the laying had been the saying, and Ash had taken it. The coat smelled of Haran's workshop, of brass and rosin and the particular dust of old wool, and when Ash put it on the sleeves were a half-inch short and the shoulders were narrower than his own coat's shoulders, and he buttoned it up and pulled the collar high and walked out into the square and across it without looking at anyone, and nobody looked at him.

The walk to Foundry Yard was an hour.

He took the long way, west across the second terrace's lower edge and down through the cooper's stair and along the canal road, where the morning had not yet fully dried the cobbles and the smell was the smell of damp stone and tar and the cold that came up off the canal in the fall. He walked behind a woman with a basket. He walked behind a young man with a child on his shoulders. He walked behind two old men whose conversation he could not hear and did not try to hear, and he matched their pace because matching their pace meant he did not have to think about pace.

Foundry Yard was the open square between the disused brass foundry on the canal's eastern side and the row of shuttered warehouses behind it. The foundry had not been worked in a decade. The warehouses had been used, after the earthquake, for the temporary storage of rubble and were, now, used for the temporary storage of nothing. The yard's flagstones were uneven where the rubble carts had cut tracks across them, and the tracks had filled with the year's first wet leaves, and the leaves were the brown of leaves that had been wet for a week.

He stopped at the yard's western edge.

The crowd was already there.

Three hundred people. Four. He did not, in the moment, count. He registered the size the way a man registered the weight of a stone he was about to lift, and he registered the shape, which was not the shape of the gathering at Halver's Square the week before. The Halver's gathering had been a fan, narrow at the plinth and widening at the edges, the shape a crowd took when it had assembled around a person. The Foundry Yard crowd was a rectangle. It had been organized. The front rows stood where the front rows had been told to stand; the aisles down the middle were aisles; the printed sheets being passed at the back were being passed in the same direction, hand to hand, by people who had not, at the start of the morning, met each other.

The people on the platform at the far end of the yard had built the platform that morning. The platform was three planks on four crates. The crates were the crates the foundry had left behind. The planks were planks. The platform was, Ash registered, exactly tall enough for a man of average height to be visible to a crowd of four hundred and not so tall that he stood above them.

He moved to the back. He kept his collar up.

A woman beside him, perhaps fifty, in the gray shawl of the cloth-finishers' guild, glanced at him once. Her glance was the glance a person gave a stranger in a crowd in the lower districts in the fall, which was the glance a person gave a stranger to confirm that the stranger was not, this morning, a problem. He nodded. She nodded. She turned back to the platform.

A young man at his other elbow was reading, slowly, one of the printed sheets, his lips moving over the harder words. He was perhaps eighteen. His hands, holding the sheet, were the hands of a tannery apprentice; they had the stain at the cuticles that did not come out for years. He read. He folded the sheet. He put it inside his coat. He looked at the platform.

A speaker was on the platform.

Not Davan. A woman, perhaps thirty-five, in a green coat that had been good once and was, now, a coat. She spoke clearly. She spoke briefly. She introduced the morning. She introduced the platform's six points, one by one, and read each in a steady voice that did not, in any one point, ask the crowd to feel anything.

"The third point," she said.

She read it.

The crowd, on the third point, made a sound.

It was not a cheer. It was not a shout. It was the sound a room of people made when the room had been listening for a sentence for a long time and the sentence had been said. A low, collective murmur, in the throat rather than the mouth, the sound of recognition. The sound moved across the rectangle the way a small wave moved across a pond when a stone had been dropped at one edge: not loud, but covering the surface.

Ash registered the sound.

He had heard it once before in his life, at twenty, in a crowded basin square in winter, when a stranger on a box had said the line that became, for him, the line he wrote in his head on every cold walk for the next three years. He had not, then, made the sound. He had made his own smaller version of it, in his throat, walking away. He had made the sound at himself, for himself, the way a person made the sound at the thing the person had not, until that moment, had words for.

Now the crowd around him made it.

He did not, this morning, make the sound. He stood with his collar up and the borrowed coat too short at the wrists and felt the sound move past him on either side, the way a current moved past a stone in a stream, and the stone did not move with the current, and the current did not stop for the stone.

The woman in the green coat finished the points. She turned. She gestured.

"Davan," she said.

The man came onto the platform.

He was younger than Ash had expected. Perhaps Ash's own age; perhaps a year either side. He was tall but not strikingly tall. He was thin in the way a person was thin who had been thin for years, not the recent thinness of decline. He wore a dark coat, plain, no ornament. His hair was cut short. His face, at this distance, was the face of a person who had been, in the half-hour before stepping onto the platform, listening to the woman in the green coat with the attention of a man who had heard her say it before and wanted to hear how she said it today.

He stepped forward to the front of the planks.

He did not, before speaking, raise a hand. He did not survey the crowd. He waited a beat for the murmur of the introduction to settle, and the beat was not long, and when the beat was over he spoke.

"Good morning."

His voice carried.

It carried without the pressure that channeled amplification put on the air. The crowd at the back, where Ash stood, heard him as clearly as the crowd at the front. Ash registered the carrying the way an engineer registered an efficient mechanism: the man had pitched his voice exactly to the size of the yard, no louder, no softer, and the yard's shape, between the foundry wall on one side and the warehouse fronts on the other, returned the voice to him without distortion. He had picked the yard. He had spoken before in this yard, perhaps, or in yards like it. He knew what he was standing in.

"Thank you for coming."

The crowd, as one, did not respond. The crowd was listening.

"I want to say three things this morning. The first is short. The second is shorter. The third will take the rest of the time we have."

A small ripple of something in the crowd. Not laughter; the acknowledgment of a man who had told them he was going to use their time well.

"The first is this. The platform you just heard read out is not new. The points in it are not new. We have been making these points, in one form or another, in this yard and yards like this one, for three years. Some of you have been with us the whole time. Some of you came this morning because a friend handed you a sheet last week. Both kinds of people are welcome. The platform is the same platform. You have not missed anything. You have arrived in time."

He paused.

"The second thing. The hearing on Twelfth-day. Some of you were in the gallery. Most of you were not. I want you to know what happened there, in plain words, because the broadsheets will tell you in their own words and you will read them and you should, but you should also have ours."

He told them. He told them in five sentences. He told them what the platform's speakers had said and what the Council had said in response and what the Council had not said, and the not-said was the longest of the five sentences. He did not, in the telling, raise his voice. He did not, in the telling, characterize the Council. He stated. The crowd listened, and the listening, Ash registered, was the listening of people who had been coming to this yard long enough to know that Davan would not, in stating, embellish, and that the absence of embellishment was the thing that made the stating land.

"The third thing," Davan said.

He paused. The pause was longer than the first. Ash watched the pause. The pause was the pause of a man who was about to begin the part he had built the morning around, and was, in the half-second before beginning, giving the crowd the half-second to know it.

"Every day," Davan said, "the strong keep what they have, and the weak lose more."

The sentence sat.

He did not repeat it. He did not raise it. He let it sit.

"This is not an accusation. This is not a metaphor. This is a description of what is happening, in this city, in this season, in the houses on the streets between this yard and the canal. The strong have not, in the last year, lost any meaningful fraction of their pools. We have asked. We have measurements. We have published them. The weak, in the same year, have lost between a quarter and a third of theirs. We have measurements for this also. The numbers are in the second sheet you were handed at the door. If you have not read them, read them tonight."

He paused.

"The decline," Davan said, "does not narrow the gap. It widens it. The decline acts on the small pool first. The decline reaches the large pool last. By the time the decline reaches the largest pool in the city, the smallest pools will have been gone for years. The mathematics of this is not complicated. It does not require a Council hearing to confirm. It requires only a person willing to set numbers next to other numbers and report what the comparison says."

The young man at Ash's elbow had taken the printed sheet out of his coat. He was looking at it. His lips were not moving. He was reading the second sheet, which was, Ash registered, the sheet with the numbers.

"Now," Davan said. "I want to address one argument. One only. I will not address the others today; the others have been addressed by others and will be addressed by others. The argument I want to address this morning is the argument of patience."

He waited.

"The argument of patience says: change is coming. Wait. There are people, important people, building the alternatives. There are workshops. There are pumps. There are ingenious mechanical devices that, in a year or in two years or in five years, will replace what is being lost. Wait. You will be helped. You will be saved. The clever and the powerful are working on it."

He paused.

"This argument is offered to us in good faith. I want to be clear about that. The people offering it are not, in general, our enemies. Some of them are our friends. Some of them are people we admire. Some of them have done more, in the past year, with their own hands and their own time and their own resource, than the entire upper terrace has done in a decade."

A small murmur. The murmur was not, Ash registered, agreement. The murmur was the sound of a crowd noting that the speaker had said something the crowd had not, in advance, expected him to say, and the noting was the noting of people who were being asked to keep listening.

"The argument is offered in good faith," Davan said. "And the argument is wrong."

The murmur stilled.

"It is wrong because it asks the wrong question. It asks: *can a better system be built?* The answer to that question is yes. I believe the answer is yes. I have walked through Stonecurve. I have seen the pump. The pump works. The pump is, in the small way a pump can be, beautiful. The answer to *can it be built* is yes."

He waited.

"The right question is: *what do the people who need it do until it is built?*"

A different sound from the crowd. Lower. Closer to the chest.

"Waiting for someone to save us is what got us here," Davan said. "Waiting is what we have done for a generation. Waiting is what our parents did, and waiting is what their parents did, and the result of the waiting is the houses on the streets between this yard and the canal where, this winter, a child will sleep three to a bed because three to a bed is warmer than one. The wait did not produce a saving. The wait produced this morning."

He did not raise his voice. He was building.

"And so we are not, this season, going to wait."

The crowd made the sound again. The low collective sound. The sound of recognition.

It moved past Ash.

He did not, this time either, make it. He stood at the back in the borrowed coat with his hands in the pockets and the sleeves of the coat too short and the wool against his wrists with its smell of rosin, and he registered, beneath the sound, a thing he had not, on the walk down, expected to register.

He agreed.

Not with the conclusion. The conclusion he could already feel the shape of, and the shape of the conclusion was a shape he had been working, in the workshop, against. The conclusion was redistribution; the conclusion was that the largest pool in the city was the resource the city had a claim on; the conclusion was that the man who held the pool, by holding it, was making the choice to widen the gap. He could feel where the conclusion was going. He could feel, also, the steps Davan had not yet taken between this point in the speech and the conclusion, and he could feel that the steps were carefully placed, and he could feel that when the steps were taken the conclusion would land.

He agreed with the diagnosis.

He agreed with it the way a man agreed with a measurement read off a rule. Davan had set the numbers next to the other numbers. The numbers were the numbers Ash had himself set next to other numbers, in the workshop, in the past month, on a scrap of paper he had set down on the under-bench shelf and not picked up again because the result of the comparison had been a shape he had not wanted to look at.

The decline reached the small pool first. The decline reached the large pool last. By the time the decline reached the largest pool, the smallest had been gone for years.

He had not, in the workshop, said this aloud. He had set the scrap on the shelf. The shelf, this morning, was at the far end of an hour's walk, in the workshop where Haran was filing brass, and the scrap was on the shelf, and the shelf was getting full.

*The mathematics is not complicated,* Davan had said.

It was not.

Ash put his hand on the foundry wall behind him.

The wall was the wall of a brass foundry that had not been worked in a decade. The brick under his palm was cold. The cold of the brick was the cold of any brick on a fall morning in the lower districts. The brick did not ask anything of him. The brick was the brick.

He held his hand against it and breathed once, slowly, and looked at the platform.

Davan was speaking. He was, now, on the part of the speech that was the part Ash had felt coming. He was naming the institutions. He was naming the Council. He was not, Ash registered, naming the Wellspring; he had not, in any sentence yet, used the word. He was using *the largest pool.* He was using *the most powerful channeler.* He was using these phrases the way a man used a phrase that did not need a name attached to it because the room knew the name, and the leaving-off of the name was a courtesy that would not, on this morning, be mistaken for absence.

Ash looked at the crowd.

A row to his left. Three women, two men, all under thirty. He did not, at first, recognize them. He scanned. The third woman from the aisle.

Vell.

She was in a coat he had not seen her in, dark, hooded, the hood down. Her hair was tied back. She had a printed sheet in her hand, folded once, the same fold as the sheet the tannery apprentice had folded. She was looking at the platform. She was, like the people around her, listening with the attention of a person being told something that fit the shape of something the person already knew.

She felt the look.

She turned.

Her eyes met his across the four rows of people between them. They held. The borrowed coat and the high collar were not, Ash registered, doing what he had hoped they would do, or perhaps Vell had simply known the shape of his shoulders for two months and would have known him at twice the distance. Her face did not change. She did not nod. She did not, in any visible way, acknowledge him. She looked at him for the length of two breaths, and her face was the face of a fifteen-year-old who had spent the last two months turning a crank in a square he had built and was, this morning, standing in a yard listening to a man explain why the crank was not enough, and the face was not hostile and not apologetic and not asking anything of him at all.

She turned back to the platform.

Ash did not move.

He scanned again. Two rows behind her, on the right side of the aisle, he saw Aren. Aren who had quit on Reska's evening shift the week before. Aren who, Reska had said, was a good worker and would find a crew. Aren had found, this morning, this. He stood with his arms folded across his chest in the posture of a man who was at home in the posture, and beside him stood the boy from the second crew who had quit with him, and the boy was holding the printed sheet, and Aren was not.

Three rows further back, on the foundry side: a woman whose face Ash had seen, in the third week, outside Block Three at the hour when the heating had failed for the second time and she had been one of the neighbors who had come out into the street with a kettle. He did not know her name. She was here. She was listening. Her expression was the expression of a person who, on coming out into the street with the kettle that night, had decided something, and had carried the decision around for six weeks, and was, this morning, hearing the decision said aloud by someone else.

Ash looked at the platform.

Davan was speaking.

"I want to close," Davan said, "with one observation."

He paused.

"There is a man," Davan said, "in the workshop in Stonecurve. Some of you have met him. Some of you work with him. He is, by every measure I have been able to take, a serious person. He is building, with serious people around him, a serious thing. I do not stand here this morning to tell you he is a bad man, or a wrong man, or a man whose work should be opposed. His work should not be opposed. His work, in fact, should be studied. There are pieces of it we will adopt."

He waited.

"But I want to say, plainly, what his work is and what it is not. His work is a proof. His work is a demonstration that there are alternatives. His work is one district, with twenty buildings, and a year of his hands and his reservoir's first six percent. His work is good. His work is also, by his own framework, fifty years from being the answer to the question this yard is asking."

He paused.

"And we do not have fifty years."

The crowd made the sound a third time. It was not louder. It was deeper. It came from further down in the chest, and it covered the rectangle of people the way the first two had covered them, and it moved past Ash on either side and did not, in moving past him, ask him to step into it.

"We do not have fifty years," Davan said, "and we do not, with respect, have one. The strong are not asked to wait for their own resource. The strong have it. The weak are asked to wait for a resource that is being built, in good faith, by a man who is pouring his hands into the making of it, and we honor him for the pouring, and we will not, this winter, freeze for it."

He stopped.

He did not, in stopping, do anything dramatic. He let the last sentence sit. He looked across the crowd once, slowly, the way a man looked across a room of people he had spent the morning with and was about to leave for a while, and then he stepped back from the front of the planks, and the woman in the green coat came forward, and Davan did not bow and did not raise a hand and did not, in any way, acknowledge applause, because there was, at the end of his speech, no applause.

There was the sound.

The sound went on for the length of perhaps three breaths and then it became the sound of a crowd that had heard the morning's speech and was, now, thinking about going home.

The woman in the green coat said something Ash did not hear. He had stopped, at *we will not, this winter, freeze for it,* hearing for several seconds, the way a man stopped hearing during the moment after a small shock, and by the time his hearing came back the woman had moved on to logistics. Sheets at the door. The next gathering. Channels for those who wished to organize in their own districts. Names of organizers in the second tier and the third. She read the names. She read them clearly. The crowd, at the back where Ash stood, was already beginning to shift, in small movements, toward the yard's edges.

He turned.

He did not, in turning, look again at Vell. He did not look at Aren. He did not look at the woman from Block Three.

He walked.

* * *

The walk back took longer than the walk down.

He took, for the second time that morning, the long way: along the canal road, then up through the cooper's stair, then west across the second terrace's lower edge. The morning had become afternoon while he had been in the yard. The sun was over the upper terraces. The cobbles had dried. A bakery in the third district had opened its second oven and the smell of it crossed his path at the corner of the cooper's stair and went on, and he walked through the smell and out of it and on.

He did not, on the walk, think clearly.

He thought of the third point. *Above all, the largest single reservoir.* He thought of the woman in the green coat reading it and the crowd making the sound. He thought of Vell's face, which had been the face of a fifteen-year-old who had decided, sometime in the last week, that turning a crank was not the only thing she was going to do this year, and her face had not been, in deciding, less Vell's face. He thought of Davan's pause before *we do not have fifty years,* the placed pause of a man who had built the speech to put the pause exactly there.

He thought, *he did not name me.*

He thought, *he did not need to.*

He thought, *he was right about the math.*

He thought it walking and he thought it climbing and he thought it past the second terrace's western edge and along the upper line of Stonecurve's outer streets, and when he came around the last corner before the square he was thinking it still, and the square was the square it had been that morning, and the basin tap was running.

He stopped at the edge of the square.

The pump was where he had left it.

It was Vell's pump, because everyone called it Vell's pump, even though Vell was, this morning, three districts away in a yard listening to a speech about why the pump was not enough. The pump's cover had been folded back along the platform's edge. A woman he did not, by name, know was at the crank. She was perhaps forty. She had come, Reska had told him a week ago, from the eastern row, because her own building's water still ran on the upper-line channeling and the upper-line channeling had been thin for two weeks and she had wanted, she had told Reska, to learn the crank in case.

She was cranking.

The crank was the crank. Three full turns to prime; six to lift; the small pause at the top of the stroke that Vell had taught the morning crew to take because the pause let the seal seat. She was doing the pause. She was doing it without thinking about it. She had learned the pause, two days ago perhaps, from someone who had learned it from Vell, and she was doing it the way Reska did the manifold check, which was the way Tesha did the lap-check, which was the way Haran said most things. The water came up the spout in the steady rope it came up in when the pump was working, and a child crouched at the basin's edge with a tin cup, and the child waited the way a child waited for a cup to be filled, and the woman cranked, and the rope of water came up, and the cup filled, and the child stood up and drank.

Ash stood at the edge of the square.

He watched a long minute.

He watched the rope of water. He watched the child drink. He watched the woman at the crank shift her weight to her other foot at the count of twenty, the way Vell had taught the morning crew to shift at twenty, because shifting at twenty meant that by forty the crank-side knee had not begun to ache. He watched the child wipe its mouth on its sleeve and run across the square toward Block Three, and he watched the woman pause for the seal-seat at the top of the stroke, and he watched the rope of water come up.

The pump worked.

Nobody was channeling it.

The pump was, in the small way a pump could be, what Davan had said it was. *Beautiful,* Davan had said. He had used the word. He had used it at a distance, with a serious face, in front of four hundred people, and he had used it as the prelude to *fifty years from being the answer.*

Ash stood at the edge of the square and watched the rope of water and thought, *he is right that it is beautiful and right that it is fifty years and right about the woman cranking and right about the child drinking and right about this winter.*

He thought, *and the answer is still the answer.*

He thought, *and I do not, standing here, know how to say that to the woman from Block Three who is, this afternoon, walking home from the yard with a folded sheet inside her coat.*

He thought it once. He did not, in thinking it, settle anything.

He looked at the pump.

The woman at the crank had not, on his coming into the square, looked over. She was at her count. She was doing the work. The borrowed coat was the coat of a man she had not seen in the square before, and the man had not, on coming in, made a thing of his coming, and she was at her count, and the count was the count.

He crossed the square.

He went past the pump. He did not stop at the pump. He went past the basin and past Block Three's door, where Jora's evening shift would, at the third hour, come in to begin the manifold check the new recruit had practiced the night before. He went up the stair to the workshop.

Haran was at the bench.

He was filing. He had been filing, perhaps, the whole morning. The shavings on the bench had collected into a small drift to the right of the vise, and the file was at a different angle now than it had been when Ash had gone out, which meant that Haran had moved the work, perhaps, twice in three hours, the way Haran moved a piece when the file had taken the high spots off one face and there was, on the next face, more to take.

Haran did not, on Ash's coming in, look up.

"You went," Haran said.

"I went."

"Mm."

Ash took the borrowed coat off. He hung it on the peg by the door, the peg Haran kept for the older coat, and he stood in the middle of the workshop in his shirtsleeves and did not, for a beat, do anything.

"He's good," Ash said.

"Mm."

"He didn't shout."

"No."

"He built it. He built it the way you build a pump. Each piece sat on the piece before."

"Mm."

"He didn't name me. He used phrases instead. He used them precisely."

"Mm."

The file moved. The shaving curled.

"He said the pump was beautiful."

Haran paused. The pause was the pause Haran took when a sentence had landed somewhere in him and he was setting the file's angle by the landing.

"He said the pump was fifty years from being the answer."

Haran turned the piece in the vise. He did not, in turning, set the file down.

"He said both."

"He said both."

"Both can be true."

"They are both true."

Haran filed.

Ash stood in the middle of the workshop. He looked at the under-bench shelf. The shelf had, this morning, the broadsheet on it, and the two letters, and the napkin, and the transcripts, and now, in his hand, which he had not, until this moment, registered as still holding it, the printed sheet a young man at the foundry-yard door had given him on the way out. He looked at the sheet. The sheet was the second sheet, the sheet with the numbers. He had folded it once, on the walk back, in the same fold the tannery apprentice had folded his sheet in, and the fold was the fold the printer had pressed for the folding.

He set the sheet on the under-bench shelf.

He set it on top of the transcripts. The shelf was the same shelf it had been that morning, and the items on the shelf were the items, and one more.

"I agree with him," Ash said.

"Mm."

"With most of it."

"Mm."

"I agree with the diagnosis. I agree with the math. I agree that the people at the back of that yard cannot wait fifty years. I agree that asking them to wait, in the words a man with my reservoir uses to ask them, sounds the way being asked to wait by a man with my reservoir would have sounded to me at twenty-two, which is the way it sounds, which is not a way I want to sound."

"Mm."

"And I think the answer he's selling is wrong."

Haran lifted his head. He looked at Ash over the rim of his glasses, in the way he looked at a piece on the bench when he was deciding whether the piece was ready for the next file.

"You think it's wrong," Haran said.

"I think it's wrong."

"Why."

Ash thought.

"Because the math doesn't change," he said. "Pooling pools doesn't grow them. Sharing a reservoir spends the reservoir faster. The decline reaches the largest pool last because the largest pool is the largest. If you redistribute it, the decline reaches everyone at the same speed. The gap closes by everyone losing together. You haven't built anything. You've leveled the going-down."

"Mm."

"And he knows that."

"Does he."

Ash thought longer.

"He knows the math," he said, slowly. "He doesn't, I think, believe the conclusion. He believes that when the redistribution happens and the math turns out the way the math turns out, the redistribution will have been worth doing, because in the meantime nobody froze."

"Mm."

"And he's not wrong about the meantime."

"No."

"And I don't know how to answer him."

"No."

Haran went back to the file.

Ash stood another minute. He looked at the shelf. He looked at the bench. He looked at the small drift of brass shavings to the right of the vise, and the angle of the file, and the way the lamp on the bench fell across Haran's hands. He registered that the workshop was warm, and that the warmth was the warmth of the small wood stove in the corner that Haran had lit at the third hour before the cart had come, and that the stove had been lit because the morning had been cold and Haran had decided, without saying, that the workshop needed to be warm by the time Ash came back from the yard. He registered the deciding without naming it.

He went to the bench.

He put his hand on the bench, beside Haran's elbow, the way he had put his hand on the bench in the second week when he had not yet, then, known whether the workshop was a place he was allowed to put his hand. The bench was warm where the lamp had been on it. The brass shavings under his palm were the small dry curls of brass that came off a file when the brass was good and the file was sharp, and he had, in the past two months, learned to clean them off the bench with the side of his hand into the small tin, and he did, now, what he had learned. He swept the shavings. He put them in the tin. He set the tin back beside the vise.

Haran did not, in the sweeping, stop filing.

"Sixth-day," Haran said.

"Sixth-day."

"You read the letters Sixth-day."

"I read the letters Sixth-day."

"You think about the yard between now and Sixth-day."

"I think about the yard."

"Mm."

He kept filing.

Ash stood at the bench. He did not, in standing, do anything more. The lamp burned. The file moved. Outside the workshop, in the square, the rope of water came up out of the spout at the count Vell had taught the morning crew to count, and the woman from the eastern row paused for the seal-seat at the top of the stroke, and a child somewhere across the square shouted at another child, and an old woman crossed slowly with a covered pot, and the day, in Stonecurve, was the day the district had been having for two months now.

He registered it.

He registered, also, that the registering was not, this afternoon, the same as the registering of the night before. The night before he had stood in the doorway of Block Three and watched Jora teach a girl the count and had thought *this is what I'm building.* This afternoon he stood at the bench beside Haran's elbow and watched the file move, and the file moved, and the file moved, and he thought the same sentence, and beneath the sentence, where the sentence had been clean the night before, there was now a second sentence, which was the sentence the man on the planks at Foundry Yard had said about the woman walking home with a folded sheet inside her coat.

He held the two sentences.

He did not, in the moment, decide between them.

He looked at the shelf. The shelf was getting full. The new sheet sat on top. The numbers on the sheet were the numbers Davan had read out, and the numbers were the numbers, and the numbers did not, by sitting on a shelf, change.

Haran filed.

The shaving curled off.

Ash, beside him at the bench, picked up the second file from the rack, the small one for the inner curve, and set the angle of his own hand to the angle of Haran's hand, and began, at the piece's other end, to file.

The two files moved in the workshop, on the same brass, at the same angle, and the lamp burned, and the stove burned, and the afternoon went on, and outside the rope of water came up out of the spout, and a child drank, and the woman from the eastern row paused at the top of the stroke for the seal-seat she had been taught two days ago to take.

The shelf was the shelf.

The yard was the yard.

The pump worked.

The math, on the new sheet, sat on top.

He filed.
