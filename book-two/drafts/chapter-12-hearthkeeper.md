# Chapter 12

The crier on the corner of Halver's Square was reading the ordinance for the fourth time that morning.

Ash had stopped at the edge of the square because his coat had caught on the stack of broadsheets pinned to the lamppost and he had not wanted to tear the cuff getting it loose. The pin was a long brass tack that someone had driven through the post into a previous tack and the previous tack into the wood, and the lamination of paper and pins was thick enough that the lamppost looked, at this hour, less like a lamppost and more like a tree that had been growing news. He worked the cuff free. He did not look at the broadsheets. He had stopped looking at broadsheets two weeks ago, around the morning a Council clerk on the second floor of the Hall had pinned one to her own door with the headline turned in.

The crier was a small woman in a grey wool cap. She had a copper bell that she did not ring, because she did not need to anymore, and she was reading the ordinance as if it were a recipe.

"Section three," she said. "Elder channelers within designated districts shall contribute a portion of daily pool. Section four. The portion shall be set by the district allocation council, not to exceed one tenth of measured capacity. Section five. Contribution shall be voluntary in form and compulsory in fact. Refusal shall be reported to the Levelers' allocation board for review."

A man at her elbow corrected her, mildly. "Compulsory in form."

"Form and fact," she said. "It's the third printing. They changed it."

"They changed it."

"Yesterday."

The man whistled, low, and walked on.

Ash stood at the lamppost with his cuff in his fingers and read, against his intention, the bottom of one broadsheet. *Three seats. Halver's South, the Cooperage Ward, the Lower Terrace.* The names were set in a black face larger than the body type. Underneath, in a smaller hand, a list of votes. *For: forty-one. Against: thirty-three. Abstaining: two.* The numbers were small, the way a Council vote was small. The small numbers had moved a wall.

He pulled his cuff straight and went on.

The Cooperage was a street and a half away. He did not look at it. He went the other way, up the canal stair, past the dry towpath, into the lower run of the Hall district where the offices sat above the street in the long row of grey stone Dorenne's predecessors had favored for the same reason they favored grey wool: it did not stain. He had been summoned to the Hall at the second hour. Her note had come the night before, tucked under the workshop door so neatly that Haran had asked, without lifting his eyes from the lathe, whether the note had been delivered or shelved.

"Delivered," Ash had said.

"By whom."

"Tessaly."

Haran had nodded once. "Mm." Then: "She walked it herself."

"She walked it herself."

The note had said: *I would appreciate an hour. Tomorrow at the second. My office. D.* No salutation. No closing other than the initial. He had read it twice and put it in the inside pocket of his coat and gone back to the bench.

He went up the steps to the Hall.

* * *

Tessaly was at the desk outside Dorenne's door.

She had her hair pulled back in the same long single braid she had worn the morning he had walked out of this office four months ago, and she had a pen in her right hand, and the pen was hovering above a survey she had not been working on when she heard him on the stair. The hovering was a signal he had learned to read in the first year. It meant she had been waiting.

"Ash."

"Tessaly."

"She's in. She's expecting you."

"I know."

"Tea?"

"No, thanks."

"There's tea."

"I know."

A small pause. Tessaly set the pen down at the angle she set pens down, parallel to the long edge of the survey, point inward, and laid her hand flat on the desk for a half a second before she stood.

"It's good to see you," she said.

"You too."

"Go in."

He went in.

The office had not changed. The big map of the city on the long wall, the lattice grid penciled over in the careful red-grey crosshatch she used for allocation overlays, the survey table by the window with its three half-rolled drawings weighted at the corners by the same three river stones she had used since his first month. The smell was the smell of the office. Pencil shavings, the particular faint bitterness of the ink she preferred, and underneath those, very faint, the cold-metal smell of her samovar, which had been on a back warmer for an hour and was idling at temperature.

She was at the survey table, not the desk. That was the change.

The survey table meant working. The desk meant receiving.

"Ash," she said. She did not look up. She finished a notation, set the pencil parallel to the long edge of the drawing, and turned. "Thank you for coming. Sit, if you'd like. Or stand. The drawings will hold longer if I leave them weighted."

"Stand's fine."

"Stand's fine."

She crossed to the samovar and poured two cups without asking. She brought one to him. The cup was the heavy white ceramic she kept for the office. The handle was warm and the body was warm and the heat in his palm was the steady single-temperature heat of a vessel that had been kept at a held draw for an hour, not the climbing heat of a cup that had been freshly warmed.

"How is Haran."

"Well."

"And the workshop."

"Running."

"And the redesign at Six."

"Holding at eighty."

"At eighty."

"At eighty."

She nodded. The nod was short. She lifted her own cup, drank a careful half-mouthful, set it on the windowsill, and turned to face him. Her hands stayed at her sides. He had watched her, in the first year, handle a council briefing and a contractor and a senior engineer in the same fifteen minutes, and her hands had moved across the survey through all of it. The stillness of her hands now was the loudest thing in the room.

"I read the ordinance this morning," she said. "The third printing."

"I saw the third printing."

"At the lamppost?"

"The crier was reading it."

"Mm."

She turned, half a step, toward the map. He had thought she would. The body turn before the words was the move. It was the gentle reorientation she had built her career on, the planet pulling the moon, and he had felt it in his chest the first dozen times she had used it and felt it now, the way he felt a draft at a door.

"The northern aqueduct," she said. "The main line that feeds the upper hospital and the eastern row of cisterns. It draws on a sequenced contribution from twenty-three elder channelers in five districts. The contribution is voluntary. Has been voluntary since I built the schedule eleven years ago. The contribution is also entirely predictable, because the channelers know each other, the schedule is published, and the system is engineered around their availability."

"I know the schedule."

"I know you know the schedule."

He waited.

"Two of the five districts are now subject to the redistribution ordinance." She said the word *redistribution* without inflection. The flatness was a tell. "Five of the twenty-three channelers are now compelled to contribute a portion of daily pool to allocation councils that did not exist a month ago and do not coordinate with my office. Section four sets the cap at a tenth. Section five permits review. The review boards have already, in two districts, raised the cap to a quarter for individual channelers identified as, the language is, *under-contributing.*"

"I read that."

"You read that."

"Yes."

"Then you understand. Five channelers reallocated. Twenty-three become eighteen. The schedule does not work at eighteen. It works at twenty-one with margin. At eighteen the aqueduct degrades to the failure threshold within four months."

"The hospital."

"The hospital is on the aqueduct. The hospital heating draws from the aqueduct's thermal exchange. The structural reinforcements at the lower terrace, the ones I put in after the earthquake, draw from the same allocation grid because I built the grid to share. I designed it to share, Ash. I built a coordinated framework precisely because no individual elder pool is reliable anymore, and a system of twenty-three is more reliable than any one of them."

"I know."

"You know."

"Yes."

A small pause. She had not raised her voice. She had not changed her tempo. The pause was the pause she made when she was deciding which tool to reach for next, and he watched her hand move, very slightly, toward the pencil on the survey table and stop short of it. She put the hand back at her side.

"If they break the allocation framework," she said, "the reconstruction stalls. Districts that depend on coordinated channeling, the hospital heating, the aqueduct network, the structural reinforcements, all of it becomes unpredictable. I can manage unpredictability for a week. I can manage it for a month at cost. I cannot manage it for a year, because the systems were not built for it, and rebuilding them in the time I would have is not a project that exists."

"You want me to oppose the ordinance."

"I want you to speak."

"To speak."

"Publicly. Once. The Wellspring on the steps of the Council, or in the lower square if the Council is uncongenial. A statement that the framework is the framework. That mandatory expenditure breaks the engineering. That the hospital heating depends on volunteers and the volunteers depend on a schedule and the schedule depends on the framework holding. You have the standing, Ash. You are the man who lived without magic for twenty-three years and now has more of it than anyone. If you say, on the record, that this ordinance is not the way, the moral weight of the movement collapses by half overnight. By half. I have run the projections."

"I'm sure you have."

"I have."

He drank from the cup. The tea was the temperature it had been when she handed it to him. It would be the temperature it had been when she handed it to him for as long as he held it, because she was holding the warm at the samovar in a back-of-the-mind draw, the way she had held the warm at every meeting in this office for a decade, the kind of accommodation he had stopped noticing in the second year and had been noticing again, lately, every time he set foot in the Hall.

"No," he said.

She did not move.

"No," she said. The repeat was not a question. It was a placeholder she set down, to look at, before she went on.

"No," he said again. "I won't oppose the ordinance. I won't speak against the Levelers. Not on the steps. Not in the square. Not in the gallery. Not at all."

"May I ask why."

"You may."

She waited.

"I lived without magic for twenty-three years, Dorenne. You're right that that gives me standing. It gives me one kind of standing. The other kind is what I remember. I remember what it was like to watch the people who could keep themselves warm keep themselves warm and tell me to be patient. I remember being told the framework would fix it. The framework, the schedule, the allocation. I waited inside that framework my whole life and the framework did not, in any year of it, deliver me a degree of warmth I had not earned by walking somewhere I could borrow it. If I stand on a step and tell those people, the people in the canal quarter, the people whose windows I have counted, that the framework is the framework and they should be patient, I am the man I spent twenty-three years hating."

"That is a narrative."

"It's my narrative."

"It is. It is your narrative and it is true and it does not change the engineering."

"I'm not arguing the engineering."

"You are not arguing the engineering, and the engineering is what is going to fail. Ash. I am asking you, not as the Director, I am asking you as the person who watched me build the schedule. The schedule is not an ideology. It is a list of pipes and contributions and times of day. The pipes do not know that the framework is unjust. They are pipes. They will fail at the failure threshold and the hospital will lose heating, and I will not be able to channel the hospital warm myself, because my pool will not stretch that far, and the Levelers will not channel the hospital warm because the hospital is on the other side of the line they have drawn, and the patients in the lower ward will be cold. I am telling you what is going to happen. I am asking you to help me prevent it."

"I know what you're asking."

"Then help me."

"I can't."

"You won't."

"I won't."

The sentence sat in the room. She did not, in the sitting of it, reach for the pencil. He marked the not-reaching. Her hand had moved, very slightly, the way a hand moved when it was about to do a thing it had decided not to do, and had stopped, and the stopping was hers and not his. She was holding the temperature.

"Then you're choosing," she said, "to let the transition framework collapse for the sake of your own comfort with the narrative."

He did not answer.

She did not push.

He drank a little more of the tea. The tea was the same temperature. She drank a little more of hers. The samovar in the corner gave its small held hum that meant the back warmer was, still, drawing.

"I am sorry I cannot help you," he said, finally. "On this one."

"Please do not apologize. It would suggest that one of us has been wronged, and I do not believe that to be the case."

"All right."

"The hospital is on Lyle Street. In case the question becomes relevant later."

"I know where the hospital is."

"Of course."

She set the cup on the windowsill. She turned half a step, toward the survey table, the way she had turned at the start. The body turn was the move and the move was, this time, the close.

"Tessaly will see you out."

"I can find the door."

"Of course."

He set the cup on the side table near the door. The cup was still warm. He paused with his hand on the handle.

"Dorenne."

"Yes."

"The schedule. The twenty-three. If you give me the names, I'll talk to them. Not to oppose the ordinance. To see what can be coordinated voluntarily, on the side of it. With the council. Without the council. If they want."

A pause. She did not turn from the survey table.

"That is not what I asked for."

"I know."

"It will not be enough."

"I know."

"I will send you the names."

"Thank you."

"Ash."

He did not turn. He waited with his hand on the door.

"It is good to see you," she said. The first name was the first name. The temperature was the temperature. The line she had given him at the start had been a polite warmth. The line she had given him at the end was a half-degree colder, and the half-degree was the entire weather of the office. He let it stand. He opened the door.

Tessaly was at the desk. She did not stand. She held the pen at the angle, point inward.

"Ash."

"Tessaly."

"The tea."

"It was good."

"Mm."

He went down the stairs.

* * *

The lower city at the eleventh hour smelled of cold stone and woodsmoke and, today, of cabbage, because the kitchens behind the long row of tenements above the canal had pulled their winter pots out for the first time and the cabbage was the cheap one. He went down through the canal stair and along the towpath because the towpath was where he walked when he did not want to walk, and the towpath was empty at this hour, and he could feel, in the pacing of it, the breathing his lungs had not been doing in the office.

He came out at Halver's Square.

A crowd was at the south corner. Not large. Twenty people, maybe. They were standing around a man on an upturned crate. The man was reading from a sheet of paper. He held the paper in his left hand and he was using his right to point.

Ash slowed.

He did not stop. He drifted across the square at the angle of a man crossing the square, and he caught the words as he passed.

"Ennick Vorel," the man on the crate read. "Apartment seven, twelve Cooper Lane. Pool capacity, measured at the last assessment, third quartile. Reported contribution to district allocation, the last cycle, none. None reported. None recorded. None observed. Ennick Vorel, your neighbors are watching. Ennick Vorel, your name is on the list. Ennick Vorel, your address is on the list."

A woman next to Ash, on the cobbles, was nodding along. Not vigorously. Just the slow nod of a person being read a list of grievances she had carried in her own pocket.

"Hoarder," the man on the crate said, conversationally, the way he might have said *neighbor.* "We use the word, friends, because it is the word. Ennick Vorel is a hoarder. Twelve Cooper Lane is the house of a hoarder. The list is not long. It does not need to be long. The list is the list."

He moved on to the next name.

Ash kept walking.

At the corner of Cooper Lane, three boys were chalking on the wall of number twelve. They had drawn a long narrow box around the door and a number inside it, the number seven, and the word *HOARDER* in capitals with the H reversed because the boy doing the H had been working from inside the box and had run out of room. The boys were not laughing. They were chalking the way boys chalked who had been told that this was a thing to do that mattered, and they were doing it with the specific thoroughness that boys gave to mattering. One of them had a piece of slate in his pocket and was checking, every few letters, that he had the spelling right.

A woman in a fourth-floor window across the street was watching them. She had her hand at the curtain. She did not call down.

Ash went on.

He passed the market three blocks further. The market was the morning market, half closed already, the long stalls being broken down, and at the cabbage stall a small argument was taking place. A man was holding a coin out to the woman behind the stall. The woman was not taking the coin.

"It's good copper," the man said.

"Take it back, Renn."

"It's good copper."

"It's good copper. I'm not selling to you."

"Mira."

"I'm not selling to you, Renn. Not this week. Not next week. You contribute, you eat. You don't, you don't. Take it down the row. Sella will sell to you. Sella has not made up her mind about the list."

"I'm on the list?"

"You're on the list, Renn."

"Mira, my pool is half what it was last winter."

"Then contribute half. The cap is a tenth. Half of half is more than a tenth. The numbers are what the numbers are."

"Mira."

"Take it down the row."

The man stood at the stall a long second. He did not put the coin away. He did not, exactly, leave. He stood with the coin between his thumb and forefinger as if the coin had become a thing he was being asked to evaluate, and he was, in the standing, evaluating it.

"I'll come back tomorrow," he said.

"Come back when you've contributed."

"Mira."

"Tomorrow, Renn."

He went down the row. He did not stop at Sella's. He went past Sella's. Ash watched him to the end of the row, where he turned, and where, at the turn, his shoulder went into the wall a half-second the way a shoulder went into a wall when a man needed a wall, and he stood there for a count of two and went on.

Ash stood at the cabbage stall a moment longer. The woman, Mira, looked at him.

"Buying?"

"No."

"Then move."

He moved.

He walked the long way back to the workshop. He did not, on the way, count windows. He had been counting windows for two months and today the counting did not feel like the work. Today the counting felt like a thing he did with his eyes to keep his eyes from doing other things, and he stopped, at the corner of the cooper's lane, and put his hands in his pockets and walked with his eyes on the cobbles.

* * *

Haran was at the lathe.

The lathe was running and not running. Haran had it on the slow gear and was working a piece of ash, the wood, into a handle that was already a handle and did not need any more work, and the lathe was singing the slow single note it sang at that gear, and Haran's hands were not on the piece. They were on the rest. He had the gouge on the rest and the gouge was not cutting.

Ash hung his coat.

"Mm," Haran said.

"Mm."

"How was the Hall."

"Cold."

"Mm."

Ash crossed to his bench. He stood at it. He did not pick anything up. He stood with his hands flat on the bench top and his eyes on the grain of the bench, which had darkened, in the four months, to a color he could not have named in the first year. His knuckles were white at the bench.

Haran moved the gouge from the rest. He set it on the small ledge above the lathe, in the place gouges went when they were resting between cuts. He did not turn the lathe off. The lathe sang.

"She asked," Haran said. Not a question.

"She asked."

"And."

"I said no."

"Mm."

A pause. The lathe sang. Haran reached behind him without looking and lifted his cup from the shelf and drank and put the cup back, and the cup made the small clay sound it made on the shelf.

"You'll see Davan today," Haran said.

"Tonight."

"Tonight."

"At the Cooperage."

"Mm."

A longer pause.

"You don't owe me the report, Ashlyn," Haran said.

"I know."

"But."

"But what."

"But the door is unbolted."

Ash looked up. Haran was not looking at him. Haran was looking at the piece on the lathe, which was the handle that did not need any more work, and Haran's mouth had the small set it had when he was holding a sentence that he was not going to say, and the holding was the shape of the sentence, and the shape was: *it has been unbolted for a while now and I am noting that I have not bolted it.*

Ash nodded.

He picked up the small file from the bench. He did not file anything. He held the file. He put it down.

"I'm going to take a walk," he said.

"Take the long one."

"I will."

He took the long one. He walked the river and he walked back. When he came in again Haran had the lathe off and was sweeping shavings into a tin, and Ash sat at the bench and worked, for two hours, on the gear-train that had been the gear-train since the second month and that did not, at the end of the two hours, look any different, and the working had been the work he had needed to do, which was to put his hands on something that would not move unless he made it move.

At the eighth hour he washed his hands and put on his coat.

"Ashlyn."

"Yes."

"He's not at the workbench tonight."

"I know."

"It's not the workbench."

"I know."

Haran said *mm* and turned back to the tin.

* * *

The Cooperage at the eighth was lit.

The yard had two long oil lamps on poles at the gate and a third on the kiln-cart rail and a fourth, newly bracketed, above the door of the inner shed, and the shed door, which had been the warped one, had been taken off and replaced with a new one in pale unpainted pine that had not yet weathered to the color of the rest of the place. Ash stopped at the gate and looked at the new door. The new door had a brass plate at eye height. The plate said, in straight even letters, *Office of the Allocation Council, Cooperage Ward, Halver's South. Hours: by posting.*

He went in.

The yard was busy. Not crowded, busy. Six or seven people at a long trestle table near the kiln-cart rail with two lanterns on the table and a third on a stool, and a stack of paper and a stack of slates and a small open ledger with a brass corner. A young woman was at the ledger. She had a pen and she was making entries and she was not looking up. At the far end of the table, two men were talking quietly with a third, who was holding a sheet and listening with the particular concentration of a man who was being explained something he had not asked to have explained.

Davan was at the inner shed.

He was standing in the doorway. He had a lantern at his shoulder, hung on a peg, and he was talking to two men Ash did not know and to Korel, who Ash knew, and Korel was nodding the way Korel nodded, which was the slow once-and-down nod of an old canal-lockhand who had been listening to plans for forty years and had concluded that nodding was a survival skill. Korel saw Ash first.

Korel touched Davan's elbow.

Davan turned.

The turn was not the turn of the workbench three nights ago. Three nights ago Davan had been at Ash's bench with his sleeves rolled and his hand on a wrench and his face the open patient face it had been since the first conversation. The turn now was the turn of a man at a doorway with a lantern at his shoulder and a brass plate on the door, and Ash registered the difference before Davan had finished the turn.

"Ash."

"Davan."

"You're early."

"You said the eighth."

"I said the eighth. I was going to be at the table at the half. Come in. Korel, give me a quarter hour."

"Take the half," Korel said. "Liss has the table."

"The half, then."

Davan stepped back from the doorway. Ash followed him into the inner shed.

The shed had been the inner shed three months ago, when he had first come into the yard. The inner shed three months ago had been a storage room. Tonight it was a room with a long table and four chairs and a survey of the lower districts pinned to the long wall, and the survey had pencil marks on it that were not Dorenne's pencil marks but had the same purposeful crosshatching, and Ash recognized, in the crosshatching, a thing he had seen Davan draw once on a napkin at the workbench, and he recognized it the way a man recognized his own handwriting in a stranger's pocket.

"Sit," Davan said.

"I'll stand."

"Sit, Ash."

Ash sat.

Davan sat across from him. He did not pick up a paper. He folded his hands on the table the way he had folded them at the workbench, the same fold, and the fold was the same fold, and the rest of him was different.

"You came from the Hall," Davan said.

"This morning."

"Mm. We knew you were summoned. Tessaly walks the notes herself."

"You have someone watching Tessaly."

"We have someone walking past the Hall at the second hour. It's the same someone who walks past the Hall at the second hour every day. It's not surveillance, Ash. It's a clerk on a route."

"All right."

"What did she ask you."

"To speak."

"Against us."

"Against the ordinance."

"Same thing, this week."

"Same thing this week."

A pause. Davan watched him. Ash watched the survey on the wall behind Davan's head. The crosshatching at Halver's South was thick. The crosshatching at the Lower Terrace was thicker. The Cooperage Ward had a small red mark at the center of it that he could not, from where he sat, read.

"What did you tell her," Davan said.

"No."

"Mm."

"I didn't tell her yes either."

"Meaning."

"Meaning I'm here."

Davan nodded. Once. The nod was a public nod. It was not the nod he had used at the bench. Ash watched himself watching the difference and put it down.

"Davan."

"Yes."

"The list."

"The list."

"The names. The addresses. The boys at twelve Cooper Lane chalking *HOARDER* on a door."

"You saw that."

"I saw that."

"Ennick Vorel."

"That was the name."

"Mm."

Davan unfolded his hands. He did not lean forward, exactly. He set his elbow on the table and the hand at the wrist and rested the wrist on the table, and the gesture was a man settling into a thing he had explained before.

"Ennick Vorel," he said, "is the cousin of a Council clerk. Third quartile pool. He warmed his own apartment and his sister's apartment through last winter, and he warmed the kitchens of the Coopers' Guild because the Coopers' Guild paid him. He has not, in eight months, contributed an ounce to anything that did not directly benefit him or someone with money. The list is not a punishment. It is a publication. We are publishing what is already true. The boys with the chalk are doing what the boys with the chalk were going to do anyway, the moment someone told them which door."

"That's not a defense of the chalk."

"It is not a defense of the chalk. The chalk is the chalk. The chalk will wash off."

"The cabbage stall is not the chalk."

"The cabbage stall."

"Mira. At the morning market. She would not sell to a man because the man was on the list. She told him to come back when he had contributed."

"Mira's a fitter at the second cistern. She works the cooperage flow on Wednesdays. Her brother lost his apprenticeship last year because the elder channeler at the dye works wouldn't share. She has reasons."

"I'm not asking about her reasons."

"You're asking about a vendor making her own choices about her own stall."

"I'm asking about a man with a coin in his hand standing in front of a stall in the morning market and being turned away because his name is on a list."

Davan said nothing for a long second.

"What would you have us do, Ash?"

The question was the question. It was not the workbench question. It was the question of a man who had been asked it before, who had answered it before, who had the answer already sitting on his tongue and was not going to put it down.

"I don't have a clean answer."

"Then let me give you the one I have. What would you have us do? Ask nicely? We tried asking. We tried organizing. We tried appealing to the Council. Nothing changed until we made the powerful uncomfortable. Now things are changing. Are you asking me to stop because the methods are wrong, or because the methods are working?"

Ash did not answer.

"I will accept either answer," Davan said. The voice was the public voice. The public voice was, tonight, a voice without a smile in it. "I will accept *the methods are wrong* and we will sit at this table and I will hear out your alternative. I will accept *the methods are working* and we will, at least, be honest about what we are arguing about. I will not accept the two of them braided. The braid is what the Hall does. The Hall has been doing the braid for thirty years. I am not the Hall."

"You are not the Hall."

"I am not the Hall, Ash. I am sitting in a room that did not exist three months ago. I am sitting at a table with a survey on the wall that is going to be the survey the lower city runs on, by spring, whether the Hall agrees with it or not. The people at the trestle in the yard have been working since the seventh hour and they will be working until the eleventh and they are not, any of them, paid. We are doing what we said we would do. It is working. You came tonight because you wanted to see it. Look at it."

He gestured, slightly, at the wall.

"Look at it, Ash."

Ash looked at it.

The crosshatching was the work of a person who had taken Dorenne's framework apart and re-laid it from the bottom. The Lower Terrace had a contribution schedule he could read across the room. The Cooperage Ward had a heating plan that ran on pooled half-tenths in a sequence he had not seen before but that, sitting in the Cooperage at the eighth hour with the new lamps lit and the new door bolted and unbolted, was, plainly, working.

"It's good work," he said.

"It is good work."

"It's not the Hall's work."

"It's not the Hall's work. The Hall's work was good once and is no longer adequate. We have not pretended this is the Hall's work."

"Davan."

"Yes."

"The hospital."

"The hospital is on the list."

"The list of what."

"The list of allocations to be reviewed. Not redirected. Reviewed. The hospital pulls from twenty-three elder channelers across five districts. Five of those channelers are now under the ordinance. The ordinance does not redirect their hospital contributions. The ordinance establishes that their other contributions, the ones to private heating, to the Coopers' Guild, to the dye works, are the contributions subject to redistribution. The hospital is not in that bucket. The hospital is in the keep-bucket. We are not unaware that there is a hospital."

"She didn't tell me that."

"She wouldn't. The keep-bucket is not the part of the ordinance that helps her case."

Ash put both hands flat on the table.

He pressed them down.

"Davan."

"Yes."

"If I came tomorrow morning and stood in your yard and told you I would coordinate with you, not as an opponent, not as a partisan, but as a man with a reservoir who could carry the keep-bucket in months when the schedule fell short, would you take the offer."

A pause.

"I would take the offer."

"And."

"And I would not stop publishing the list."

"I know."

"I would not stop the chalk. The chalk wears off. The list does not. The list is the thing that is changing the city, Ash. Not your reservoir. Yours is one man. The list is a city remembering it has neighbors."

"I know."

"Then you know."

"I know."

Ash stood.

He stood the way he stood when he had, in his head, sat down in a place and gotten up from it, and he had been sitting in this place, in some form, since the towpath in Chapter Eight. He looked at Davan across the table. Davan did not stand. Davan watched him, the patient watch, the watch that had been the workbench watch in a different shape.

"I'll come tomorrow morning," Ash said.

"Come at the seventh."

"Seventh."

"There's a worker I'd like you to talk to before you leave tonight."

"Tonight."

"Tesha. She came over from your district last week. She's at the trestle. She has a question for you and I told her, when she came in, that I would not answer it for her. I would let her ask the man himself. If he came."

"Davan."

"Yes."

"You knew I'd come."

"I hoped."

He went out.

* * *

Tesha was at the end of the long trestle.

Ash had known her face in the second month of the district. She was the woman who had taken over the second pump after the first crew had rotated, the one who had asked Tova, the morning of the seal failure, whether the seal had been brass or fused, and had nodded when Tova said fused and had gone on to mix the patch compound without further comment. She had a long face and a steady mouth and the kind of hands that did not, at rest, look like hands that had ever rested. She had a small slate and a pencil at the slate and she was, when he came up, finishing a column of figures.

"Ash."

"Tesha."

"Sit."

He sat.

She did not look up immediately. She finished the column. She drew a line under the column. She wrote, beneath the line, a single number, and she circled the number, and she set the pencil down.

"You came," she said.

"I came."

"Davan said you would. I told him you wouldn't."

"You told him I wouldn't?"

"You hadn't been by the district in eight days. I thought you'd gone to ground."

"I was at the workshop."

"That's the same."

"It's not the same."

"It's the same, from where I sit."

He almost smiled. He did not, exactly. Tesha did not, exactly, smile back. The not-smiling between them was the temperature of the workbench at the second hour of a job that was going right.

"Tesha. Davan said you had a question."

"I have a question."

"All right."

She did not ask it. She picked the pencil back up. She turned the slate over. She drew a small box on the back of the slate, a square, and inside the square she drew a smaller square, and inside the smaller square she drew a circle.

"The pump in Building Six," she said. "I've been working on it with Mira. The new patch. Eighty-percent-Mira's-hand, twenty-percent-the-original. We took it apart on the third and we put it back together on the fourth and we ran it on the fifth and on the seventh it stuttered and on the eighth I rebuilt the seat by hand. The seat is rough. The pump runs."

"It runs."

"Eighty-three. We measured. Up from seventy-eight. We're working it."

"That's good work."

"It's all right work. Mira is better at the seat than I am. I'm better at the housing. We'll get to ninety by spring. Maybe not. Maybe eighty-eight. The numbers will be the numbers."

"Tesha."

"Yes."

"The question."

She set the pencil down again. She looked at him, fully, for the first time.

"I came to the meeting last week. The Cooperage one. The Tuesday one, with Korel. I sat at the back. I listened. I came home and told Mira about it and Mira asked me what I thought. I told her I thought Davan was right about the elder channelers and the dye works and the cousin with the chalk on his door. I told her I thought you were right about the pump. I told her I thought the eighty-three was a number that mattered. I told her both of those things in the same sentence and Mira looked at me and said, why are you telling them to me like they cancel."

"Mm."

"So I came back. I came back to the trestle. I sat down with Korel and I asked him how to enroll, and he enrolled me, and I have been, since the seventh of the month, attending Tuesdays. I am also at Building Six on Wednesdays. I am at the workbench on Thursdays. I am at this trestle on Fridays."

"That's a lot of days."

"That's the days."

She watched him.

"The question," Ash said.

"The question is. Why can't we do both?"

He did not answer.

He did not answer because the answer he had, the long-form answer, the one with the framework collapse and the schedule and the dependency problem, the one with the *you can't redistribute what isn't there,* the one with Davan's sister Nessa on her bobbin, the one with the eighty-three percent pump, was an answer that, sitting across a trestle from Tesha at the eighth hour with the lanterns on the table and the open ledger at the woman's elbow halfway down the bench, dissolved on the way to his mouth like sugar in tea.

"Tesha."

"Yes."

"I don't have a good answer."

"That's not an answer."

"It's not an answer."

"All right."

She picked the pencil back up. She drew, beside the box-and-square-and-circle, a small line, and at the end of the line a small dot, and she did not name what the line and the dot were.

"I'm going to do both," she said. "I told Mira I would. I'm telling you I am. I want to know if I'm welcome at the bench on Thursday."

"You're welcome at the bench on Thursday."

"Good."

"Tesha."

"Yes."

"You're welcome on Wednesday too."

"I know."

"I want to be clear about that."

"I know, Ash."

She erased the line and the dot with the side of her hand. She did not erase the box and the square and the circle.

"Go home," she said. "It's late."

"I'll go home."

"Go to your father's. You haven't been."

"How do you know I haven't been."

"Maren told me. At the cisterns. Last week."

"Maren talks to you."

"Maren talks to everybody who asks. Go home, Ash."

He stood.

He stood and he looked, once, at the survey on the wall through the open shed door, and at Davan in the doorway of the inner shed talking to a man who was holding a paper and listening, and at Korel at the kiln-cart rail with his hands in his pockets the way a man stood who had decided everything thirty years ago and was watching the next thirty get decided by other people. Davan saw him. Davan nodded once. Ash nodded back.

He went out the gate.

* * *

The street was cold.

He went up past the canal stair and past the lamppost with the brass tacks and past the closed cabbage stall, where a single lantern still burned on a crate at the back because Mira had not finished her counting. He did not look at the chalk at twelve Cooper Lane. He did look up, once, at the window above number twelve. The curtain was closed.

He walked the long way home.

He thought about it the way he thought about a thing on a walk, which was the way a man laid bricks if he were not his father, which was unevenly, with his hands cold, with the mortar setting in the wrong places.

He thought about Dorenne at the survey table. He thought about her hand not reaching for the pencil. He thought about the half-degree that the *Ash* at the end of the meeting had been colder than the *Ash* at the beginning. He thought about Tessaly's pen, hovering. He thought about the brass plate on the new door of the inner shed and the survey on the long wall behind Davan's head and the boys with the chalk and the H reversed. He thought about the man at the cabbage stall standing with the coin between his thumb and forefinger, evaluating it as if it were a thing he had not, before this morning, had cause to evaluate. He thought about Mira saying, *take it down the row.*

He thought about Tesha's slate.

The square and the smaller square and the circle.

He thought about the question he did not have an answer to.

*Why can't we do both?*

He thought about the answer he had been carrying, the long one, the one with the framework and the schedule and the eighty-three percent pump, and he turned the answer over in his hands the way a man turned a piece of work over to check the underside, and he saw, on the underside, what he had not wanted to see, which was that the answer was not an answer, the answer was a description of why the question was hard, and the question was not asking him whether it was hard, the question was asking him why he had not, in twelve months, tried.

He had not tried.

He had built one district. He had taught Tesha the patch and Mira the seat and Vell the housing. He had stood on a step in the lower square at the Cooperage rally and listened to Davan name the man with an ocean asking the thirsty to wait, and he had walked back, and he had not, in any week of the months between then and now, asked himself the Tesha question. He had not asked it because, somewhere underneath, he had decided the question had a no in it, and the no had been the no of *you cannot redistribute what isn't there,* and the no had been technically correct and morally insufficient, and Tesha had named the insufficiency at a trestle in the Cooperage at the eighth hour of a Friday by drawing a square on a slate.

He stopped at the corner of his street.

The lamp at the corner was the lamp the lighter had set at the sixth hour. The wick had been trimmed badly. The flame was a half-flame above the half-cup. He stood under the half-flame with his hands in his pockets and the cold at his collar and he thought about the hospital on Lyle Street and the keep-bucket and the twenty-three names Dorenne would send him in the morning and the seventh hour at the Cooperage yard and Tesha at the bench on Thursday and Tesha at the trestle on Friday, and he could not, at the corner under the half-flame, see the shape of the thing that did both. He could see the request for the shape. He could see the shape's absence. He could see, very clearly, that the absence of the shape had been, for him, a comfortable absence, and that the comfort had been, until tonight, the silent argument his life had been making to itself: *I am right about the future and I am irrelevant to the present and these two things are different problems and neither belongs to me.*

They were the same problem.

They had always been the same problem.

He did not have the shape. He did not pretend to have the shape. He stood under the half-flame and did not pretend.

He went up to his apartment.

He did not channel the lamp. He did not channel the kettle. He sat at the small table by the window with the kettle un-warmed and his coat on, and he took, from the inside pocket, the note that had been pinned under the workshop door the night before. He smoothed it with the side of his hand. He read it. He folded it the other way and put it in the other pocket, the outside one, the one he would put his hand on tomorrow at the Hall when he climbed the stairs to ask Tessaly for an hour of the Director's afternoon, an hour she had not offered, an hour he had not yet earned the right to request.

He did not know what he would say in the hour.

He knew he would have to say something.

He went to bed with his coat on the chair and the note in the pocket and the wick trimmed badly outside the window and the half-flame, faintly, on the ceiling. He did not sleep for a long time. When he slept, he slept the way a man slept who had set down a comfort and not yet picked up a tool, which was lightly, and with his hands open.

In the morning he went to the Cooperage at the seventh.

He did not, on the way, count windows.

He did not, on the way, look at the chalk.

He went, the way Tesha went, on Wednesdays and Thursdays and Fridays, which was at the pace of a person who had not yet figured out the shape and was, in the absence of figuring, walking.
