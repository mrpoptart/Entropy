# Chapter 14

He woke before the lamp had been trimmed and lay looking at the ceiling, and the ceiling was the ceiling.

The plaster had a hairline that ran from the corner over the bed to a point above the wardrobe, and the hairline had been there since the year his father had repaired the roof and the roof had settled, and the hairline had not lengthened in a decade. He had counted it as a child. He counted it now. He stopped counting halfway and let his eyes go to the window, and the window was the colour windows were in the half-hour before the light came up over the curve, which was the colour of nothing.

He had slept in his clothes.

He had come up the stair the night before in the dark, because Haran had set the lamp down at the door of the workshop and said *home,* and he had gone home, and he had not made the lamp at his own door, and his mother had not been awake, and the kettle on the back of the stove had been the kettle on the back of the stove, and he had not warmed it. He had sat at the kitchen table in the dark for the length of time a man sat in a kitchen in the dark, and then he had gone up to bed in his shirt and his trousers and lain down on the cover and not pulled it over him.

Now it was morning.

He sat up. He put his feet on the boards. The boards were cold and he did not look for the slippers. He went to the basin in the corner and wet his face and pushed his hair back with wet hands and looked at himself in the small clouded glass that had been the small clouded glass since his sister had broken the better one at twelve, and his face was the face. He did not shave. He went down.

His mother was at the stove. She had the kettle on. She did not turn.

"There's bread," she said.

"Mm."

"Cut a slice."

He cut a slice. He stood at the board and ate it standing, because sitting at the table would have been the table, and the table was a place where a person had to be a person at, and he was not yet. His mother poured tea into a cup and set the cup on the corner of the board near his hand without looking at him, and went back to the stove, and rinsed the pot.

He drank the tea.

"Where," she said, after a minute, with her back still to him.

"Out."

"Mm."

"To see Ryn."

She did not say anything for the length of two pots being moved, the small one off the heat and the big one onto it. Then she said, without turning, "Good."

That was all she said. She did not turn around. She went on at the stove. He set the cup down. He kissed the back of her shoulder where the wool of her shawl had gone thin from being kissed in that one place over years, and she patted his hand once, briefly, with a hand that was wet from the rinsing, and he went out.

* * *

He took the long way down.

He did not take it on purpose. He took it because his feet went the way his feet went, and his feet had not gone toward Ryn's workshop in a year, and the going was the kind of going a body did when it had to remember the route by walking the route. He went down the upper curve and across the small square where the trough was iced at the rim and not in the middle, and down the steep lane where the cobbles were slick where the sun had not been, and across the second bridge, and along the canal a way, and past the long brick wall of the cooperage, and then up two streets into the trade quarter where the shopfronts were doors with stencils and the stencils said what was done inside in the plain way of the trade quarter, which was to name the thing by what the thing did. *Pumps. Lattice. Flux & seat work.* No names of men over the lintels. The trade quarter did not put names over lintels. The trade quarter put what you could come in with.

Ryn's workshop was the third door on the right of the second street.

He had remembered it as the second. He stood at the second door for a count and read the stencil. The stencil at the second door was *Lattice & relay,* which was not Ryn's. He stepped along. The third door was *Pumps & lattice — repair only.* The *repair only* had been added later. The paint of those two words was newer than the paint of the rest, and the brush had been a smaller brush, and someone had done the addition standing on a crate, because the line was straight in a way a line was straight when the painter had the lintel at eye level.

The door was open a hand's width. He pushed it.

The shop was warm. The warmth was not the warmth of a heating run; the warmth was the warmth of a small forge in the back and three bodies working and a kettle that someone, somewhere, kept warm without thinking about it. The front room had a counter, a slate on the wall with names and times chalked, a bench under the window with a man on it asleep with his cap over his face, and the smell of grease and brass and hot iron and the wet-paper smell of the rags in the bucket by the door.

Ryn was on the floor at the back, under a section of lattice she had pulled off a wall.

She had her sleeves rolled. Her hair was tied back with what looked like the same twist of cord she had tied it back with at fourteen, which it was not, but the angle of the tying was the angle of the tying. She had a brass tool in one hand and the hand that was not holding the tool was inside the lattice up to the wrist. She did not look up at the door.

"Sit on the bench," she said. "Move his feet."

The man on the bench did not move his feet. Ash moved them. He sat. The man on the bench made a small sound and did not wake.

"Be a minute," Ryn said.

"All right."

She did the thing she was doing. She did it with the kind of attention that did not include him, which was not, he registered, an excluding. It was the attention a person at work gave to the work. He watched. She had something in the lattice she was trying to seat, and the seating was a seating that required two hands to be held in two places at once, and she had two hands and the two places were not in reach of two hands at once, and she was solving it by the small adjustments of a person who had solved it before by the same small adjustments.

She channeled.

She channeled the way she had always channeled, which was without ceremony, the small flicker of warmth at the back of his attention that he had felt a thousand times in his childhood and not, in a year, felt at all. The lattice clicked. She made a sound in her throat that was not a word and was not, exactly, satisfaction; it was the sound of a thing seating that had not, the first three times, seated. She pulled her hand out of the lattice.

"Hand me the small one," she said. "On the bench by your knee."

He looked at the bench. There were five small ones. He picked the one nearest his knee.

"Not that."

"Which."

"The one with the wrapped grip. Other end."

He picked the one with the wrapped grip. He held it out. She did not turn around. She held her hand back over her shoulder, palm open, and he set the handle into her palm, and her fingers closed around it, and the closing was the closing of a hand that had been closing around handles in this room for years.

"Thanks."

"Mm."

She did the next thing. The next thing took longer. He sat with his hands on his knees on the bench next to the man with the cap over his face, and he watched, and the watching was the first thing he had done in a week that was not a doing.

She finished.

She did not stand right away. She stayed crouched at the wall with her hands on her thighs and looked at the lattice she had put back on, and she looked at it the way a person looked at a thing they had finished and were not yet sure they had finished. Then she stood. She was shorter than he remembered. He had stopped remembering her at the height she had been at fourteen, which was the height she had been at fourteen, and she had been the same height since seventeen, and he had not seen her enough in the last year for the seventeen-height to overwrite the fourteen-height.

She turned.

She looked at him. She looked at him the way she looked at things, which was a look that arrived and then stayed and did not, while it was staying, perform the staying. The look went over his face once. It went over his shoulders. It came back to his face.

"You look terrible," she said.

He laughed.

He did not mean to laugh. The laugh came out of him before he had decided whether the thing she had said was a thing to laugh at, and the laugh came out from somewhere under his ribs that had been closed for what felt to him, when he heard the laugh, like a season. The laugh was not a long laugh. It was a short laugh and it caught at the end and went into a breath, and the breath was a breath, and he put his hand over his face for the length of the breath, and Ryn did not say anything, and when he took his hand down she was looking at him still, and the look had not changed, and one corner of her mouth had gone up the small distance one corner of her mouth went up when she had landed a thing.

"Right," she said. "Wait at the door. I have to wash my hands and tell Pell where I'm going."

"You're going."

"With you. We're walking. Door."

He went to the door.

The man on the bench, behind him, said, without taking the cap off his face, "She'll have you carrying the bucket."

"All right."

"Don't carry the bucket. It's a test."

"All right."

He stood at the door. He looked at the slate on the wall. There were six names chalked, and times next to them, and small marks next to the times that he did not know the meaning of, and Ryn's hand had written all six. The hand had not changed. The way she made a four was the way she had made a four when they had been in the schoolroom together and she had finished her sums first and used the rest of the time to draw small bad horses in the corner of her slate.

She came past him, drying her hands on her apron. She pulled the apron off over her head and hung it on the peg. She took a coat down. She did not take a scarf.

"Come on."

* * *

They walked.

She walked the way she walked, which was at the pace of a person who walked to get somewhere, not the pace of a person who walked to walk. He had to lengthen his step the first half-street and then he remembered, and his step lengthened on its own, and they went down the trade quarter and up out of it the way they had gone down and up it together a thousand times when they had been small and going on errands for one mother or the other, and the geography of it was a geography that did not need to be looked at.

She did not ask.

He waited for her to ask. She did not ask. She walked. After two streets he understood she was not going to ask, and the not-asking was the room she was making, and the room was the room.

He talked.

He did not talk about Davan. He did not talk about the square at the foot of the curve, or the brass cap, or the proof-of-concept, or the reservoir that sat in his chest at thirty-seven of one hundred and had not moved in a week. He talked about himself. He talked about the way it had felt, in the autumn, to know what he was doing and to know it was the right thing and to walk out of a room where the people in the room had not agreed it was the right thing and to know that the not-agreeing was not, on its own, going to change his mind, and to know also that the not-agreeing was a fact about the world and the fact was going to be there in the morning. He talked about feeling trapped between positions a man could not hold at the same time and could not, also, set down. He talked about waking up in the night with the shape of the next day in his head before the day had asked to be shaped. He talked about doing the right thing in the wrong way. He talked about doing the wrong thing in the right way. He said both phrases, one after the other, and stopped, because he had heard himself say them and they had been the kind of phrases a man said when he had not yet found the sentence the phrases were trying to be a sentence about.

She walked.

She did not look at him while he talked. She looked at the street. She nodded once at a woman in a doorway who nodded back, and the woman did not look at Ash, and Ryn did not introduce him, and the not-introducing was the kind of not-introducing that a person did when the introducing would have been a managing, and Ryn did not, this morning, manage.

They came up to the small square where the trough was iced at the rim, and she stopped at the trough. She put her hand on the iron rim and looked at the ice and did not break it. He stopped beside her.

"You done," she said.

"For now."

"All right."

She took her hand off the rim. She turned and looked at him. The look was the look she had given him in the workshop, but longer, and without the small distance the workshop had put between them when she had been at the wall and he had been at the bench. They were standing close enough now that he could see the line of grease at the base of her thumb where she had not, at the basin, washed the whole of her hands, and the small white scar on her cheekbone from the hot wire when she had been nineteen, and the way her eyes, when they were doing the work of looking, did not do anything else.

"You became someone else," she said. "You left. I didn't know if you were coming back."

He did not answer.

"It's not, I'm not telling you off. I'm saying what it was. From here."

"I know."

"I don't think you do."

She said it without heat. She said it the way she had said *not that* about the small tool, which was the way of a person who had been corrected on the same wrong tool in the past and did not, anymore, sharpen the correction.

"I watched you save the city from the earthquake," she said. "Everyone watched. You know that."

"Yes."

"I was on the third bench at Hesh's when the floor went, and I held onto the table, and the table held, and I went out into the street and the street was the way the street was, and I stood in it, and then the air did the thing it did, the thing only you could have done, and the buildings stopped coming down. I stood in the street and I watched the buildings stop coming down, and I watched the dust hang, and I watched a man two doors up sit on the kerb and put his head between his knees, and I went home."

"Ryn."

"Wait. I went home and the kettle on the stove was the kettle on the stove, and I warmed it. I warmed my tea. I sat at my table and I drank the tea, and I thought, while I was drinking it: he doesn't need me to do that anymore. He doesn't need anyone."

She was not looking at him now. She was looking at the trough. She had her hands in her coat pockets and her shoulders had set the way her shoulders set when she had decided to say a thing through to its end without the kind of pauses that let a person interrupt.

"And I missed you," she said. "Not the Wellspring. You."

He stood.

He stood with the iron rim of the trough cold against his hip where he had leaned without meaning to, and the cold went through the cloth of his coat and into him and he did not move away from it. The square was not empty. A boy went by with a basket. A woman crossed at the far corner with a child by the hand. The child looked at them and the woman did not look at them. The world was the world.

"I'm sorry," he said.

"I know."

She said it without the small softening her voice would have put on it five years ago. The *I know* was a *I know,* and it sat where she had set it.

"You were sorry the last time too," she said. "When we ran into each other at the cooper's gate, and you had the coat on and the look on, and you were already halfway out the door. You said sorry then. You meant it then. You went."

"Yes."

"Being sorry isn't the same as being here."

"I know."

"Do you."

"I'm here now."

She turned her face toward him. She did it slowly, the way a person turned to look at a thing they had already decided how to look at and did not need to hurry the decision.

"Are you," she said. "Or are you here because the other places didn't work out."

He opened his mouth.

He closed it.

He did not have the answer that began with *no.* He had the answer that began with *I.* He had the answer that began with *Davan.* He had the answer that began with *both.* He stood with the rim of the trough at his hip and the question in the air, and the question did not move. She did not move it. She stood with her hands in her pockets and let the question be the question, and after a minute, which felt longer, she took a breath.

"Walk," she said.

He walked.

* * *

They went down out of the small square the way they had come up, and she took, without saying, the route that ran along the canal and not the route that ran past the cooperage. The canal route was longer. The canal route was also the route they had walked when they had been fourteen and had needed to walk somewhere they were not going to be seen by mothers, and the route had become a route the way routes did, by the walking of them.

She talked.

She talked about her work. She talked about the lattice in Building Four on the north side, which had failed in the autumn and been patched with a relay that was the wrong gauge, and about the patch holding through the first cold snap and going at the second, and about the woman on the third floor of Building Four who had two boys, and about the woman's youngest, who was six and had a cough, and about the way the woman had stood in the doorway of Ryn's shop with her coat buttoned wrong and not asked Ryn to come, because the woman was not a woman who asked, and about Ryn going anyway, because Ryn was the kind of woman who went when the buttoning was wrong, and about the patch she had put on the lattice, which was a patch and not a fix, and about the patch holding for now, and about not knowing how long now was.

She talked about Lema, who was a woman in her own building, two floors down, who had moved in when Ryn had moved in and was sixty now, and whose flat in the autumn had been warm into the evening, and whose flat in the winter had not been warm past the third hour of the afternoon, and who had taken to sitting in Ryn's kitchen in the late afternoons with a book, because Ryn's lattice was newer and held longer, and because Ryn made tea, and because Lema would not say *my flat is cold,* but she would come and sit in Ryn's kitchen, and Ryn would make tea, and the tea was the saying.

She talked about the channelers in her trade who had been reassigned. She used the word reassigned. She put a small distance around the word, the way she had used to put a small distance around a word a teacher had used in the schoolroom that the teacher had got slightly wrong.

"They were on lattice maintenance," she said. "Three of them. Good ones. Eight years on the work, between them. The Hall took them off the lattice in the autumn and put them on the south block warming. Then in the new year they took them off the south block and put them on the priority list, which is the third-floor offices and the upper terraces and the residences of people whose names are on a paper. So now my lattice work is done by me and Pell and a boy who started in the spring, and the patch on Building Four is the patch on Building Four because I had a morning to do it in and not a week."

"Ryn."

"I'm not asking you for anything. I'm telling you what it is."

"All right."

"I don't want the redistribution," she said. "I don't want what the Levelers want. I went to a meeting in the autumn, in Pell's building, and the man who was speaking, he was a good man, he was right about the third-floor offices, he was right about most of it, and I sat in the back and listened and at the end I went home and warmed my tea and thought, no. Not because he was wrong. Because I do not want to live in a world where the way we get through the winter is by deciding which neighbour to take warmth from."

"Mm."

"I don't want the pumps either. I came past Building Six last week. Branner was at the handle. He's at the handle every afternoon. He has a daughter on a list. He's at the handle. I do not want a world where Branner is at the handle for the next ten years."

"Then what do you want."

She did not answer for half a street.

"I want the world to stop getting harder," she said. "I want my lattice to hold. I want Lema to be warm in her own flat. I want the boy in Pell's building who started in the spring to have the eight years to learn the work in. I want the woman in Building Four to be able to button her coat right when she comes out the door."

"Ryn."

"And I know the world is not going to stop getting harder. I know that. I have known it for two years. I am, this winter, in the part of knowing it where the knowing is in my hands at work, and not in my head at the kitchen table. I do not need you to fix it. I am telling you what it is."

He nodded.

He nodded the small nod a man nodded who had heard a thing and had not yet said anything about the hearing of it. She was not, he registered, asking him to say anything. She had been talking the way a person talked who had been carrying the sentences for a while and had decided that today was a day to set them down, and the setting-down was not a giving. She was setting them down where he could see them. What he did with the seeing was not the part she was managing.

They came to the bench at the canal corner where the wall was low and the wind off the water was the wind. She sat. He sat. The wood of the bench was cold and they did not say anything about it.

"So what do I do," he said.

She looked at the water.

"I don't know," she said. "I'm not the person who knows. I fix lattice."

"Ryn."

"But I know what you don't do."

He waited.

"You don't disappear again," she said. "That's the first one. You don't go up the curve and back into the workshop and decide that because the conversation was hard the conversation is over. You came down. Come down again."

"All right."

"And you don't decide you know the answer and build it alone and forget that the people you're building for have opinions. You did that with Dorenne. You did it in the district. You're doing the thing you always do. You find the smart answer and you build it and you wonder why nobody's grateful."

"That's not, that's not the whole."

"It is. I know you. It is."

He did not argue. She had said it without performing the saying, and the not-performing had taken the room out from under the arguing, and he sat with the words on the bench between them, and after a count he nodded, the small nod again, and she let the nod be the answer.

"You built a water system," she said, "for people who didn't ask for a water system."

"They needed it."

"Maybe. Probably. I'm not arguing the water. The water is good. Branner is at the handle and the cistern is filling and the building is drinking. I am saying that you went and you decided what they needed and you built it and you trained them in it and you came back up the curve, and all the way through that, the part you didn't do is the part where you sat down with them first."

"Ryn, the cistern was at twelve percent. There wasn't time."

"There is never time. There will never be time. That isn't the point. The point is that it is the same thing. It is the same thing in the district as it was with Dorenne as it was, before, with me. You go. You decide. You come back with the answer. You hand the answer over. You wonder why the hand on the other end of the answer is, sometimes, slow to close around it."

She looked at him.

"Maybe they needed a water system," she said. "Maybe they needed someone to listen first. Maybe both. You skipped one."

He sat with his hands flat on his thighs.

The water at the canal moved. A duck came along the wall on the far side and went under the bridge and did not come out, and he watched the place where it had not come out for a count, and the duck came out at last from a place further up than he had been watching, and the duck went on.

"All right," he said.

"All right."

"I hear it."

"I know."

She put her hand, briefly, on his wrist. The hand was warm from her pocket and smelled of brass and grease, and the thumb pressed once at the inside of his wrist where the pulse was, and she took the hand away again and put it back in her pocket. She did not say *don't* and she did not say *I see you,* and he was not sure which of the two the hand had been for, or whether it had been for both, and the not-being-sure was, he understood, the room she was making for him to be in without managing what he did with it.

"Are you cold," she said.

"Yes."

"Walk."

* * *

They walked again.

She took, this time, the route up the lane behind the cooperage and through the small alley that gave onto her street, which was the route she walked at the end of a day when she was tired, because the route had three corners that broke the wind and the long route along the canal had none. The route was the route of a person who had walked the route every winter evening for years, and the walking of it was the walking of it.

Her building was a four-storey on a narrow street. The door was painted a dark green that had once been a brighter green and was, as he stood at the door, the green it had become. She did not put her hand on the door. She turned, with her shoulder to the green of it, and looked at him.

"I'm not going to ask you up," she said.

"All right."

"Not because I don't want to. Because if you came up today you would think you had done the thing, and you have not done the thing, you have come down once."

"All right."

"You used to come by on Tuesdays."

"I know."

"Come back tomorrow."

He looked at her.

"Tomorrow's Tuesday," she said.

"I know."

"Not because you're failing. Don't, don't come back tomorrow because you've decided this is a thing you have to do. Come back tomorrow because you used to come by on Tuesdays."

"Ryn."

"Yes."

"All right."

"Good."

She stood on the step. She did not go in yet. She looked at him for the count of a breath, and the look was the look she had given him in the workshop, but with the small distance of the workshop replaced by the smaller distance of the door, and one corner of her mouth was at the small height it had been at when she had landed *you look terrible,* and she did not, this time, say the thing she had landed.

"Eat," she said. "You ate the bread at the board this morning. I can see it from here. Eat at lunch. Eat at supper. Eat tomorrow."

"How can you see it."

"I can always see it."

She turned. She put her hand on the green door. She pushed it.

"Tuesday," she said over her shoulder.

"Tuesday."

The door closed.

He stood on the street a count he did not count. The wind came along the narrow street and went past him and he did not turn from it. A woman came out of the next door with a child by the hand and the child looked at him and the woman did not look at him, and the woman and the child went down the street, and he watched them go, and when they had turned the corner he turned the other way and went up out of the trade quarter the way he had come in, and the way he had come in was, when he had come in, not the way he had remembered, and the going out was, this time, the right way without thinking.

* * *

He came up to the workshop in the late afternoon.

Haran was at his bench. Tesha was at hers. The lamp had been trimmed and not yet lit. The broadsheets were under the brass weight, slightly off-centre, where they had been the day before, and where they would be the day after, until someone put them somewhere else, which someone, at some point, would.

He hung his coat.

He went to the basin. He washed his hands. He dried them on his front. He came to the bench.

Haran did not look up.

"Ryn," Haran said.

"Yes."

"Mm."

Haran bent the wire. He set it. He turned, with one hand on the rack, and looked at Ash from the angle he looked at things from, and did not, for once, say *eat.*

"Tomorrow," Ash said.

"Mm."

"I'm going down again tomorrow."

"All right."

He did not say anything else. He picked up the gear from the small clamp at the corner of the bench. He looked at the next tooth. He filed.

The burr came off in two passes.

He filed the next.

* * *

Tuesday was a Tuesday.

He went down the curve in the mid-morning, because Tuesday was a day she did not open the workshop until the third hour, and he had remembered that on the way down, and the remembering had been the kind of remembering a body did when it had not, in a year, used the information, and had, on the day the information was needed, found the information where it had been left. He came into the trade quarter and along the second street, and the third door was open a hand's width, and he pushed it.

She was at the counter with a list.

She looked up when the door opened, and she looked at him, and the look went over his face once and came back, and she did not say *you look terrible,* though the small height at the corner of her mouth said she had thought it, and had decided, as a kind of mercy, not to land it twice.

"Pell," she said, over her shoulder. "I'm out for an hour. The boy comes in at the third, put him on the bracket work. If Mara comes in for the relay, tell her tomorrow."

A voice from the back said, "Mm."

Ryn came out from behind the counter. She took her coat down. She took, this time, a scarf. She wrapped it once round her throat without looking at it.

"Walk," she said.

"Walk."

They walked.

They walked the route along the canal, because the canal route was the long route, and the long route, today, was the route. The water at the canal moved. The duck, which was probably not the same duck, was at the wall on the far side, and went under the bridge, and came out, and went on. Ryn did not put her hand on his wrist this time. She walked beside him at the pace she walked at, and he walked beside her at the pace, and they did not, for the first quarter of the walk, say anything, and the not-saying-anything was a different not-saying-anything than the not-saying-anything of the day before, and he could tell the difference, and he did not have a name for it, and he did not need one.

After a quarter she said, "Tell me about the boy at the lime kiln."

"What."

"Davan said it. I heard it from Pell. The boy at the lime kiln who lost his arm. Did you know him."

"No."

"Did you know there was a boy."

"I knew there were boys. I did not know that boy."

"Mm."

She walked.

"Tell me a name," she said. "From the district. One name. A person you know by name."

"Branner."

"Tell me about Branner."

He told her about Branner.

He told her about Branner the way she had told him about Lema, in the small, unornamented way of a person reporting what was true. He told her about the daughter on the Sixth Ward list. He told her about the rhythm at the handle. He told her about the way Branner had nodded at him over the pump in the yard yesterday, and about the way he, Ash, had stood at the gate, and not gone in. He told her about not going in. He talked for the length of three streets. She did not interrupt. When he had finished she walked another half-street without saying anything, and then she said, "Good. That's one. Tomorrow tell me one more."

"Tomorrow."

"Tomorrow's Wednesday. Wednesday I'm at the shop till the fourth. Come by at the fourth. Walk me home."

"All right."

"And eat."

"I will."

"At lunch."

"At lunch."

"And don't come down on Thursday."

"Why."

"Because Thursday is the day you'd come down to prove to yourself you're coming down. Don't. Go up the curve. Do your work. Come down on Friday."

He nodded.

He nodded the small nod he had nodded the day before at the bench at the canal corner, and she saw the nod, and the small height at the corner of her mouth was at the small height, and they walked on, and the canal was the canal, and the duck went on under the bridge, and the wind was the wind, and the city, around them, was the city.

He did not have an answer.

He had, on the bench inside him where the not-channeling sat, and the two true things from the day before sat, a third thing now, which was Ryn's hand briefly on his wrist at the canal yesterday, and Ryn's voice saying *Tuesday,* and Ryn's voice saying *tell me a name,* and he set the third thing down with the other two, and the three of them sat together, not arguing, not resolving, and the not-resolving was, again, the work.

He walked her back to the shop.

At the door he stopped. She stopped.

"Wednesday," he said.

"Wednesday."

"At the fourth."

"At the fourth."

She put her hand on the green of the doorframe, which was a different green from the door of her building, and she looked at him once more, and then she went in, and the door went to behind her, and he stood on the street for the count of a breath, and then he turned, and he went up the curve.

He took the route past the cooperage.

He took it because the route past the cooperage was three streets shorter and he wanted, today, to be at his bench by the fifth hour, and the wanting was the first wanting in a week that had been a wanting toward a thing and not a wanting away from one.

He came up to the workshop.

He hung his coat.

He went to his bench.

He filed the fifth tooth.

The burr came off in two passes.

He filed the sixth.
