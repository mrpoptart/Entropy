# Chapter 11

The bread was on the table when he came through the door, and the bread was the bread, and his mother was at the stove with her back to him and her hand on the rail of the iron lid as if she had only just laid it there.

"You're late," she said, not turning.

"I am."

"By the half-hour."

"Closer to the hour."

"By the hour, then."

She did turn, after that, and looked at him the way she looked at him when she was, in the looking, doing the small inventory she did at the door before any of them had said anything that mattered. He stood with his coat half off and his hands at his sides, and his hands were the hands of the workshop, dark in the creases, the right thumbnail rimmed with the pitch from the wrap on the eastern row, the cuff of his sleeve the cuff of a man who had pushed it back to the elbow more times that day than he had pushed it down.

"Wash," she said.

"Mm."

"In the basin. Use the soap."

"I know where the soap is, Mum."

"You used to."

He went to the corner basin. The new lattice on the basin wall sat a hand's-breadth above the tap, pale and clean. She had not turned it on. The kettle on the stove was the old kettle, the dented one from the lower flat, the one she had carried in the box she had carried herself across the bridge, and the kettle was breathing the small steady breath of a kettle warmed by a hand laid on its body and not by the new disc on the wall, and he registered the kettle the way he registered anything in the apartment that had survived the move, which was to say he saw it and did not say anything about it.

He washed.

He scrubbed under the nails with the small brush she had set on the basin's lip the way she had set the small brush on the basin's lip in the lower flat for twenty-three years. The water ran gray. He scrubbed until it ran clear. He dried his hands on the rough towel and turned and his father was at the table.

"Ashlyn," his father said.

"Da."

"Sit."

He sat.

The table was the table from the lower flat. His mother had not taken the table the building inspectors had offered. The chairs were three of the four old chairs and one new one, and the new one was at the end where Maren sat when Maren sat, and the new one had not, in the year, been replaced or moved.

His father had the bowl. The bowl was the cracked bowl. It sat at his father's elbow, empty, with the small hairline up the rim Ash could trace from across the table without looking, because he had traced it across this table for the years it had been a bowl, and the bowl was on the table the way the kettle was on the stove, which was the way his mother kept her hands on the things that had survived.

"Stew," his mother said, from the stove.

"Mm."

"It's been on since the fourth hour."

"It smells good."

"It's been on since the fourth hour, Ashlyn. By the seventh it would have been better. By the eighth it is what it is."

"It'll be good."

She did not answer that. She lifted the lid with the cloth on the handle and the steam came up, and the steam was the smell of the lower-quarter stew she had made his whole life, the marrow stock and the root and the small dried sourleaf she crumbled at the end and not before, because the leaf was the leaf you crumbled at the end. She crumbled it. The cloth went back on the rail.

"Maren?" Ash said.

"Coming."

"When."

"When she's coming."

His father, at the table, had been holding his cup and he set the cup down, slowly, and laid his hand flat on the wood beside it, and he said, "Did I tell you about Berran's curtain."

"You did not."

"Berran across the hall."

"The one with the boy."

"The one with the boy. Yes."

He let the pause sit. He was, at the table, sitting in the chair the way he sat, with one shoulder a quarter higher than the other from the year the wall had come down on the scaffolding and the shoulder had set not quite right, and the chair was the chair and the cup was the cup and his hand was flat on the wood beside the cup the way it had been flat on the wood beside the cup since Ash had been a boy who climbed onto the chair to sit beside him.

"Berran's curtain caught," his father said.

"Caught how."

"Singed. The lattice on his window draws hot, off-pattern, when the cold comes in off the gorge wrong, and the curtain hangs the way a curtain hangs, and the curtain caught at the hem on a Threeday. He was at the bench. The boy saw it. The boy beat it out with a broom."

"The boy."

"Eight years old."

"Mm."

"Berran came across the landing the next morning to ask me what I thought."

"What did you think."

His father lifted the cup. He drank. He set the cup down. He laid his hand flat on the wood again. He had, Ash registered, set the cup down at the same place each time, which was the small spot a thumb's width left of the bowl with the crack, where the wood was a half-shade darker because the cup had been set there for a year of evenings.

"I told him," his father said, "the curtain was not the problem."

"No."

"Like a chimney with a bad cap. You can replace the curtain. You can replace it twice. The third time you replace the curtain, the curtain is not the curtain. The curtain is the thing that tells you the cap is wrong."

"The cap."

"The lattice. He has had the lattice since the move. The lattice has never sat right. He uses it because he was given it. He has a perfectly good stove."

Ash watched him. His father's hand turned on the wood, palm up, the thumb tracing the small ring the cup had warmed.

"I told him to bank the stove and let the lattice sit till spring."

"Did he."

"He will. He won't, this week. He will, next."

"Mm."

His father looked at him. The look was the look of a man who had told the story he had meant to tell, and who was, in the look, telling Ash the second story under the first one without putting any weight on the second one, and Ash heard both stories and did not, in the hearing, do anything with the second.

"Good story," Ash said.

"It's the curtain, mostly."

"Mostly."

"Mm."

His mother brought the bread. She brought the bread on the board with the long knife laid across it and she set the board down on the table where the board went and she went back for the stew. She did all of this in the rhythm of a woman who had brought bread to a table for thirty years and who did not, in the bringing, perform the bringing. The board sat where the board went. The knife was at the angle the knife was always at. She came back with the pot in both hands with the cloth doubled, and she set it on the trivet, and the trivet was where the trivet was.

"Cut the bread, Ashlyn."

"Mm."

He cut the bread.

He cut three slices and then, in the cutting, four. He cut the fourth slowly. He did not look up. He laid the four slices on the board and he set the knife back across the board the way she set the knife back across the board, and his mother was at the stove with her back to the table and her hand on the rail of the lid for a beat she did not need, and she did not, in the beat, turn.

A key in the door.

* * *

She came in slowly.

She came in the way a person came in who had walked the last half a block on the strength of a sentence she had told herself at the corner, and she stopped inside the door and laid the bag at her feet and worked the buttons of her coat with her fingers, and the fingers did not, in the working, do the buttons in the time the buttons should have been done in. She did not look up while she worked them. She got the third button and stopped, and breathed once, and got the fourth.

"Maren," his mother said, from the stove.

"Mum."

"You're late too."

"I am."

"Coat on the hook."

"In a minute."

She got the last button. She lifted the coat off her shoulders the way she lifted everything she lifted, with the small economical motion she had used since she was twelve, and the motion was the motion, and the coat came off, and she hung it. She turned. She looked at the table.

"Da."

"Mar."

"Ash."

"Mar."

She came to the table. She sat in the new chair at the end. She sat in it the way a person sat who had measured, walking the half block, exactly how much it was going to cost her to sit, and she put both hands flat on the table the way her father put one hand flat on the table beside his cup, and her hands were her hands, and the hands were a half-shade paler than her hands had been at the dinner in the last year, and the knuckles of the right hand were the knuckles of a hand that had been making a fist on the way over and had only, at the door, let the fist go.

His mother set a plate in front of her.

"Tea?"

"No."

"There's the pot on."

"No, Mum."

"All right."

His mother went back to the stove. She ladled stew into the plates. She brought the plates two at a time, the way she had brought plates two at a time before her hip turned, and Ash saw the brought-two-at-a-time and did not, in the seeing, get up. He had learned, in the year since the move, not to get up. The not-getting-up was the courtesy. The courtesy was the courtesy his mother had, in the year, taught him by the small quiet method of taking the plate out of his hand the first three times he had tried to help. After the third time he had stopped trying. She brought the plates. She brought his last. She sat.

They ate.

His father said, after a while, "It's good, Leska."

"It's been on since the fourth hour."

"It's good."

"Mm."

Maren ate.

She ate the way a person ate who had decided, before sitting down, that eating was something she was going to do whether or not she was hungry, because the eating was the thing that made the sitting at the table the thing it was supposed to be. She lifted the spoon. She lowered the spoon. The spoon did not, in the lifting, do anything the spoon had not done a thousand times at this table. The not-doing-anything was the thing Ash watched. He watched it without looking. He watched it the way he had learned, in the workshop, to watch a thing he was not allowed to look directly at.

"The eastern row," his father said.

"Mm."

"You finished the wrap."

"On Fourth-day."

"Held in the cold snap."

"It held."

"Good work."

His father said it quietly. He said it the way he said it. He did not look up from his plate when he said it. The cup was beside the bowl with the crack. The hand was flat on the wood.

"Mm," Ash said.

"Maren." His father turned his head a half-inch. "Your work."

"My work," Maren said.

"What's on this week."

"The Hall annex. The west stair."

"The reinforcement."

"The reinforcement."

"Mm."

She lifted the spoon. She lowered it. She set the spoon down on the lip of the plate, and she put her hands flat on the table again, and she said, "We're behind."

"Behind on the stair."

"Behind on the stair. Behind on the corridor. Behind on the." She stopped. Her jaw set. She unset it. "Behind on most of it."

"Crew?"

"Crew is fine."

"Materials?"

"Materials are fine."

"Mm."

He let it go. He did not, in the letting, look at her. He did not, Ash registered, ask the third question, because the third question was the question that had no answer at this table tonight, and his father knew the third question and did not ask it, and his mother, at the stove with her back already half-turned to scrape the pot clean, knew it too, and did not turn.

Maren picked up the spoon.

She put it down.

"Tea," she said. "Mum. After all."

"All right."

His mother did not say anything else. She poured the tea. She poured it from the kettle on the stove, the dented one, the one she had warmed with her own pool from the back of her chair across thirty years. She poured it into the cup that had been Maren's cup since Maren was nine, the one with the small chip at the rim that Maren turned toward her own mouth so she would not feel the chip on her tongue. She set the cup down at Maren's right hand the way she had set it down at Maren's right hand for fourteen years.

Maren wrapped her hands around the cup.

She wrapped both hands around it. She wrapped them the way a person wrapped their hands around a cup of tea they had been given, on a Threeday in the first cold of the season, after a long walk, when the hands were cold. She did not lift the cup. She sat with both hands on the ceramic and she did not, after a while, drink.

His father did not look up from his plate.

His mother, at the stove, turned the cloth on the rail of the lid by a quarter, the way she turned a cloth that was already where the cloth went. She did not turn around.

Ash watched the cup.

He watched it for the count of a breath. He watched it the way he had stood at the bakery wall and watched the western pump cycle at eighty, with one hand in his pocket and the other on the cold brick. He looked away. He looked at his father's hand on the wood. He looked at the bowl with the crack. He looked at the bread on the board. He cut another slice. He laid it on his father's plate without asking. His father said nothing. His father took the bread. His father broke it.

"Berran's boy," his father said, after a while. "Eight years old."

"Eight," Ash said.

"He beat the curtain out with a broom."

"Quick boy."

"Quick boy."

"Mm."

Maren lifted the cup at last. She lifted it slowly. The lifting was the lifting of a person who was, in the lifting, also lifting the day. She drank. She set the cup down. Her hands went back around it. She did not, this time, lower them to the table.

His mother sat.

She sat in her own chair and lifted her own spoon and ate. She ate the way she had eaten since he was a boy, which was without ceremony and without putting the spoon down between mouthfuls, the eating of a woman who had decided long ago that food was the work and not the occasion. She ate, and her eyes went, twice, to Maren's hands on the cup, and the eyes did not stop, and the eyes did not say anything, and she went on eating.

* * *

After the plates Maren stood up.

"I'll do them."

"Sit, Mar," his mother said.

"I'll do them."

"Sit."

"I'll do them, Mum."

She said it the way she said *Right* at the close of a meeting. She had already moved to the basin before his mother could answer, and his mother, at the table, set the spoon down at the lip of her own plate and laid her hand flat on the wood beside it the way her husband laid his hand flat on the wood beside his cup, and she did not get up. She did not say anything else.

Ash carried the plates.

He carried them two at a time the way she carried them, and he set them on the counter beside the basin, and he stood at Maren's elbow and he did not say anything. Maren did not look at him. She was at the basin with her sleeves pushed back to the elbow and the soap in the small dish at the basin's lip and the water not yet on. The new lattice was a hand's-breadth above the tap. The old lever for the cold tap was at her right hand.

She lifted her hand to the lattice.

She laid her palm on it.

The lattice did not warm.

He saw the not-warming before she did. He saw it the way he saw a seal go at a joint, which was the way he saw any small mechanical fact: it was there before it was named. The lattice was cold under her hand. Her hand stayed on it. Her jaw set. Her shoulders, under the shirt, moved a quarter-inch up and held. The lattice came on. It came on three breaths after it should have come on, and the water from the tap she had not yet turned ran cold against the basin floor when she did turn it, and then it warmed. The warming arrived a count after the water did. The water was cold and then warm and then warm at the right warm, and her hand was still on the lattice and the lattice was, by the time the right warm arrived, a thing she was holding a draw against rather than a thing she had set and walked away from.

She washed a plate.

The plate was the plate. It was the lower-quarter plate with the small blue rim that had been four plates and was now two because two had broken in the move, and she washed it the way she washed any plate, with the rag in the right hand and the plate in the left, and the rag went around the rim and across the face and around again, and she set the plate in the rack.

Her hands were shaking.

It was a shake at the level of the small shake, which was the shake a hand made when the hand was at the end of a count of effort that had been going on for longer than the muscle had been built to do. He had seen the shake on Jora's hand in the third week of the wrap, in the cold, when Jora had laid wool for six straight hours and had come down off the ladder and held the cup of tea Haran had set on the bench, and the cup had moved a quarter-inch in the holding. Jora had been at the end of a day. Maren was at the start of a plate.

He picked up the cloth.

He picked up the dry cloth from the hook by the basin where the dry cloth had hung in the lower flat for twenty-three years and where his mother had hung the dry cloth in this kitchen since the move, on the same hook, on the same nail, at the same height, and he stepped to the rack, and he took the plate.

He dried it.

He dried it the way he had dried plates beside her since he was eleven, which was to say without ceremony and without speaking. He set it on the shelf above the rack. He came back. He took the second plate. He dried it. He set it on the shelf. He came back. He took the bowl.

He dried the bowl with the crack last.

He dried it with the cloth folded so that the crack was held by the cloth on both sides at once, the way his mother had taught him to dry the bowl with the crack when he was twelve. He set it on the shelf where the bowl with the crack went, which was the upper shelf, the second from the left, where his mother could reach it with her good arm without going up on her toes.

He did not say anything.

Maren did not say anything.

She washed the cups. She washed the spoons. She washed the long knife from the bread board. The water ran. The lattice held. Her hands shook. She kept washing. She did not, in the washing, look at him. He did not, in the drying, look at her. They worked the way two people who had been doing this work in the same room for fourteen years worked, which was the work that required no looking, which was the work that knew its own choreography, and the choreography ran, and the water ran, and the cups went into the rack one at a time.

When the rack was full she stopped.

She stood with her hands in the basin and the water not running and her hands not coming out, and her shoulders were the line they had been at the lattice, and she said, quietly, with the cloth still in her right hand and her wrist in the water:

"Don't."

He had the bowl with the crack on the shelf. He had set it. He had his hand still on it because the cloth had caught at the crack on the way out. He turned the cloth. He freed it. He set the cloth on the rail beside the basin.

"Don't what," he said.

"Whatever you're about to say."

A breath.

"I know what you're noticing."

A breath.

"Don't."

He stood at the rail.

He stood at the rail with the cloth on it and the rack full to his right and his sister to his left with her hands in the water, and he heard the *Don't* the way he had heard *it is not enough* on the towpath two weeks ago, which was to say he heard it as a sentence that had been waiting in the room a long time before the room had given it the floor. *Don't* was the floor. *Don't* was the sentence she had said instead of every sentence she had not said since she was twelve and he had not had a pool and she had, at a dinner he did not now remember, lifted a cup and warmed it across the table to him and seen, in his face, the thing his face did when she warmed a cup across the table to him, and had stopped doing it, and had not done it again at any table either of them had sat at since.

The silence after the *Don't* was the silence of twenty-three years.

It was the silence in which he had not asked her, when she was fourteen, what it felt like to set a wall. It was the silence in which she had not asked him, when she was sixteen and he was eighteen, how he made it up the forty flights without breaking and how his hands had got the way they were. It was the silence in which their mother had set the kettle on the rail and not turned and their father had laid his hand flat on the wood beside the cup and not looked up. It was the choreography. It was the choreography of a family that had, twenty-three years ago, made a small careful agreement at a table and had kept the agreement at every table since.

The agreement was, this evening, ending.

He felt it end. He felt it end in the rail under his hand and in the cloth on the rail and in the bowl with the crack on the shelf above his head, and he felt it end the way a man felt a seal go at a joint, which was to say he felt it before it was named, and he did not name it.

He picked up the cloth.

He picked it up because the cloth was what he had. He did not pick it up to do anything with it. He held it. He held it folded once, the way his mother had taught him to fold a cloth, and he stood at the rail beside his sister, and he did not say *I see you.* He did not say *I know what's happening.* He did not say *let me help.* He did not say any of the sentences that had walked across the square beside him on the walk over from the workshop, the sentences he had drafted at the bench between the second and third plate at the meeting on Fifth-day, the sentences that had, in the drafting, been the sentences a man with twenty-three years of doing it the hard way might offer to a woman who had not yet been required to do it the hard way at all.

He had been ready to give one of them.

He had been ready at the door, half ready in the coat half off, ready at the basin with his hand on the small brush, ready at the table when she had wrapped both hands around the cup. He had been ready in the way a man was ready who had thought for a month about a thing and had, in the thinking, made the thing portable, sized for an evening, sized for a kitchen, sized for the shape his sister's pride could be expected to receive without breaking. He had been ready.

He set the readiness down.

He set it down on the rail beside the cloth. He did not set it down because she had asked him to. He set it down because the *Don't* had been, in the saying of it, the most honest word he had ever heard her say, and any sentence he could put in the air after it would be a sentence said over the top of her honesty, and he was not, this evening, going to be the brother who did that.

He stood at the rail with the cloth.

He waited.

After a long while she lifted her hands from the basin. She lifted them slowly. She did not, in the lifting, shake any harder. She lifted them the way she had buttoned her coat at the door, which was the way she did the things she had decided to do whether or not the body wanted to do them. She dried her hands on the towel he held out, and the holding-out had not been a sentence, and the towel was a towel, and she dried, and she handed the towel back, and she folded her sleeves down the way she folded everything, exactly, button by button.

She did not look at him.

She turned to the doorway. She paused with one hand on the frame. She did not turn her head. She said, to the frame:

"Coat."

"Mm."

"Walk me to the corner."

"Yes."

She went to the table. She said her goodbyes. He did not hear them. He was at the rail with the cloth still in his hand. He folded it. He set it back on the rail beside the basin where the cloth went. He took his coat off the hook.

His mother was at the stove.

She had, in the time he had been at the basin, washed the pot. The pot was upside down on the trivet to drain. The cloth on the rail of the lid had been folded once and set on the back of the stove where the cloth went between dinners. She was at the stove with her hand on the rail of the lid and her back to the room, and she did not turn, and her shoulders, under the shawl she had taken off the back of her chair while he had been at the basin and put on while he had not seen her put it on, were the line her shoulders made when she had decided not to turn.

His father was at the table.

He had not stood. He had laid his hand flat on the wood beside the cup, and the cup was empty, and the bread board was clean, and he looked up when Maren came to the table and he reached out and laid his hand on her wrist for the count of a breath, and he did not say anything, and Maren did not say anything, and the hand came off, and he laid it flat on the wood again.

"Da," she said.

"Mar."

"I'll come Threeday."

"Mm."

"If I can."

"If you can."

"Mm."

She kissed her mother on the side of the head. Her mother did not turn. Her mother lifted her hand from the rail and laid it on Maren's hand on her shoulder for a beat, and the beat was the beat, and the hand came off, and her mother went back to looking at the stove.

Ash held the door.

* * *

The corner was three blocks.

They walked it slowly. They walked it the way he had walked any street with her, which was to say at the pace of the slower of the two of them, and the slower of the two of them was, this evening, his sister, and he matched her without thinking about the matching and he did not, in the not-thinking, say anything about it.

The street was the street.

It was a Threeday in the eleventh week of the season and the cold was the cold off the gorge and the lamps along the lower wall were the lamps along the lower wall and the lattice in the lamps was, on three of the seven, dim. He registered the three. He had registered the three on the way over, and he had registered them three weeks ago, and he had registered, in the same registering, that the three had not been the same three each week, which was the small useful fact about the lattice on the lower wall, which was that the three could be moved from one lamp to another by the maintenance crew that walked the wall on Sixth-day. He did not say any of this.

She walked with her hands in her pockets.

He walked with one hand in his pocket and the other at his side because his other hand was the hand he had held the cloth in, and the hand had not yet decided to go in the pocket. He registered the hand. He let it be at his side. The hand was the hand.

At the corner she stopped.

She did not look at him.

"You said nothing," she said.

"I did."

"For an hour."

"For more than an hour."

"Mm."

A breath.

"That was the right thing to do."

"Mm."

"Don't make me say it twice."

"I won't."

"Mm."

A breath.

"Ash."

"Mar."

"Threeday."

"Threeday."

"At Mum's."

"At Mum's."

"Don't be late."

"I'll try."

"Don't try."

"I'll be there."

"Mm."

She did not lift a hand to his collar.

He registered the not-lifting. She had, since he was sixteen, lifted her hand to his collar at the corner she left him at, the small surgical adjustment that was the gesture she used when she did not have a word she would say. She did not, this evening, lift the hand. The hand stayed in the pocket. The not-lifting was the loudest thing on the corner. He did not, in the registering, name it. He nodded the quarter-inch his father nodded. She nodded the quarter-inch back. She went left. He went right. He did not turn to watch her go.

He walked.

He walked the long way, because the long way was the way that took him along the lower wall and past the three dim lamps and across the square at the foot of the bridge and up the bridge, and the bridge was the bridge, and the wind on the bridge was the wind on the bridge, and at the top of the bridge he stopped.

He stopped with one hand on the rail.

He looked at the gorge. The gorge was dark. The lamps on the far side were the lamps on the far side. He thought, with the small clarity a man got on a bridge in the cold, about the twenty-three years.

He thought about the forty flights.

He thought about the iron stove and the flint and steel and the porridge thickening on the stove before his father came down. He thought about the cabinet hinge he had fixed at the fourth hour with the screwdriver and the hand. He thought about the saffron in his pocket on the morning the Wellspring had come, and the way his hand had been the hand that knew how to lift a cup that did not warm. He thought about the years before that, the small specific competences, the wedge under the door that did not draw, the rag wool in the gap under the window, the count he had taught himself for the climb up the stairs to keep his breathing even, the way of standing at a tap with cold water and washing without flinching.

He thought about Maren.

He thought about her hand on the lattice tonight. He thought about it lasting three breaths longer than her hand had ever lasted on a lattice in the kitchen of the old flat, and he thought about the water arriving warm a count after it should have, and the count was the count, and the count was, he registered, the count Tova's pump had run short of on Tuesday morning at the half-hour.

He thought: *she has none of the skills the twenty-three years gave me.*

He thought: *she has none of them because she never needed them.*

He thought: *the twenty-three years are the one thing I have that nobody else can give her.*

He stood on the bridge.

He thought: *and the cost to her pride.*

He thought: *I am not ready.*

He thought: *neither is she.*

He thought: *the conversation is not unspoken anymore.*

He thought: *it is unfinished.*

The wind moved on the gorge. The lamps on the far side did what lamps did. He took his hand off the rail. He went on across the bridge.

* * *

He did not, going home, turn back at the door of the flat.

He had, on the walk to the bridge, thought of turning back. He had thought of turning back at the corner where Maren had gone left, and at the foot of the bridge, and at the top, and on the far side. He had not turned back at any of them.

But at the door of the workshop, with his hand on the bolt, he stopped.

He stopped because he had, in the walk, registered that he had left a thing at his mother's. The thing was nothing. The thing was the cloth he had set on the rail beside the basin, which was already where the cloth went. The thing was the bowl with the crack, which was already where the bowl with the crack went. The thing was nothing he could go back for.

He went back anyway.

He went the half-block back to the corner. He did not turn the corner. He stood at the corner with his hand on the cold brick of the wall and he looked, the way he had stood at the bakery wall on the morning he had not gone to the western pump, down the lane to the door of his mother's flat. The window of the kitchen was on the second floor. The lamp was on. The curtain, his mother's curtain, the new one from Dorenne's office that had been on the rod since the move, was open at the corner she had pulled it open at when she had come into the kitchen at the second hour, which was the corner she always pulled it open at, because the corner she always pulled it open at was the corner that let her see the sky over the gorge while she stood at the stove.

His mother was at the stove.

He could see her from the corner. He could see the line of her shoulder against the lamp, and the line was the line her shoulder had made when he had taken his coat off the hook. She had not, in the time it had taken him to walk Maren to the corner and to walk the long way and to stop on the bridge and to stand at the top of the bridge and to walk back, moved. She was at the stove with her hand on the rail of the lid, and the lid was on the pot, and the pot was upside down to drain on the trivet, which meant the hand on the rail was the hand on a rail that had nothing to be on the rail for.

She was holding the bowl.

He could see it in the angle of her arm, the small held-out angle of an elbow that was holding a thing at the level of the chest. He could see, by the held angle and the cant of her shoulder, that the bowl was the bowl with the crack. He could see, because he knew his mother's body the way a son knew his mother's body, that she was looking at the bowl, and was not looking at the stove, and was not looking at the lamp, and was not looking at the window, and was looking at the bowl in her own hands.

He stood at the corner.

He did not call up. He did not go in. He did not raise a hand. He stood with his hand on the cold brick the way he had stood at the bakery wall with his hand on the cold brick on the morning the western pump had cycled at eighty, and he watched, and he did not move.

After a time she set the bowl down.

She set it on the back of the stove, beside the kettle, where the kettle was. He saw the bowl come down to the back of the stove and he saw the small motion of her hand brushing the rim once with the side of her thumb, and the brushing was the brushing she did to the bowls on the shelf when she set them, which was the small private check against the crack, the small private confirmation that the crack had not, in the year, gone any further. She brushed the rim. She lifted her hand. The hand went to the rail of the lid again.

The shoulder did not move.

He stood for a count.

He did not let himself stand longer than the count. He took his hand off the brick. He turned. He went back down the lane the way he had come, and across the square at the foot of the bridge, and over the bridge, and the wind on the bridge was the wind on the bridge, and at the top of the bridge this time he did not stop.

At the door of the workshop he stopped.

He stood with his hand on the bolt. The bolt was the bolt. Haran had, three weeks running, left the door unbolted at the eighth hour, and Haran had left the door unbolted tonight, because Ash registered, in the laying of his hand on the bolt, that the bolt was already drawn. The door was the door Haran had left the door tonight. He pushed it.

He went in.

The workshop was dark except for the lamp at the back bench. Haran was at the back bench. Haran was winding wire. Haran did not look up. Ash did not, at the door, say anything. He took his coat off. He hung it. He went to the stove. The kettle had been moved to the back of the stove and was warm. There was, on the bench beside the kettle, a second cup. The cup was upside down on the bench. The cup had been there since the seventh hour. Ash registered the cup. He turned it right way up. He poured. He drank.

He stood at the bench.

The reservoir was where the reservoir was. He registered the level. *Thirty-seven.* He had been at thirty-seven in the morning. He had been at thirty-seven all day. He had not, this evening, drawn for anything. The not-drawing was, in his hand at the cup, a thing his hand registered the way his hand registered any thing it had not done.

Behind him, Haran wound wire.

He wound for the count of forty. He set the former down. He picked it up. He wound for the count of twenty more. He did not, in the winding, turn.

Ash drank the tea.

He thought about the bowl. He thought about his sister's hand on the lattice. He thought about *Don't.* He thought about the cloth on the rail. He thought about his father's hand laid flat on the wood beside the empty cup, and the hand laid for a count of a breath on Maren's wrist, and the hand off, and back on the wood. He thought about the corner where she had not lifted her hand to his collar. He thought about the bridge. He thought about the words he had not said in the kitchen, the sentences sized for an evening, sized for a kitchen, sized for the shape of a sister's pride, all of them set down on the rail beside the cloth, all of them, this evening, kept.

The conversation was unfinished.

He thought it the way he had thought *I will say the second one again* on the bedroll on Fifth-day night. He thought: *not tonight.* He thought: *not next week.* He thought: *when she will hear it, and not before.*

He set the cup down.

He went up the stair. He lay on the bedroll. He did not light the lamp. The reservoir sat at thirty-seven. His hand, on his chest, was the hand that had held the cloth. The hand was the hand.

He closed his eyes.

Below, in the workshop, Haran wound wire. The lamp burned. The door, in the way Haran had left the door now for three weeks running at the eighth hour when there was a thing on Ash's mind, was unbolted, and stayed unbolted, and the wind moved against the high window the way the wind moved against the high window, and the night went on.
