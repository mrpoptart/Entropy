# Chapter 21

The workshop was already warm when he came in, and Haran was at the bench by the window with a letter open under his hand.

The forge was banked low. The first light of the morning had not yet come around the buildings opposite, but Haran had been here long enough to have set the kettle, and the kettle had already gone cold, which meant he had set it and forgotten it, which meant he had been reading the letter for some time.

Ash hung his coat. He crossed the floor. He did not say good morning. The shape of Haran's shoulders forbade it.

"Where," Ash said.

Haran did not look up.

"Two," he said.

"Two."

"Coldfell. And the Rise."

Ash set his hand flat on the edge of the bench. The wood was scored along its length with thirty years of small marks, the place where Haran rested a file, the place where a vise had bitten in once and left a half-moon. He looked at the marks because he could not, for a moment, look at the letter.

Coldfell. He had been there in the autumn. He had stood in the meeting room above the granary and watched a woman named Eddis lay out a pump diagram on a table and say, in the dry careful voice of someone who had already done the math twice, *we can hold to spring*. Eddis had a daughter. The daughter's name was something with a soft beginning. He could not get the name back. He had told himself he would remember the name and he had not remembered the name and now Coldfell was in the letter on Haran's bench.

"How," he said.

"Quiet," Haran said. "It says quiet."

"The pumps."

"Half. Half built."

"And the Rise."

"Further along. Further than Coldfell. They had the second well in. They had it in three weeks ago. The lattice they were building over the cisterns. They did not get the heating on."

Ash did not answer.

Haran turned the letter over. There was nothing on the back. He turned it back.

"It is going faster," Haran said.

"Yes."

"Faster than the building."

"Yes."

Haran set the letter down on the bench. He set it square to the edge, the way he set tools down. He did not speak for a while. Then he reached up and ran a thumb along the underside of his jaw, the slow thoughtful gesture he made when he was choosing whether a thing needed to be said now or later.

He chose later.

"The kettle's gone cold," he said.

"I'll do it."

"Use the striker."

"I know."

Ash took the kettle to the sink. He filled it. He set it back on the burner and reached for the small steel striker on the shelf above and scraped it twice before it caught, and the burner came up blue and even, and he stood there a moment with his hand on the rim of the kettle and watched the flame and did not channel anything into it. He had not channeled anything into a kettle in a long time. The flame did the work of a flame. The water would boil when water boiled.

Coldfell. *Sera*, he thought. The daughter's name was Sera. He had remembered it when he stopped trying.

He put the name down where the other names lived. There was a place inside him for the names now. It had grown larger this winter. It would grow larger again before the spring ran out.

He went back to the bench.

* * *

Tesha came in at the back hour of morning, the one between the first shift and the proper start, and she came in fast.

She had her satchel on one shoulder and a length of copper wire in the other hand and a coil of something cloth-bound under her arm, and she dropped the copper on the bench before she had her coat half off, and she said, "Ash. Ash, I need you," and then she stopped, because Haran was looking at her, and Tesha had learned over the last two months to stop when Haran looked at her.

Haran nodded, once. Permission to begin.

She began.

"The block."

"Which block."

"Mine. Six. No. Not Six. The one next to Six. The one across the alley. They copied it."

"Copied what."

"All of it. The hybrid. The whole, the whole patchwork. They watched us put it in over the winter and they did it themselves last week. Without asking. Without telling anyone. They just did it."

She had the cloth-bound coil unwrapped on the bench now. Inside it was a thing that had been a heating lattice once and was now an attempt at a heating lattice, the wires looped in the right shape and the spacing wrong, the tap-points soldered in places that would hold a current and not pass one. He looked at it. It was the work of someone who had seen the thing assembled and had not seen it explained.

"It was hot when they put it in," she said. "Two days. Two days and then nothing. I went over yesterday morning because someone came and got me and I went over and the woman who did the work, she's called Berre, she had it apart on her kitchen table and she was crying. Not loud. She wasn't, she wasn't loud about it. She had it apart and she didn't know which part. She kept saying *I followed it. I followed it.* And she had. She had followed what she'd seen. She'd just, she hadn't seen the inside of it."

"Tesha."

"And it isn't only her block. There's another one. Off the lower terrace. I heard yesterday. They've started one too. Off a sketch someone made from memory. A *sketch*, Ash. They're working off a sketch."

She stopped.

She put her hands flat on the bench, the way her sister put hands flat on a kitchen table, except Tesha was no kin of Maren's and the gesture was hers and it meant, in her, that she had finished the part of a thing she could say in one breath.

Haran lifted the failed lattice in two hands. He turned it. He did not say anything for a long moment. Then he set it down.

"How many people in the block," he said.

"Forty. Maybe forty-two."

"Cold."

"All of them. Last night down to freezing. They've been burning lamp oil for the heat. Lamp oil, Haran."

"How many do you train at a time."

"Four. Five if I push. And it takes, it takes weeks, you can't, you can't show somebody the inside of a lattice in a morning, the principle, the spacing, the way the tap-points—"

"I know."

"I can't be in two blocks at once."

"I know."

"And there are going to be more blocks."

"Yes."

Tesha took her hand off the bench. She looked at Ash. She had not, in the whole telling, looked at Ash. She looked at him now because she had said the thing about more blocks and she did not know what to do with the next sentence and she was looking for the next sentence on his face.

He did not have it.

He had her spring mechanism on the bench at his elbow, the small clever piece she had brought him a week ago, the improvement on his improvement, and his hand had moved to it without his asking, the way a hand moved to a thing that fit it. He took the hand away.

"Show me Berre's," he said. "This afternoon. After the district."

"You're going to the district."

"After."

"All right."

"And Tesha."

"What."

"Bring the sketch. The other one. The one off the lower terrace. Bring me the sketch too."

"They won't want."

"Tell them I want to see what they got right."

She looked at him. She nodded. She picked up the failed lattice and wrapped it back into the cloth, and she did it carefully, the way a person wrapped a thing that had hurt somebody, and she went out.

The door closed.

Haran did not say anything.

The forge ticked.

Ash stood at the bench and looked at the place on the wood where the failed lattice had been and where it was no longer, and he did not, for a long moment, do anything else.

* * *

Ryn came mid-morning.

She came in the way she came in lately, which was without announcement and with the small economical movements of a person who had been here often enough to know where to put a coat. She put the coat on the third hook. She did not go to the bench. She went to the stove and put a hand against the side of the kettle to see if it was warm, and it was, and she poured herself a cup, and only then did she come over.

She set the cup down on the bench beside Ash's elbow. She did not sit.

"Davan spoke last night," she said.

"Where."

"The square at Carter's Yard. Open meeting. Two hundred, maybe. Smaller than the autumn ones. Tighter."

"All right."

"It's turned."

"What."

"The speech. The whole, the way he speaks. It isn't redistribute anymore. It isn't even mostly redistribute."

"What is it."

She picked up the cup. She did not drink. She held it.

"He said, *we must be honest about what's coming*. He said it twice. He, he kept going back to it. The hope was the lie, that was his line. The hope was the lie and we're done with the lie now. He said something about, about the water in the southern terraces, about what it would mean when the lattices there gave, and he didn't say it like he was warning. He said it like he was telling them how it would be. Like he had already seen it."

"Ryn."

"He named you."

He nodded.

"You want it," she said.

"Yes."

She set the cup down. She put her hands in her coat pockets. She had not taken the coat off. He saw, now, that she had not taken the coat off. He let the seeing sit.

"He said," she said, and she stopped, and she got the line. She had got it before she came in. She had walked across the city with the line in her mouth.

"*The Wellspring chose to build water pumps while the world goes dark. History will judge the scale of that miscalculation.*"

She did not say it as a quotation. She said it the way she said any sentence, without theater, the words laid down one after the other, and that was worse, because the line did not need theater. The line carried itself.

He did not move.

He had thought, when this came, that it would land in him as anger. He had thought it for a month. He had rehearsed the anger. The anger did not come. What came was a small cold place behind the breastbone, and a slow distinct awareness of his own breath, and the picture of Davan as he had last seen him, in the doorway of the long hall in the Quarter, the one good shoulder a quarter higher than the other, the whole man inclined forward into a sentence he had not been going to finish.

"Ryn," he said.

"Mm."

"You aren't telling me about the speech."

She looked at him.

"No," she said.

"Tell me."

She took her hand out of her pocket. She put it on the bench. She had small hands. He had known that for a long time without having looked at it directly. Her thumb pressed against the wood and lifted and pressed again.

"I saw the same look on my father's face," she said, "the year he stopped trying to find work. The exact same look. He had it for a month before anyone else saw. He had it standing at our window in the morning. I was nine. I was nine and I saw it on him for a month and I didn't know what I was seeing and then he, he stopped. He didn't, he didn't kill himself. He stopped. He sat in the chair. He sat in the chair for two years."

"Ryn."

"Davan has the look."

"I hear you."

"He had the look last night. He had it before he started talking. I came across him in the alley behind the platform and he was standing there with his hand on the wall and he had the look, and then he went up and he gave the speech, and the speech was the look in words."

She stopped.

She took her hand off the bench.

"I'm not telling you what to do," she said. "I don't know what to do. I'm just telling you."

"All right."

"All right."

She picked up the cup. She drank now, finally, a small sip, and she set the cup back down, and she went to the third hook and she took her coat off and hung it properly, and she crossed back to the workbench and sat down on the stool opposite him and picked up the piece of soft wire he had been straightening when she came in, and she straightened it.

She did not speak for a long while.

That was how she helped.

* * *

He went to the district at noon.

The lower stairs were drying after the night's rain, and the stones were dark in the seams and pale on the tops, and the smell of the lime dust was on the air, and a cart had gone through earlier and left two wet tracks that did not quite parallel one another. He came down the stairs at the steady pace he had taught himself this winter, the one that used about two-thirds of what walking used to cost him. He had stopped, at some point, calling the discipline a discipline. It was just how he walked now.

The pump in Building Six ran.

He heard it before he saw it. The small steady tic of the lifting arm, off-rhythm with the larger thump of the cylinder, and under both of them the wet sound of water moving up through the pipe and into the cistern. A boy he did not know was standing on the wooden box beside the housing with a rag in one hand and a small pot of grease in the other, and the boy was greasing the joint above the lift arm with the slow attention of somebody who had been shown how a week ago and was not yet bored of being good at it.

The boy looked up. He saw Ash. He nodded.

"Going," he said.

"Going."

"Berman says the third bearing wants pulling at the next moon. Says you'd want to know."

"Tell Berman I trust his ear on bearings."

"All right."

The boy went back to the joint. Ash went on past the housing and into the courtyard.

The heating was on. He could feel it before he saw the lattice. The courtyard had the soft uneven warmth of the hybrid system, the warm patches and the cool patches and the long thin band of warmer air at chest height where the upper run of the lattice fed, and a woman with a child on her hip was standing in one of the warm patches with her face tipped up into it and her eyes closed, and she did not see him cross the courtyard, and he did not break her standing in the warm.

In the work-shed off the courtyard, two of the workers he had trained were teaching three he had not.

He stopped at the doorway. He did not go in. He watched.

Marren, the older of the two, was kneeling at a low bench with a section of the secondary lattice in pieces in front of him. He had a piece of chalk and he was drawing on the bench top, the small careful diagrams Haran had taught him to draw, the ones that showed not the shape of the lattice but the path of the heat. The three new ones were leaning over him. One of them, a girl maybe sixteen, was nodding too quickly. Marren paused, looked at her, and put the chalk in her hand without saying anything. He pointed at the lattice. He waited.

She drew.

She drew it wrong.

Marren did not correct her. He let her draw it wrong, and he watched her draw it wrong, and when she was done he tapped the place where the wrong began and did not say what was wrong, and she looked at the place, and she looked at the lattice, and after a moment she rubbed the chalk line out with her thumb and drew it again.

She drew it right.

Marren nodded.

Ash watched another minute and went on.

Three weeks since he had channeled here. He had counted, this morning, on the way down the stairs. He had not channeled in the district in three weeks, and the pump ran, and the heating was on, and Marren was teaching a girl whose name he did not know how to draw the path of heat on a bench top. The district breathed without him. He had wanted this. He had wanted it for months. He had said the words out loud to Haran, *I want them to do it without me*, and the words had been true, and he had imagined that when the day came he would feel a thing like relief.

He did not feel relief.

He felt the way a person felt watching a child cross a room without holding to anything for the first time, the way the love and the fear lived in the same place behind the breastbone, and the breath went uneven for the count of the crossing.

He turned out of the work-shed and went the other way along the courtyard, because the next thing was the thing he had to look at, and he had been delaying it.

The alley to the next block was twelve paces.

He took them.

The block on the other side was cold.

He could feel it in the air the moment he stepped through the alley mouth. The temperature dropped a clear three degrees. The courtyard on this side was empty except for an older man sitting on a stool by a doorway with a blanket over his knees, and the man was smoking, and the smoke went straight up because there was no wind, and the cold came down off the upper galleries the way cold came down a stairwell.

A woman came out of the building opposite and saw him.

She knew him. He did not know her by name. She came across the courtyard fast and stopped two paces short of him, and she did not, for the first long moment, say anything. She looked at his coat. She looked at his hands.

"I heard you'd come," she said.

"I came."

"They said you were across the alley."

"I was."

"Berre's on the third floor. She's been at it since the dark. She, she has been at it. I can take you up."

"I'll come up."

"It isn't, it isn't holding. We had three nights of it and then it stopped."

"I know."

"My mother is on the second floor."

"How is she."

"She is in two blankets in the chair. She is. She is sixty-eight."

He looked at her. He did not say what he wanted to say, because what he wanted to say was *I will fix it tonight*, and the saying would have been the old shape, the shape he had spent the winter putting down, and he could not, would not, pick that shape back up because a woman he did not know had a mother in two blankets in a chair on the second floor.

He said the thing he was learning to say instead.

"Let me see Berre's work," he said. "Let me see what she did. Then we'll know what we have."

"All right."

"And."

"What."

"Your mother. Get her down to the courtyard across the alley for the afternoon. The warm patches. There's a place by the southwest corner. Tell them I sent you. Tell them she's to sit there until evening."

"All right."

"Your name."

"Halis."

"Halis. Thank you for coming to me."

She nodded. She turned. She went toward the door of the building, and he followed her, and behind him the older man on the stool did not look up from his cigarette, and the smoke went on going straight up, and the cold came on coming down.

* * *

Berre's lattice was on her kitchen table.

He stood over it for a long time. He did not pick it up. Tesha would bring the other one in the afternoon. He looked at this one and made himself see it as it was, not as the thing it had failed to be.

Berre stood at the window with her arms folded. She had not said three words since he had come in. The kitchen smelled of cold lamp oil and of a kind of soup that had been made too thin for too many days. There was a child in the doorway behind him, small, watching, and Berre did not send the child away because Berre had stopped, for the moment, being able to do small things.

"Berre," he said.

"Mm."

"Look at this with me."

"I don't want to."

"I know. Look anyway."

She came over.

He pointed at the upper run. "What did you do here."

"I followed it."

"What did you see them do."

"They wound it left over right at the cross. I wound it left over right. That part is right."

"That part is right."

She looked at him. The looking at him was the first piece of work she had done in two days that was not wrong.

"Now look at the spacing," he said.

He did not say *and you'll see what's wrong*. He waited. He let her look. The child in the doorway breathed. The cold came down off the upper galleries. Berre looked at the spacing for the count of fourteen and her hand came up to her mouth and she said, "Oh."

"Yes."

"It isn't even."

"No."

"I, I measured by eye. I thought I had the eye."

"The eye comes after the rule. That's all. You were one step ahead of yourself."

"I cost them the heat."

"You got them three nights of heat that they would not have had if you had not done it. Three nights."

"And then nothing."

"And then we fix it. Tesha is coming this afternoon. I am coming with her. We will do it together. You will do it. We will stand at your shoulder. By tonight there will be heat in this block."

"By tonight."

"By tonight."

She put her hand flat on the table beside the lattice. He saw the tremor in it. He did not name the tremor. He moved his hand a finger's width nearer to hers on the table and set it flat too, and they stood like that for a moment over the failed lattice, and the child in the doorway watched, and the cold came down.

"Berre."

"Mm."

"You did the work. You will do the work again. The doing again is the same work."

"All right."

"All right."

* * *

He came back to the workshop in the afternoon with the cold of the second block still on his coat and a folded paper in his hand that was the sketch from the lower terrace, which Tesha had brought him at the alley mouth, and a list in his head of three things that needed doing before evening and four that needed doing this week and a longer list under those that he was not letting himself look at yet.

There was a message on the workbench.

It was on the bench under the small iron weight Haran used for letters. The paper was cream, not the gray paper of the workshop. The seal at the fold had been pressed and broken; Haran had read it. The hand on the outer fold was a hand he had seen before, on documents, on dispatches, on a single short note last spring that had told him an inspector would be at the lower terrace at the eighth hour.

Dorenne.

He set down the sketch. He took off his coat. He went to the kettle and poured himself a cup of water from it without lighting the burner, because the water was warm enough to do, and he stood at the stove with the cup in his hand and he did not, for a count of breaths, go back to the bench.

Then he went back to the bench.

He picked up the letter.

The first paragraph was the formal one. The Levelers' assembly, on the seventeenth, by a margin he did not need to read because the margin was named in the next clause, had voted to revoke the infrastructure authority she had held under the joint accord, effective at the close of the working week. She was, the paragraph said, no longer empowered to direct works of channeling, to schedule lattice maintenance on the public networks, or to issue requisitions against the storage banks of the corps.

The second paragraph was shorter.

It did not have the shape of the first. The language was the same language, the same careful institutional cadence, but the sentences had got shorter, the way a person's sentences got shorter when they were saying a thing they had not, until they sat down to write it, decided to say.

She had, the paragraph said, the use, until the close of her tenure, of the logistics network associated with the Office of Civic Maintenance: the courier lines, the supply registers, the cross-quarter scheduling rooms, the long-standing relationships with the freight masters and the warehousemen and the foremen of the outer works. She had also the institutional knowledge, which the paragraph called by its proper administrative name and which he understood to mean the names and the willingness of about three hundred people who would do a thing if she asked them to do it and would not do it if she did not.

She offered these.

The third paragraph was one sentence.

*I am not asking for a partnership. I am offering what I have left while I have it.*

He read it twice.

He set the letter down. He set the iron weight back on it, square to the corner. He did not, for a long time, do anything else.

Haran came in from the back room. He had a pair of tongs in his hand and a piece of bar stock in the other, and he saw Ash at the bench, and he saw the letter, and he set the tongs and the stock down on the second bench and came over.

He did not pick up the letter. He did not need to. He had read it earlier.

"Well," he said.

"Yes."

"What will you say."

"I don't know."

"What does it ask of you."

"It doesn't. It says it doesn't."

"Mm."

"It does ask. The asking is in the not asking."

"Yes."

He looked at Ash. He waited a long moment. Then he said the thing he had been going to say in the morning and had chosen not to.

"It is not the same Dorenne," he said.

"No."

"That matters."

"I know it matters."

"It matters and it does not solve it."

"No."

"You have to choose."

"I know."

"Today."

"This week."

"Today, Ash."

He looked at Haran.

He saw, for a half-instant, the man Haran had been thirty years ago, the one Ash had only ever seen the silhouette of in stories, the one who had walked out of the corps with both hands open and the long discipline ahead of him and no one yet at the bench beside him. He had not, before today, seen that silhouette through Haran's face. He saw it now.

"Today," he said.

"Good."

* * *

They went back to Berre's at the fourth hour, and they fixed the lattice with Berre's hands and Tesha standing at her shoulder, and Ash standing at Tesha's shoulder, and Marren from across the alley standing in the doorway with a piece of chalk in his hand and not coming any further in because the chalk was not needed yet, and by sundown there was warmth coming off the upper run in the second block, uneven, imperfect, real.

Halis brought her mother back across the alley at dusk. The old woman carried her own blanket folded over her arm. She did not look at Ash when she passed him in the courtyard. She looked at the stones. He watched her go up the stairs of her own building one at a time, and on the third stair she put her free hand against the wall, the way Halis had said she would, and on the fifth stair she did not, and on the seventh stair she did, and the door closed behind her, and Halis came back down to him in the courtyard.

"Thank you," Halis said.

"Berre fixed it."

"Yes."

"I did almost nothing."

"You did the part she could not do."

He did not answer. The part he had done was the part of standing there and not saying *I will fix it tonight*, and the part of not lifting the work out of Berre's hands when he could have, and the part of the breath at his shoulders that he had held even and did not let go of until Berre's hand on the upper run came up steady. The part he had done could not be named in a sentence to Halis. He let it not be named.

"Tomorrow," Halis said.

"Tomorrow."

"You'll be back."

"Tesha will be back. I will come the day after."

"All right."

She did not say goodnight. She turned and went to her own door and went in. The cold of the courtyard had started to lift a fraction. Not warm. Not yet warm. Less cold. He stood in the alley for a moment and felt the line of less-cold meeting the warmth out of the next courtyard, and the line was not even, and it never would be, and that was how it would have to be, and he went back to the workshop.

* * *

The workshop was empty when he came in except for Haran at the bench by the window, and Ryn across at the second bench with a bundle of split leather she was working through with a knife, and the kettle warm again, and the forge banked low for the evening.

He hung his coat. He went to the bench.

He did not sit.

Haran did not look up from the small piece of brass in his hand.

"You're not going to solve all of this," he said.

"I know."

"You're not going to solve most of it."

"I know that too."

Haran picked up a small gear mechanism that had been on the bench for two days, a thing he had been intending to set into a clock that was waiting in the back room. He turned it once in his fingers. He set it down. He set it down with the small final placing he gave to objects that were finished.

"Good," he said. "That's a start."

Ash did not move for a while.

He had been waiting, all day, for some sentence that would carry. He had not known he was waiting for it until Haran said it. The sentence carried. It did not solve anything. It carried.

He looked at the gear mechanism on the bench. It was a small thing. It would set into a clock in the back room and the clock would tell time for somebody on a wall for the next forty years. It would do that whether the Wellspring rose or fell. It would do that if the southern terraces went out. It would tic in a kitchen, in a back room, on a stair landing where a pipe was wrapped wrong. The gear did not know any of that. The gear was a gear.

He breathed.

He sat down.

* * *

Ryn was the last to leave.

She put the leather aside at the half hour past dusk. She pulled her coat off the third hook. She did not put it on. She held it folded over her arm. She came across the room and stopped at the workbench and stood a moment and did not say what she had come over to say.

He waited.

He had learned, this winter, how to wait for a sentence the way he had once known how to fill the silence in front of one.

"You look different," she said, "than you did six months ago."

"How."

She thought about it. She did not rush it. He watched her think. The thinking was not a small thing in her. The thinking moved across her face in the small adjustments at the eye and the corner of the mouth, the slow pulling-together of a thing she would not say until she had it true.

"Like someone," she said, "who's going to lose something and knows it and isn't running."

He did not answer.

She did not need him to.

She put the coat on. She did the buttons. She went to the door. At the door she paused with her hand on the latch and did not turn, and after a moment she turned the latch, and she went out, and the door closed.

He listened to her step on the outer stair. He listened to the second step. He listened to the third. He did not listen to the fourth because the fourth was the turn at the landing and he could not hear it from here, and after that the building swallowed her, and she was gone into the city.

He sat at the bench.

* * *

The workshop was very quiet.

Haran had banked the forge an hour ago and gone home. The kettle had cooled. The lamp on the second bench was the only thing burning, the small steady light of a wick trimmed by a man who had been trimming wicks for thirty years. The forge ticked as it cooled. A small wind came under the door and went up into the eaves and made one of the loose tin ridges sing for a fraction of a second, and was gone.

He picked up Tesha's spring mechanism.

He had not picked it up since the morning. It had sat at his elbow through the whole of the day, through Ryn at the bench, through the message under the iron weight, through his own going and coming. He picked it up now.

He turned it in his hand.

It was clumsy. He had thought so the first time she had brought it to him and he thought so now. The bend at the second arm was a degree off. The catch was set with too much spring; it wanted easing. The plate she had cut for the housing was a little oversize and would chatter at the corner if it ran cold.

It was better than his.

He had built the original last winter and it had worked and it had solved a problem and it was, in the way his work was always, elegant. The line of it was clean. The motion of it was small. It did, with a half a turn, what an older mechanism would have wanted three to do.

It was wrong.

He had not seen the wrong until Tesha had brought him hers. Hers was wider at the second arm because hers was made to be repaired by a person who could not see the inside of it without taking it apart, and his could not be repaired by such a person. His worked beautifully and broke completely. Hers worked clumsily and broke partly, and the part-broken state of it was a state a person could fix. His mechanism had been designed for a maker. Hers had been designed for a user. He had not, in the year of building, understood that there was a difference.

He turned it in his hand.

He could feel the reservoir.

He had not, until the long quiet of the workshop, looked at it directly. He had looked at it through the day in the side-glances a person used to track a thing they were afraid of. Now there was no day left. There was the bench and the lamp and the cooling forge and the small wind under the door and the spring mechanism in his hand and the reservoir inside him, and he set the mechanism down on the bench and laid both hands flat beside it and looked.

The bottom was there.

He could feel the bottom.

It was not a bottom in the way the floor of a room was a bottom. It was a thinning. A change in the texture of the thing inside him, a place where the substance of it that had once felt without limit, that had felt, in the way a child's lungs felt, like a thing he could not exhaust if he tried, became thinner and thinner and met something that was not it.

He had not been able to feel it last spring.

He had been able to feel it this winter only as an idea, the thing the discipline pointed toward, the abstraction that made him careful. He could feel it now. He could feel it the way he felt the rim of a cup with a finger. The bottom was a real thing. The bottom was not far.

The fear was there.

It was the old fear, and he knew it the way a person knew an old room in the dark. *Ordinary means worthless. Powerless means purposeless.* The shape of the words was the shape they had been at sixteen, at twenty, at twenty-three the morning he had stood at the river. It had not gone anywhere. He had thought, at one point this winter, that he would put it down. He had not put it down. It would not go.

It sat in him beside the bottom of the reservoir.

It sat in him beside something else.

The something else was harder. It was not peace. He knew what peace looked like in him and this was not peace. The problems were too large for peace. Two communities had gone dark this morning. Berre's lattice was warm and uneven and would need fixing again in a month. The sketch from the lower terrace was on the bench under his elbow, and when he looked at it tomorrow he would see, in the lines someone had drawn from memory, both the failure that was coming and the willingness of forty strangers to do a thing badly because nobody could come and do it for them.

It was not resolve. He had been resolved. The resolve had broken twice this year and he did not trust it as a thing to lean on.

It was a willingness.

The willingness to keep building knowing the building was not enough. The willingness to walk into an ordinary life knowing the ordinary terrified him. The willingness to trust that Berre, on the third floor of a cold block, would learn faster than he could go to her, and that Marren would teach the next one, and that Tesha would carry the part he could not, and that Halis's mother would or would not be sitting in two blankets in a chair tomorrow night, and that he could not, by any expenditure of any reservoir, prevent the *or would not*, only adjust the odds.

He sat with the willingness.

He sat with the fear beside it.

Neither of them won.

That was the new thing. The new thing was that neither of them had to win. They could sit in him at the same time and he could go on. The going on was the work. The going on was all he had been asked to do and all he had ever been asked to do, and he had spent twenty-three years thinking the going on was a small thing that was beneath his real life, and in fact the going on was the life, and the rest was the dressing of it.

He picked up the spring mechanism again.

He looked at the catch. He took up the small needle file from the rack at his elbow. He drew the file once, light, across the catch, easing the spring by the smallest fraction. He set the file down. He pressed the catch with his thumb. The catch moved truer.

He set the mechanism on the bench, square to the corner, the way Haran set things down.

It was Tesha's. He would give it back to her tomorrow. He would say the file had gone over it once. She would see the once. She would set it back into her work.

* * *

He banked the forge by hand.

He took the small iron rake from the hook beside the chimney and pulled the coals to the back of the hearth, and he set the cap over the embers, and he closed the damper to the second notch, the way Haran had shown him in the first week and he had then done a thousand times. The cap was warm in his hand for a moment after he set it. He let it cool. He hung the rake.

He did not channel the forge down. He had not channeled the forge down in a season. He had once thought, when he had first started doing this by hand, that he was performing a discipline. Now it was the way he banked a forge.

He took his coat off the hook.

He blew out the lamp.

The workshop went into its night shape, the dark of the forge cooling at the back, the dark of the high windows, the small line of street-light coming under the door. He stood a moment in it. He had stood in it a thousand times. He had stood in it at sixteen behind Haran's elbow with a feeling he had not had words for, and at twenty-three the day after the river when he had not known if he could come back into it, and at the end of the winter when he had stood here with the discipline new in him and the city going dark outside the door.

He stood in it now.

He went out.

* * *

The street was cold.

The lamps along the lower terrace were lit, most of them. Two were not. He counted them as he went, the way he had counted them all winter, and the count was the count it had been a week ago, which was three more dark than it had been a month ago. He went past the corner of the lane that led down to the cisterns. He went past the doorway where in the autumn a man called Jenner had used to sit playing a small flute for change and where there was no man and no flute now. He went up the long stair to the upper terrace.

On the wall beside the third landing somebody had drawn the Levelers' circle-and-line in chalk in the autumn, and somebody else had scratched it out in the winter, and tonight, beneath the scratched-out place, somebody had written, in a rough confident hand, in chalk that had not yet been rained on:

*WHAT NOW?*

The letters were the size of a hand. The chalk was new. Whoever had written it had written it tonight, perhaps an hour ago, perhaps less. The dust of the chalk was on the stone of the wall under the writing where the hand had brushed it as the hand had moved away.

He stopped.

He stood in front of the writing.

He did not have an answer. He had not had an answer in the workshop and he did not have an answer in the street. The city did not have an answer. Davan, who had been the answer for a while, had stood last night in a yard and used the word *honest* in a way that meant *no answer*. Dorenne had sent a letter that said she had no answer. Haran had said, at the bench, *that's a start*, and the start was not an answer either, only a place to begin from.

The wind came down the upper terrace.

It moved the dust at his feet.

He looked at the writing. He looked at the chalk. He looked at the place on the stone where a person's hand had been an hour ago. He thought, for a small moment, about lifting his own hand and taking up the small piece of chalk that was on the ledge below the writing and adding something of his own. A word. A mark. Some sign that he had been here and read the question.

He did not pick up the chalk.

He did not have a word.

The question was honest. The chalk was honest. The night was cold and the power inside him was nearly gone and the woman on the second floor across the alley from Berre's was sitting by a lattice that would need fixing again in a month and the rural networks had lost two more communities since dawn and his sister was learning, in their parents' apartment, how to boil a kettle by hand, and all of that was true at the same time, and the question on the wall held all of it at once and demanded no piece of it back.

He kept walking.

The terrace went up. The terrace levelled. He turned at the corner and went the long way along the upper street toward home, past the windows where the small lights were lit, the warm narrow points of channeled flame in the windows of the houses that still had it, and the unlit windows of the houses that did not, and the houses in between where someone had set a candle, and the houses where a thing was burning in a fire-pot, and the houses where the dark was complete.

He passed a child on a stoop. The child was eight or nine. The child was reading a book by the light of a small oil lamp set on the step beside her, the light bad, the book held close, the child's face frowning into the page. He went by. The child did not look up. He did not break her in the reading.

He went on.

A cart went past him going down toward the cisterns with two men on it and a load of rough lumber and one of the men was singing something low and tuneless, the way a man sang when he was singing for his own breath and not for any listener, and Ash heard the tune for a few measures and lost it as the cart turned, and he went on.

He passed the alley where, in the autumn, he had first met Tesha.

He passed the corner where, in the winter, Ryn had stood with him for a long quarter hour while a snow moved through.

He passed the place where his hand had been on the wall beside a wrapped pipe that morning at his parents' building, except this was a different building, on a different terrace, and the pipe at this corner was not wrapped wrong, and he put his hand on the wall anyway for no reason and took it away and went on.

The night was cold.

The reservoir was almost gone.

He was very tired.

He was also, in some small place, more himself than he had been in a year, and the more-himself was not a triumph and not a relief and not a thing he could have explained to anyone, even to Ryn, even to Maren in the kitchen this week, even to Haran who would not have asked. The more-himself was a quiet fact. He carried it the way he carried his coat.

He came to the door of his building.

He did not go up at once.

He stood for a moment on the step. He looked back the way he had come, down the long line of the upper terrace, the lights and the not-lights, the chalk on the wall four streets back, the city he had grown up in and would, in some shape that was not yet, grow old in. He looked at it for a long count.

He turned.

He put his hand on the latch.

He kept walking.
