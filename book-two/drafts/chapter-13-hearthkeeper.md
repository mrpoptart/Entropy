# Chapter 13

The broadsheet came in under Haran's arm with the rain on it.

It was the morning broadsheet, the second edition, and the rain had taken the top corner before Haran had got it under his coat, and the ink at the headline had bled a finger's width down into the white. Haran set it on the bench. He did not put it under the brass weight. He set it flat, with his palm on it for a count of two, and then he lifted the palm.

"Read it standing," Haran said.

Ash had been at the bench since the fourth hour. He had not slept past the fourth hour in three days. He set the file down. He came around to the side where Haran's palm had been, and he read.

The headline ran in the heaviest face the press kept. *Public Accountability Session at Halver's Square.* Underneath, in the lighter face, the subhead. *Elder channeler collapses on the platform.* Underneath the subhead, in the running face, the body, and the body was set as the press set the bodies of the broadsheets it intended to be quoted, which was to say without the small editorial flourishes the press used when it wanted to keep a free hand. The body was the report.

He read it through once. He read it through again.

The session had been at the second hour of the afternoon. The square had been full. The Levelers had set up a low wood platform at the south end, against the warehouse wall, and on the platform they had set a single chair and a basin and a length of clear pipe that ran from the basin out to the public heating manifold at the edge of the square. The pipe was a visible pipe. The visibility was the point. Channelers came up to the platform in turn, sat in the chair, put a hand to the basin, and gave. The crowd watched the water rise in the clear pipe. The crowd applauded what it could see.

Sera had been seventh in the line.

Sera, the broadsheet said, was sixty-two. Sera had maintained the public heating runs for the canal-block bath-houses for thirty years. Sera was on the named list at the lower threshold, and Sera had come to the session voluntarily, the way most of the named had come, because the alternative to coming voluntarily was being a name on a wall.

She had sat in the chair. She had given. The water had risen in the clear pipe to the mark the Levelers had set as the day's contribution, and the crowd had applauded, and Sera had started to stand, and someone in the front row had called out.

The press did not name the someone. The press said *a voice from the crowd.* The voice had asked her to give again. To show the crowd what an elder pool could do when an elder pool was not holding back. The crowd had taken up the asking. Sera had sat back down. She had given a second time, and the water had risen past the mark, and the crowd had applauded, and the voice had asked again.

She had given a third time.

On the third giving, the broadsheet said, she had folded forward over the basin. The Leveler standing at the platform had caught her at the shoulder. The crowd had gone quiet. Two of the men at the platform had taken her under the arms and lifted her, and her knees had not held, and they had set her down on the wood of the platform, on her knees, with her head on the seat of the chair, and her hand in the basin, and the water in the clear pipe still rising, slowly, because she had not yet pulled the channel back.

Someone, the press said, had pulled the channel back for her. The pipe had stopped filling. The crowd had not applauded. The crowd had stood, the press said, and watched.

The image, the press said, had been taken by the press-boy at the south corner of the platform. The image was on the second page. The press had set it large.

Ash turned to the second page.

The image was the image. It was a wood-block, which meant a press-boy had cut it overnight from a charcoal sketch a press-boy had made standing in the crowd, and the cutting was rough at the edges and exact at the center. At the center was Sera. She was on her knees. Her forehead was on the seat of the chair. Her hair had come loose at the nape and was falling forward across her cheek. Her hand, in the basin, was the hand of a person who had not put the hand there to give but to hold herself up. The two men at her shoulders were not faces. The press had made them shapes. The crowd behind the platform was a line of shoulders and the small carved suggestion of mouths, and the mouths were open.

He looked at the image for a long time.

Haran did not speak.

After a while Ash set the broadsheet down and went to the basin and washed his hands, which did not need washing, and dried them on his front, and came back. The rain at the window had thickened. The rain was the rain that came in the third week of the month most years, the long rain that took the city sideways and ran down the inside of the windows because the casements had not been re-sealed since the second winter. He looked at the rain.

"There's a worker," Haran said.

"Mm."

"He's downstairs. He was in the square. Tesha brought him."

"All right."

"He'll come up if you want him."

"Bring him up."

Haran did not move toward the stair. Haran set his hand on the bench, palm flat, the way he set his hand when he was choosing the next sentence with care.

"Ash."

"Mm."

"He was at the front."

"All right."

"He was the one who pulled the channel back."

Ash looked at him.

Haran's face did the thing his face did when he had brought a man into a room for a reason and was, before the man came up the stair, telling Ash the reason, so that Ash would not have to be told the reason by the man, who would not want to tell it.

"All right," Ash said.

Haran went to the stair.

* * *

The worker's name was Olen.

He came up the stair the way a man came up a stair he was not sure he should be on, which was slowly, with one hand on the rail, and at the top he stopped, and he looked at the workshop the way men looked at the workshop the first time, which was with the small careful look of a person who had been told about a place and was learning, in real time, what the place actually was. He was in his forties. He had a carter's coat on, the heavy oilcloth, and his hair was wet at the front from the walk over.

"Sir."

"Ash. Just Ash."

"All right."

"Sit, if you want."

He did not sit. He stood inside the door with his cap in his hands. Haran went past him to the kettle and put the kettle on, and the putting-on of the kettle was the thing that let Olen's shoulders come down a quarter, and Haran knew that, and Haran did it for that reason, and Olen did not know that Haran knew, and the not-knowing was the kindness.

"You were at the square," Ash said.

"Yes."

"Tell me."

Olen turned his cap in his hands.

"I've, I've not done this. I've not told it. I came to Tesha because Tesha was at the corner of my street last week with a broadsheet for the school and I, I knew her face. And I, I said to my wife this morning, somebody should tell him. And my wife said, then go. So I went."

"Thank you."

"I'm not, I'm not a Leveler."

"I didn't ask."

"I'm not against them either. I want to be clear. My wife's mother is in the canal block. The bath-house is what keeps her from the cough in February. I want the bath-house. I want it warm. I want the lattice fixed and I want it fair. So I went to the session. I went to see it."

"All right."

"I was at the front."

"All right."

He turned the cap.

"She was, she was small. Sera. I'd seen her at the bath-house at the warming hours. She'd come in with her sister. She'd done the morning channel for the south run for, I don't know, since I was a boy. She wasn't, she wasn't a public person. She was a woman who did the work."

"Yes."

"She came up. She sat. She gave once. The pipe filled to the mark. The crowd, the crowd cheered. It was, it was a real cheer. There were children up on shoulders. There was a woman beside me with her hand at her mouth. People were glad. I want you to know that. People were glad."

"All right."

"Then a man behind me, he, he called out. He said *more.* Just like that. *More.* And another voice took it up. *Show us what an elder can give.* And a third voice. And then it was a chant. *More. More.* And it wasn't, it wasn't an angry chant. It was the chant of a crowd that thought it was at a celebration. Like at the harvest fair. Like when the strong man lifts the second stone."

"Yes."

"She looked at the man at the platform. The Leveler, the one running the line. She looked at him to see what he would do. And he, he did the thing. He turned his hand. Like this." Olen turned his palm up, slowly, flat. "Like he was saying, your choice. And the crowd kept on. *More. More.* And she sat back down."

The kettle started.

Haran took it off. He did not pour. He set it on the trivet and stood at the kettle with his hand on the handle, and Ash saw, at the corner of his eye, that Haran's knuckles were white at the handle, and that Haran was looking at the back wall of the workshop and not at Olen.

"She gave again," Olen said. "The pipe went past the mark. The crowd cheered. And the man behind me, the same man, he called out *again.* And the chant came up again. And she, she didn't look at the man at the platform this time. She looked at the basin. She looked at it the way, the way you look at a thing you've decided you're going to do because the not-doing has become the worse thing."

"Yes."

"She gave a third time. And the third time she went over."

He stopped.

He turned the cap. He turned it the other way.

"I was at the front. I saw her face when she went over. She wasn't, she wasn't proud. She wasn't ashamed. She was, she was tired. She was so tired. She had been tired before she sat down, I think. I think she had been tired for a year. And she put her head down on the chair, and her hand was in the basin, and the pipe was still filling, and nobody was, nobody knew what to do."

"You pulled the channel."

"I, yes."

"You're a channeler."

"A small one. Small pool. I, I do my own house. I don't, I don't do public work. But I was at the front. And the pipe was still filling. And nobody was stopping it. And I saw her hand in the basin and I saw her wrist, the way it was, the way the wrist goes when the hand isn't holding the channel anymore but the channel is still open because the body has set it open and the body hasn't, hasn't pulled it back. So I, I reached in. I put my hand on the basin. And I pulled it back."

"All right."

"It wasn't, it wasn't hard. The channel was thin. It came back easy. She, she'd given everything. There wasn't much to pull."

"All right."

"And then the crowd went quiet. And the man at the platform, the Leveler, he, he said, he said *thank you, friend,* to me. To me. Like I'd helped. And the two men at her shoulders lifted her, and her knees didn't hold, and they set her down on the wood, and her head was on the chair, and somebody in the crowd called for a doctor, and a doctor came. From the crowd. There was a doctor in the crowd. He, he saw to her. He said she'd recover. He said she needed warmth and food and a week. He said it loud. He said it so the crowd could hear."

"And the crowd."

"The crowd left."

"Quiet."

"Quiet at first. Then talking. By the time I got to the corner of the square they were already saying it different ways. I heard a woman say *she gave brave.* I heard a man say *they pushed her.* I heard a boy say *did you see the pipe.* Everyone had a sentence. Everyone's sentence was different."

"Yes."

"I came home. I, I sat with my wife. And I said to her, somebody should tell him. The man at the workshop. Because the broadsheets are going to write it up, and the broadsheets are going to write it up six different ways, and somebody who was at the front should, should just tell him what was there."

"Thank you, Olen."

"I don't, I don't want anything from this. I'm not a Leveler. I'm not against them. I'm just, I'm a man who was at the front."

"I know."

Haran poured the tea.

He brought a cup to Olen, and Olen took it in both hands, and the cup steadied his hands, and he looked at the cup and not at Ash, and after a moment he drank. Haran brought a cup to Ash. Ash held it. He did not drink.

"Is she home," Ash said.

"She's at her sister's. The doctor went with her. The Levelers brought a cart. They were, they were good about the cart. I want to say that. The cart was quick. The blanket was warm. The Leveler at the cart, he stayed with her until the doctor said she could go. They didn't, they didn't leave her."

"All right."

"They didn't make her give again either."

"No."

"She gave the third time on her own."

"Olen."

"Mm."

"On her own."

He looked at Ash. He turned the cup in his hands.

"On her own," he said. "With a thousand people chanting *more.*"

Ash did not say anything.

Haran set his hand on the bench, palm flat, the way Haran did when a thing had been said that he did not intend to follow with another thing.

After a while Olen drank the tea down. He set the cup on the bench, carefully, at the angle a man set a cup down when he had been given the cup by a man whose work he respected. He put his cap back on his head.

"Thank you for, for hearing it."

"Thank you for telling it."

"All right."

He went to the stair.

At the stair he turned.

"Sir."

"Ash."

"Ash. The pipe. The clear pipe. They're going to use it again. They've got the platform built. They've got a session next week. They've, they've got a list of names already. I heard a woman at the corner of my street say her grandfather is on the list. He's seventy-one. He does the warming for the row above the cooperage. He's, he's going to go. Because you go, when your name is on the list. You go."

"I know."

"All right."

He went down.

* * *

Haran did not speak for a long time after.

He sat at his bench and turned a length of soft wire in his fingers and looked at the rain on the window. Ash stood at his own bench with the cup of tea in his hand and did not drink it, and after a while he set it down on the bench at the place his cup went down, and he saw, in setting it, that his hand was not steady. He looked at his hand. The hand kept on shaking. He set the hand flat on the bench and pressed down, and the bench was the bench, and the pressing down was the thing that worked when the hand would not stop, and after a count of ten the hand was still.

"Ash."

"Mm."

"The reservoir."

"I know."

"You haven't drawn."

"I know."

"All right."

"Haran."

"Mm."

"Thirty years of bath-houses."

"Yes."

"Thirty years of warming the canal block in February."

"Yes."

"And the crowd that, the crowd that got warmed by her work for thirty years was the crowd that, that chanted."

Haran set the wire down.

He did not say anything for a while. Then he said, the way he said the small things he said when he was sure of them, "The crowd doesn't know it was her. The crowd has never known it was her. That's the thing the lattice does. The lattice makes the warmth and takes the name off it. So when the crowd looks at the basin and the pipe, the crowd sees a basin and a pipe. The crowd doesn't see thirty years."

"They should see thirty years."

"They should."

"They don't."

"They don't."

Ash picked the cup up. He drank from it. The tea was cold. He drank it anyway, the way Dorenne had drunk from the cold cup in her office, and the drinking from a cold cup was, he saw, a habit that began on a day a person could not have told you afterward.

"Haran."

"Mm."

"I have to go see Davan."

"I know."

"I don't know what I'm going to say."

"Decide on the stair after."

"All right."

"Eat something first."

"Haran."

"Eat something first."

He ate. The brown loaf his mother had brought. He cut a slice and ate it standing at the bench, and Haran cut a slice for himself and ate it standing at his own bench, and the two of them stood in the workshop with the rain on the window and ate, without speaking, and when Ash had finished the slice he took his coat from the peg and Haran said nothing and Ash went to the stair.

At the stair Haran said, "Ash."

"Mm."

"He's not your enemy."

"I know."

"It will feel like he is."

"I know."

"He isn't."

"I know, Haran."

"Go."

He went.

* * *

The Open Hand had a runner at the door now.

He was a boy of fourteen, in a coat too big for him, and he stood at the foot of the stair to the cooperage and he had a list on a small board and he was checking names off the list as people came in. He looked up when Ash came across the lane.

"Sir."

"I need to see Davan."

"Yes, sir. He, he's in. I'll, I'll just, just one minute."

He bolted up the stair.

Ash waited in the rain. The rain had thinned to a drizzle but the stones were dark and the lane smelled of the brewery at the corner and the wet straw at the cooperage door, and he stood with his hands in his coat pockets and watched a woman across the lane bring a basket of laundry in off a line under an eave and bundle it under her arm. The boy came back down.

"He says come up. He says, he says now."

"All right."

The room above was as it had been. Liss at the south table, the ledgers, the man at the north window with the green wax. The two new tables. There were more crates of broadsheets at the wall than there had been on his last visit. The crates were addressed in the same clean hand. Liss looked up. Liss did not say *he's in the back.* Liss looked at his face and looked back at her ledger.

The back room.

Davan was at the desk. Korel was not in the room. The chair against the wall was empty. The pot of tea was on the wick-plate. Davan stood when Ash came in, which was new, and the standing was the small acknowledgment of a thing, and Ash saw the standing and did not say anything about it.

"Ash."

"Davan."

"Sit."

"I'll stand."

"All right."

Davan sat back down. He did not put his hands flat on the desk. He folded them in his lap. The folding was a posture, and the posture was a man taking a thing seriously, and Ash saw that, and he saw that Davan had been waiting for him.

"You read it," Davan said.

"I read it. And a man came to the workshop. He was at the front."

"Olen."

"You know his name."

"We have a list of who was at the front. He's on it. He pulled the channel back. The man at the platform thanked him."

"Davan."

"Say it."

Ash put his hands on the back of the empty chair. He did not sit. He gripped the chair-back and looked at Davan across the desk, and he heard, in his own ear, that his voice had gone to the quiet register that came before it went to the other register, and he kept it in the quiet.

"She collapsed. Your people watched her collapse and they didn't stop it."

Davan did not move.

"She was contributing to a community project," he said. "Voluntarily. People overextend themselves at work every day."

"She was performing for a crowd that would have called her a hoarder if she'd stopped. That's not voluntary. That's coercion with a civic face."

The room held it.

Davan's hands stayed folded. His face did not move. He looked at Ash the way he had looked at him at the bench in the second month, which was directly, and the directness was the thing Ash had liked about him then, and the directness was the thing that, now, made the conversation harder, because the directness meant Davan was not going to look away.

"What did you expect?" Davan said.

It was quiet.

"Ash, what did you actually expect? You have an ocean of power. She had a cup. She can refill it tomorrow. Tomorrow her pool will regenerate and she'll be fine. Your reservoir never regenerates and you're spending it on plumbing. Who's being more reckless with their power?"

"This isn't about me."

"It's always about you."

He said it without heat. He said it the way a man said the next move on a board, which was the way Davan had said *neither do I* on the last visit, and the saying was the move and the move was the saying.

"You're the most powerful person alive," he said. "You could heat every building in this city for a year and still have power left. Instead you're building hand-crank pumps and teaching people to insulate their walls with fiber. Every day you spend on your workshop project is a day the powerless go without what you could give them. You ask me about one woman who overexerted herself. I'm asking you about every child who's cold tonight because the Wellspring is building toys."

The word landed.

*Toys.*

Ash did not move. He did not let go of the chair. He felt the chair-back under his hands, and he felt the small ridge in the wood where someone had set a hot kettle down once and the varnish had bubbled, and he watched Davan, and he watched Davan watching him, and he did not say anything for a count of ten because he did not trust the first sentence that wanted to come.

The second sentence came.

"You know what they are."

"I know what they are. They're pumps. They're fiber. They're hand-crank lamps. They are the things people use when there is no magic left, and there is going to be no magic left, Davan, you know this, you have heard me say this, you have heard Haran say this, you have heard the Hall say this in three different voices. The reservoirs are not coming back. The pools are thinning. The lattice that warms the bath-house is being held up by twelve elder channelers and when those twelve are gone the lattice goes with them. The work in this room and the work in the workshop are the same work. They have always been the same work. You used to know that."

"I still know that."

"Then don't call them toys."

"I called them toys because that's what they look like to a woman in the canal block tonight. To her they look like toys. To her tonight she does not need a hand-crank pump in eighteen months. She needs a warm bath-house at the second hour of tomorrow morning. We can agree the pump matters. We can agree the long thing matters. You're asking me to be careful about how I get the warm bath-house. I'm asking you why the most powerful man in the city is not in that bath-house tonight, putting his hand to the wall."

"Because if I do it tonight I cannot do it next year, and next year there is no Sera. Next year the lattice has thinned by another twelve. Next year the bath-house is cold not because a woman is on a list but because there is no woman left to put on a list. I am not refusing to give. I am, I am refusing to give in a way that makes the next year impossible. That is not the same thing."

"From where she was kneeling it is the same thing."

"This isn't about me."

"It is."

"Davan."

"Say it differently, Ash. Tell me the bath-house is warm tonight. Tell me the canal-block elderly slept warm tonight. Tell me the building on Carver Lane with the cracked seal on the heating run had warm walls when the rain came in this evening. You can't. You can't tell me any of that. You can tell me you are building a thing that may, in three years, allow a worker to crank a pump to fill a tank that may, with insulation, hold heat in a wall on a mild night. That is a real thing. I have never said it isn't. But it is not warm tonight. And tonight is what we have."

"Tonight is what we always have. There is always a tonight. The tonight before this one was a tonight, and the tonight after will be a tonight, and the tonight after that. If the answer is always *tonight,* the answer is always to spend the reservoir on the tonight, and the reservoir goes, and the tonight after that has no reservoir to spend, and the bath-house is cold anyway."

"The reservoir is not the lattice."

"It is, if I spend it."

"Then spend a third of it. Spend a tenth. Spend whatever you can spend without ending the project. You have an ocean. Give us a cup. Give us the cup that warms one bath-house for one winter. You'll still have an ocean."

"And next year you'll ask for a cup."

"Yes."

"And the year after."

"Yes."

"And in twenty years."

"In twenty years there is no me, Ash. There is no me and no Sera and no twelve elder channelers. There is your reservoir, and there is whatever the project has built. That is the plan. That is your plan. And I am asking you why, in the meantime, while we are getting to that plan, while the lattice is collapsing and the elders are giving the last of what they have, you are not, you are not in the lattice with them."

"Because I have one of these."

He did not lift his hand to his chest. He did not need to. Davan knew. Davan had heard him say it before, at the bench, in the second month, when the saying had been a confession and not a position.

"Because I have one of these. And if I spend it, it's gone. And the thing it's gone for has to be the thing that, that is worth spending it for."

"Sera."

"Sera is one woman."

"Sera is the lattice."

"Sera is one woman, and the lattice is a system, and the system was built by people taking turns at it, and the system works because, because nobody in the system has had to be the lattice. If I become the lattice, the lattice has one tendon, and the tendon is me, and when I am gone the lattice goes."

"You are not the lattice. You are a man with a reservoir who will not open it."

The line went where Davan had aimed it.

Ash heard it go. He held the chair-back. He looked at the corner of the desk where the wood had darkened from the wick-plate, and he looked at the green wax on the table behind Davan, and he looked at Davan's hands, folded in his lap, still folded, and the folding was, he saw, the cost of the conversation. Davan's hands did not fold. Davan's hands at the bench had moved. The folding was the holding. The holding was the not-shouting. Davan was not shouting. Davan had not raised his voice once.

"I'm not going to oppose the ordinance," Ash said.

"I know."

"I'm not going to defend the lattice as it is."

"I know."

"And I'm not going to give you a cup."

"Why."

"Because you'd put the cup on a platform and the next day the line would be at the platform and the line would have a name on it, and the name would be Sera's, and the line would say, *He gave a cup. Why won't you give a thimble.*"

"That's not what we'd do."

"It is, Davan. Not because you'd want to. Because the platform has its own logic and the logic is not yours and is not mine and will not be argued with. You built the platform. The platform builds itself now. You saw what it did to her. You watched the platform do it."

"I watched the crowd do it."

"The platform put her in the chair. The crowd did the rest. The platform is not innocent of the crowd."

"Neither is the lattice."

"No."

A pause.

"Ash."

"Mm."

"I am not going to stop."

"I know."

"The ordinance is going to be enforced. The next session is on Thursday. The list is going up tomorrow. If you're not with us, you're, you're in the way."

"I'm not in the way."

"You're in the way to a woman in the canal block. Every day you don't open the reservoir, you're in the way."

Ash looked at him.

He looked at him for a long time, and Davan looked back, and the looking was the looking of two men who had sat at a bench together in the second month with a kettle between them and had argued about a thing, and had argued about it again, and again, and had, in the arguing, built the friendship that was the bench. The bench was not in this room. The desk was in this room. The desk was not the bench.

"Davan."

"Mm."

"You're asking the drowning to be patient while you decide which lifeboat to build."

He said it quiet. He had not planned to say it. It had come up out of the conversation the way a stone came up out of a river when the water went down, and he had said it before he had decided to, and once he had said it the room held it the way the room had held *toys.*

Davan did not answer.

He did not answer for a long time. He looked at Ash, and his hands stayed folded, and his face did not move, and after a while he looked down at the desk, and he looked at the corner of the desk where the wood had darkened, and he set his thumb on the dark place. He did not press. He set his thumb there.

"That's a good line," he said.

"It isn't a line."

"It is. It's a good one. I'll think about it."

"Davan."

"I'll think about it, Ash. That's what I can give you. I'm not going to stop. I'm not going to take the platform down. I'm not going to take the list off the wall. The session on Thursday is going forward, and the names are going up tomorrow, and the next ordinance will go to the Council in the spring. I'll think about your line. That's what I have."

"All right."

"You'll think about mine."

"Which one."

"All of them. Pick the one that hurts the most. Think about that one."

He stood.

He came around the desk. He did not put out his hand. He did not, Ash saw, want to put out his hand and have Ash not take it, and he did not want to put out his hand and have Ash take it and have the taking become a thing the room behind the door watched. So he did not put out his hand. He stood at the corner of the desk with his hands at his sides, and he looked at Ash, and he said:

"I'm sorry about Sera."

"I know."

"I am, Ash."

"I know."

"I won't do that one again."

"That one."

"The chair. The pipe. The way it was set up. We will not do that one again."

"Will you do another one."

A pause.

"Yes."

"All right."

"All right?"

"You told me the truth. I asked the question. You answered it. All right."

"Ash."

"Mm."

"Walk yourself down. Korel isn't here."

"All right."

He went to the door.

At the door he turned.

Davan was at the corner of the desk. He had not moved. His thumb was still on the dark place at the wood. He was looking at the place his thumb was.

"Davan."

"Mm."

"At the bench."

"Mm."

"You said once. At the bench. You said, *the day this becomes a job, find me a hammer and put it in my hand.*"

Davan looked up.

His face did the thing it had not done in the room until now. It moved. It did not move much. The mouth went, and the eye at the corner went, and the moving was the moving of a man who had been holding a shape for an hour and had, for a count of one, let the shape go.

"I remember."

"Mm."

"It's a job, Ash."

"I know."

"I haven't asked for the hammer."

"I know."

"I might."

"All right."

He went out.

* * *

The stair was the stair.

He went down it slowly because Haran had said decide on the stair after, and he was on the stair after, and he stopped at the second tread because the second tread had given a quarter under his weight and he had not noticed it on the way up. He stood there. He put his hand on the rail. The rail was cold. The cold was the thing it had been at Dorenne's stair on the day of the ordinance, and the thing it was now, and the cold was, he saw, the thing the city did to the rails because the city had stopped warming the stairwells when the lattice had thinned.

He went down.

At the cooperage door he stood under the eave and watched the rain, which had thinned again but not stopped. The runner-boy was still at the foot of the stair. The boy looked at him and looked away, the way a boy looked at a man he had been told was important and had been told not to look at. Ash nodded at him. The boy nodded back.

"Sir."

"What's your name."

"Ben."

"How long have you been at the door."

"Since the second hour."

"Are you cold."

"A bit."

"Do they bring you tea."

"Liss does. She brings it down twice."

"All right."

He stood in the rain a moment longer. He did not know what he was waiting for. He looked at the lane and at the brewery sign at the corner and at the woman across the lane who was now hanging the laundry back up under the eave because the rain was thinning and she had made the calculation he had watched her make backwards an hour earlier. He watched her make it. The calculation was small. The calculation was the kind of calculation a person made all day.

He went on.

* * *

He took the canal road.

He took it because the canal road went past the public heating manifold at the south end of Halver's Square, and he wanted to walk past the manifold, and he did not want to walk past it from the square side because the platform was still up.

He came at it from the canal side.

The manifold was the manifold. It was a stone box with the lead pipes coming out of it at four angles, and the pipes ran into the cobbles and out of sight, and the box had the small brass plate at the south face that the Hall had set there in the year he was born, and the plate had the names of the engineers and the date, and the plate had been polished, recently, by someone, because the brass was bright and the brass had not been bright the last time he had walked past.

He stopped at the box.

He put his hand on the stone. The stone was warm.

He had not expected the stone to be warm. The stone had been cold the last time he had walked past, in the second week, when the Levelers' contributions had not yet entered the manifold. The stone was warm now. Not hot. Warm, the way a wall was warm in a house in the third hour of an evening when the heating had been on since the morning and the wall had taken it. He kept his hand on the stone.

A woman came along the canal road with a basket on her hip. She saw him at the box. She slowed. She did not stop. She came past, and at the corner of the box she said, without looking at him, the way people said the small things they said in the lower city when they were saying them to no one in particular and to everyone:

"Bath-house was warm this morning."

"Mm."

"First time in three weeks."

"Mm."

"My mother went."

"Mm."

She went on.

He kept his hand on the stone.

He stood there until his hand was the temperature of the stone, and then he took his hand off, and he looked at his hand, and his hand was warm, and the warm came from the stone and the stone came from the manifold and the manifold came from the contributions and the contributions came from the platform, and Sera had been in the chair on the platform, and Sera had given three times, and the third time she had given on her own, with a thousand people chanting *more,* and the warm in his hand was the warm she had given, and the warm was real, and the cost of the warm was real, and both were real and both were true.

He walked on.

At the next corner he stopped at the broadsheet wall.

The wall had a new sheet. The new sheet had two sheets, side by side, because the press-boys had set them at the same time, and the press-boys had set them because the press-boys had understood, the way press-boys understood, that the city would want to see the two sheets side by side, and the city would want to choose between them, and the choosing would sell broadsheets. The two sheets were two views.

The first sheet had the wood-block. Sera on her knees. The chair. The hand in the basin. The hair at the cheek. The headline read *The Cost.* Underneath, in the running face, an account of the session, the chant, the third giving, the collapse. The account was, as accounts went, careful. It named the voice in the crowd as *a voice from the crowd.* It named the Leveler at the platform. It named Sera. It named the doctor. It did not name Olen, but it named *a channeler at the front who pulled the channel back.* It named the bath-house. It named the thirty years.

The second sheet had a different image. It was a wood-block too, by a different hand. The image was of the manifold. It was of the brass plate. The plate was bright. Around the plate, a small crowd of women with baskets, going into the bath-house at the corner. The headline read *Warm Walls.* Underneath, in the running face, an account of the bath-house at the second hour of the morning. The account was careful too. It named four women at the warming hours by name. It named the bath-house attendant. It named the temperature of the water at the second hour. It said, in the last paragraph, that the warming was the result of the contributions under the ordinance, and that the contributions had come from twelve named channelers, and that one of the named channelers, on the previous afternoon, had been Sera.

The two sheets were side by side.

He read both. He read the first twice. He read the second twice. He stepped back.

A man at the wall beside him, an older man in a fishmonger's apron, said to him, the way men said things to strangers at the wall:

"They're both right."

"Mm."

"That's the thing. They're both right."

"Yes."

"My wife went to the bath-house this morning. First time since November. She came home and she cried at the door because she'd been warm."

"Mm."

"And the woman in the picture there, she's at her sister's. My niece is at her sister's house. She brought the broth."

"All right."

"What do you do with that."

He did not know the man. The man was a man at a wall. He turned and looked at Ash for the first time, and he did not, Ash saw, recognize him. The man was a man asking the wall.

"I don't know," Ash said.

"Mm."

"I'm trying to know."

"Yes."

"I haven't got there yet."

The man nodded.

"My wife says, she says we'll know in a year."

"In a year."

"In a year we'll know if it was worth it. She says, she says we don't know now. We can't know now. We have to wait the year and then we'll see what got built and what got broken, and then we'll know."

"That's a good wife."

"She is."

The man went on.

Ash stood at the wall.

He stood until the rain came back, and the rain came at the corner of the sheets first, and the press-boys had set the sheets in the heavy paste that took a rain, but the rain at the corner went under the paste at the seam, and a corner of *The Cost* lifted, slowly, in the wet, and curled. The image of Sera kept. The image of the manifold kept. The corner of the headline curled. He watched it curl. He did not put it back.

He went home by the canal.

* * *

The workshop had the lamp at the door lit.

Sun had come and gone. The wick was at the right height. The flame in the cup sat where it should sit. He stopped at the door and looked at the flame. He had not, before this year, looked at a lamp. The lamp was a lamp. Now the lamp was a thing he looked at, because the lamps had been the first thing the city had stopped doing right when the pools had thinned, and the lamps had been the first thing to come back when the lampers had been retrained, and the lamp at the door was a lamp at the door, but it was also the small quiet sign of a thing that worked.

He went in.

Tesha was at her bench.

She had a second pump-head open. Mira was beside her, on a stool, with a cloth at her knee and a small cup of grease at her elbow, and Mira was watching Tesha work and was not, Ash saw, talking. Tesha had taught Mira not to talk while Tesha was working the seal, because the seal was the thing that took attention, and Mira had learned. The two of them looked up. Mira raised her hand. Tesha did not.

Haran was at his bench with the wire.

He had laid out a length of it on the bench, straight, and he was bending it at quarter-inch intervals the way he bent it when he was making a small clamp from scratch, and the clamp was, Ash knew, the small clamp Tesha had asked for the week before, for the second pump-head, because the first clamp would not hold the smaller bracket. Haran did not look up.

Ash hung his coat.

He went to the basin. He washed his hands. The water was cold. He dried his hands on his front. He came back. He stood at his bench. He picked up the gear he had filed in the morning. He set it down. He picked up the file. He set it down.

He sat.

He sat on the stool at his bench, and he put his hands flat on the bench, and he looked at the bench. The bench had a small ring at his place, where the cup went. The bench had a long groove at the south edge from the file Haran had given him in the second year. The bench had a knot at the north corner where the wood had been from the same tree as Haran's bench, because Haran had cut both benches from the same plank, and the knot in Haran's bench had been at the south corner, and the knot in his had been at the north, and they were, Haran had said, the bench's two faces of the same tree.

He looked at the bench.

After a while Haran came over with the bent wire.

"Tesha."

"Mm."

"Is this it."

She turned. She took the wire. She held it against the second pump-head, at the small bracket, and she nodded.

"It's it."

"All right."

Haran went back to his bench.

Tesha set the wire down. She came over. She did not sit. She stood beside him at his bench, and she put her hand on the bench beside his, not touching, and she looked at the bench too. She did not ask. She did not say anything for a count of ten. Then she said:

"Two truths."

He looked at her.

"What."

"Two truths. Mira said it. On the way back from the corner. She'd been to the wall. She saw both sheets. She said, *two truths.*"

"Two truths."

"The bath-house was warm. Sera collapsed. Two truths."

"Yes."

"Both true."

"Both true."

"Both not enough."

He did not answer.

She did not press. She stood beside him at the bench, and after a while she lifted her hand from the bench and went back to the second pump-head, and Mira moved over a quarter on the stool to make room for her, and Tesha sat, and she picked up the wooden pick, and she went back to the seal.

Haran came back to his bench with another length of wire.

He bent it. He bent it slowly, the way he bent a clamp when he was thinking, and the bending was the thinking, and the thinking went into the wire and stayed there.

Ash sat at the bench.

He picked up the gear. He set it in the small clamp at the corner. He picked up the file. He looked at the next tooth. He looked at the angle the eye did not want, and then at the angle from the other side, the way Haran had taught him, and he held the file at the angle Haran had taught him, and he did not, yet, file.

He thought about Sera.

He thought about Olen's hands turning the cap. He thought about Davan's hands folded in his lap. He thought about the manifold under his palm, warm. He thought about the man at the wall with the wife who had cried at the door.

He thought about the reservoir.

He thought about the reservoir at thirty-seven, and he thought about the thing inside him that the reservoir was, and he thought about how long it had been since he had drawn from it, and he thought about the drowning, and he thought about the lifeboat, and he thought about Davan's *I'll think about it,* which had been, he saw now, the only thing Davan could give him in that room, and which was, he saw, what he had given Davan back.

He filed.

The first tooth came clean. He filed the second. The second came clean. He filed the third, and the third had a burr he had not seen at the start, and the burr came off in four passes, and he filed a fourth, and he kept on, and the workshop kept on, and Tesha at her bench worked the seal, and Mira beside her watched, and Haran at his bench bent the wire, and outside the window the rain came back and went and came back, and the lamp at the door burned at the right height, and the curve outside the window was the curve, and the city beyond the curve had, in one day, asked an elder to give a third time, and watched her give it, and warmed a bath-house with what she had given, and put two sheets side by side on a wall, and the sheets had been read, and the sheets were both true, and the bath-house was warm, and Sera was at her sister's, and the manifold was warm under a hand, and a man had said *we'll know in a year,* and the year had not started yet, and the gear, in the clamp at the corner of the bench, was getting clean.

He filed the next.
