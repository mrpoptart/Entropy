# Chapter 10

The boy from Building Six came at the second hour.

Ash heard him before he saw him. The square at the second hour was the square at the second hour, which was the small noise of the morning crew on the pump and the bell at the bakery and the slap of a wet cloth on the basin's stone, and the running of a child's boots across the stones was a sound the square did not make at the second hour. The boots came at a run. The run was a run that knew where it was going.

Haran had heard it too.

He set the file down. He did not turn.

"Workshop," Haran said, without raising his voice.

The boy was at the door. He was perhaps eleven. He had a cap in his hand and the cap had been in his hand for the run, and the hair under the cap was wet at the edges from the work of running.

"Sera said come," he said. "Sera said come now."

"Building Six," Ash said.

"The pump."

"What's it doing."

"Not."

The boy said the word in the way a boy of eleven said a word he had been told to repeat, exactly, without elaboration. Ash registered the *not.* He registered, behind the *not,* Sera's voice the way Sera spoke at the morning meeting, careful, low, with the small flat at the end of every other sentence that came of saying a thing she was not sure she was allowed to say.

He took the bag from the hook by the door. The bag had pliers and a wrench and the small leather roll with the picks. It also had, in the inner pocket, the brass shim Haran had cut for him in the third week, and the two replacement gaskets Reska had cut from the old harness leather, and the bag was the bag he had been carrying to the buildings since the second month, which was the bag of a man who had decided that the work was no longer the work of the workshop alone.

"Bag," Haran said, without looking.

"I have it."

"Mm."

He went out with the boy.

The boy ran a half-pace ahead the whole way to Building Six, which was three blocks at the long edge of the district, past the cooperage door and across the second canal bridge and along the wall of the old dye-yard. The boy ran. Ash walked. The boy doubled back twice and then walked the second half because the second half was the half a boy walked when he had been told to fetch a man and had fetched him.

Sera was at the cellar door.

She had her sleeves up. She had her hair tied. She had, in the way she stood with the door propped open against her shoulder, the stance of a person who had been at the pump for the half-hour before the boy went, and had decided, in the half-hour, that the half-hour was over.

"It's the seal," Sera said.

"Which seal."

"The lower."

"Mm."

"I tried the gasket. I changed the gasket. It went again."

"Show me."

She led him down. The cellar was the cellar. It had the small lamp at the foot of the stairs and the dry smell of old brick and the water-smell from the second feeder and the sound of the manifold ticking the way the manifold ticked when the manifold was not pulling. The pump was on the west wall. The pump was the pump he had built in the first month. The casing was open. The lower seal was visible.

He crouched.

He looked at it for a count of ten.

The seal was the seal he had fused in the first month with a small pour, perhaps a tenth of a percent, to bind the brass collar to the iron of the pump body where the original fitting had cracked in the earthquake. The fuse was the fuse. It had held for eight months. The crack was not in the fuse. The crack was in the brass two fingers above the fuse, where the brass had fatigued at a join Ash had not, in the first month, replaced. The new gasket Sera had cut was good. The gasket was riding a brass that was no longer flat.

"It's not the gasket," he said.

"No."

"It's the brass above."

"I see it."

He looked at her.

She had crouched beside him. She was looking at the brass. She was looking at the brass the way a person who had been told the system worked looked at a part of the system that had stopped working, which was the looking of a person waiting to be told what to do.

"You see it," he said.

"I see the crack."

"You can fix the crack."

She did not answer at once.

She looked at him. The look was the look she had given him at the second-week meeting when he had asked her if she wanted Building Six and she had said yes and had said it the way she said most things, with the small flat. She had been at the cooperage on Second-day. He had not registered her there. She had been at the back, against the wall, with a child on her hip he had registered without registering whose child.

"I don't know how," she said.

"It's brass."

"I know it's brass."

"You can shim it. Cut a piece. Sleeve the join."

"I haven't done that."

"You've watched."

"I've watched you do it."

She said it without sharpness. She said it the way a person said a true thing she had not, until the saying, allowed herself to say. She said *I've watched you do it* and she said *do* the way another person would have said *fix,* and Ash registered the choice of verb the way he had registered, in the year, every choice of verb made by every person in his vicinity, which was the registering of a man who had been listening to people choose words for him for a year.

He looked at the brass.

He could fix it. He could fix it in twenty minutes with a sleeve and a small pour. He could fix it without a sleeve, with a fuse only, in five.

He looked at Sera.

She was waiting.

She was waiting the way a person waited for a man to do a thing she had asked him to come and do.

He fixed it.

He fixed it the way he had said he would fix it, which was a sleeve and a small pour. The sleeve he cut from the brass scrap in his bag. He hammered it round on the lip of the pump casing. He set it. He poured. The pour was a tenth of a percent. The fuse took. The brass above the fuse heated and cooled in the count of forty. He set the new gasket. He closed the casing. He primed the pump.

The pump drew.

It drew at the count Sera knew. The manifold ticked. The pressure came up. The water moved.

"It'll hold," Ash said. "It's stronger than it was."

"Thank you."

"Mm."

She did not, in the *mm,* meet his eye.

She closed up the cellar after him. She walked him to the stair. At the stair she said *thank you* again, and she said it the way she had said it the first time, and the second saying was the saying of a person who had registered, between the first and the second, that the thanks was not for the work.

He went up into the square.

* * *

He walked back the way he had come.

The morning was cold. The light at the second-and-half hour was the light at the second-and-half hour, which was a high pale light off the canal that did not warm the stone of the bridge. He crossed the bridge. He passed the cooperage. He passed the corner where Vell stood at the morning crew on the western pump, and Vell did not look up, and Ash did not stop.

He walked.

He registered, in the walking, the count of a thing he had not, in the first month, set himself to count.

She had sent for him.

She had not sent for Reska. Reska was the senior. Reska was the person in the district who had, in the year, learned the most of the work. She had not sent for Jora. Jora would have been at Building Four at the second hour and Building Four was two streets closer than the workshop. She had not sent for Haran. Haran was, in the district, the man who fixed brass.

She had sent for him.

She had sent for him because the seal was a seal he had fused, and the fuse was a fuse, and the fuse was the part of the pump only he could be asked to set right. She had not sent for the man. She had sent for the pour.

He walked.

He registered it without saying the registering aloud. He registered it the way a man registered an account he had not, in the year, opened the second column on. He registered it and walked the last block to the workshop and went in.

Haran was at the bench.

"Pump," Haran said.

"Brass above the seal. I sleeved it."

"Mm."

"Tenth of a percent."

"Mm."

Haran did not look up.

"She sent for me," Ash said.

"Mm."

"Not for Reska."

"Mm."

Haran filed.

Ash hung the bag on the hook. He took off his coat. He sat at the bench. He did not, at the bench, pick up a tool. He sat with his hands flat on the wood and looked at the wood. The wood was beech. The grain ran the way the grain ran. He had sat at the bench a great many times in the year and looked at the grain.

"Mm," Haran said, again, after a long time.

Ash did not answer.

* * *

The chimney in Block Three drew poorly on Fourth-day.

Tesha came and got him at the third hour. She did not run. She walked. She came across the square with her hands in her pockets and her cap pulled down against the wind off the canal, and she said *the chimney in eight is sour,* and Ash said *which flue,* and she said *the lower,* and he said *I'll come.*

He came.

He looked at the flue. The flue was a flue. The flue had a bend at the second course where Ash had set the brick in the second month, with a small fuse to hold the angle against the load of the upper pipe. The fuse was holding. The angle was true. The draw was poor because the cap above the roof had warped in the cold, and the warp was a warp in tin that a person with a hammer and a flat iron could mend in a quarter-hour.

He went up to the roof with Tesha.

"Cap," he said.

"Yes."

"It's tin."

"Yes."

"Hammer it flat. Set it on the seat. Reseat the seam."

She looked at the cap.

She looked at it the way Sera had looked at the brass.

"Show me," she said.

He showed her.

He did not, in the showing, channel. He took the hammer she had brought and the small flat iron from his bag, and he set the cap on the iron, and he hammered the warp. The cap came flat. He set the cap on its seat. He pressed the seam. The seam took.

He gave Tesha the hammer.

"You set the next one."

"There isn't a next one."

"There will be."

"Mm."

She did not, in the *mm,* sound like she believed there would be a next one she would set. She sounded like a person who had asked him to come up to the roof and had been answered by a man who had handed her a hammer.

He went down the ladder.

He went home the long way. He went past Building Six. The pump was running. Sera was not at the cellar door. The basin tap, when he passed it, ran clear, and the count on the slate, in Reska's hand, was the count of a morning that had drawn its share.

He walked.

* * *

The eastern row insulation cracked on Fifth-day.

The crack came in the cold snap that came down in the evening of Fourth-day and held into Fifth-day, and the panel that cracked was the panel at the back of Building Two on the east face, where the wrap met the new pin Jora had set in the third week. The pin had pulled. The wrap had torn. The cold had come into the wall.

Reska sent the boy.

The boy was the boy from Building Six, the same boy, with the same cap. He came at the half-hour after the third hour, and he said *Reska says,* and Ash said *I'll come,* and he came.

He fixed the panel.

He fixed it with a strip of leather and a small fuse at the pin and a re-laying of the wrap, and the fuse was a tenth of a percent again, and the wrap held, and the wall, when he laid his hand against it at the count of ten, was beginning to come back.

He stood at the wall.

Reska was beside him. Jora was in the alley. Two of the morning crew were at the foot of the wall with the rolls of rag wool and the small bucket of pitch. They were watching him.

He took his hand off the wall.

"Reska."

"Mm."

"Next time it pulls, you set the pin."

"I don't have your pour."

"You don't need my pour. The pin's mechanical. The leather strip carries the load. The fuse only holds the corner."

"Then the corner won't hold without you."

He did not, at once, answer.

He looked at the corner. He looked at the corner the way a man looked at a thing he had done in the second month and had not, until this morning, looked at, which was the looking of a man who had laid a piece of work and had not, in the laying, asked the question of whether the piece of work could be laid by another hand.

"I'll think about that," he said.

"Mm."

* * *

The cistern's intake valve stuck on Sixth-day.

He freed it.

He freed it with a small pour at the seat, and the pour was a tenth of a percent, and he stood on the catwalk over the cistern with his hand on the cold iron of the valve wheel and he counted, in his head, the pours he had laid in the week.

A tenth at Building Six.

A tenth at the eastern row.

A tenth at the cistern.

Half a percent over fourteen days, with the chimney a pour he had, in the doing, not made because the chimney had been a hammer and an iron. Half a percent on repairs. Half a percent the second week would not, on the count, be different from. Half a percent every fortnight. A percent a month. Twelve a year. Sustainable for the district he had built. Sustainable, at twelve a year, for the district. Sustainable for thirty-five years on his current draw if his current draw stayed flat, which it would not, because the current draw was the draw of a single district, and a single district was not the city.

He held the valve wheel.

The iron was cold under his hand.

He walked back across the catwalk to the door, and he went down the ladder, and he did not, at the foot of the ladder, look at the man at the cistern who had asked him to come up.

* * *

He called the meeting on Seventh-day at the third hour.

He called it in the workshop. He called the morning crews and the evening crews and the building leads and the two boys who had stayed. He sent word with the boy from Building Six, who carried the slips for the bakery and now carried slips for Ash. The slip said *third hour, workshop, all hands.* The slip did not say what the meeting was for.

They came.

Reska came first. Jora and Tesha came together. Sera came at the half-quarter. Vell came last and stood at the back, against the wall, with her cap in her hand, the way she had stood at the cooperage. There were eleven of them, with two of the building leads who had come in from the second block. The workshop was not built for eleven. They stood. Haran was at the back bench. He was not, this morning, working brass. He was not, in the morning, doing anything with his hands. He sat on the stool with his hands on his knees, and he looked, when Ash came in from the square, the way he looked when he was not going to speak.

Ash stood at the front bench.

"Thank you for coming."

"Mm," Reska said.

"I have a question. A few questions."

He paused.

"Who can fix the seal in the pump in Building Six. Without channeling."

Silence.

He had not been sure, when he framed the question in his head on the catwalk, whether the silence would come. He had hoped, against his sense of the answer, that it would not come. The silence came. The silence was the silence of eleven people in a room who had heard a question and were waiting, severally, for one of the others to answer it.

He waited.

He waited the count of fifteen.

Sera looked at the floor.

Reska looked at him.

"Without your pour," Reska said.

"Without the pour. Without anyone's pour. With pliers and a sleeve and a hammer."

Reska did not answer.

"Has anyone done it."

"You've done it."

"Has anyone watched it done. The whole job."

"Sera watched."

"Sera."

Sera looked up.

"I watched."

"Could you do it."

"I, I don't know."

"Mm."

He let the *mm* sit. He did not, in the letting, perform. He had registered, in the asking, that the silence was the silence he had walked back from Building Six with on Fourth-day. The silence had not been the workers'. The silence had been his.

"Who can adjust the chimney flue in Block Three."

A hand.

The hand was a hand from the second block. The man who put it up was the man who had come in with the second-block leads, broad-shouldered, perhaps thirty, with a tin-snip in his hip pocket. Ash had registered him without learning his name. Ash registered, in the registering, the failure of the registering.

"Your name."

"Dael."

"Dael. You can adjust it."

"I've worked tin twenty years. The cap is the cap. I can do the cap."

"And the flue inside."

"The bend."

"The bend."

"I've not done the bend with the fuse on it."

"Without the fuse."

"Without the fuse I can lay a flue."

"Mm."

He registered Dael. Dael was the answer to a question he had not been asking, which was the question of who already knew the work that the work had been laid over. Dael had been at the back of the room because Dael had not, in the year, found a reason to come closer.

"Who can diagnose the panel crack on the eastern row. Without me."

A different hand. Two hands. Reska's hand. Tesha's. Jora's, after a half-beat.

"You three."

"Yes."

"The repair."

"The repair, with the pin and the leather, yes. The fuse at the corner, no."

"Without the fuse."

"Without the fuse it'll pull again in a hard wind."

"It'll pull."

"Mm."

"Who can clear the cistern intake valve. Without me."

He waited.

He waited the count of fifteen again.

Nobody.

The nobody was a nobody he had registered before he asked. He had registered it, on the catwalk, with his hand on the wheel, in the way a man registered a fact he had not yet said. He said it now. He said it by asking the question and letting the answer be the silence.

"Mm," he said.

He looked at Haran.

Haran was on the stool. Haran was looking at Ash. Haran's hands were on his knees. He had not, in the meeting, moved.

Ash looked at the floor.

He looked at the floor the way a man looked at the floor when he had been the man asking the questions and had, in the asking, learned the answers.

"Thank you," he said. "That's what I needed."

They went.

They went without the chatter that came out of meetings the way chatter came out of meetings, which was the way the chatter did not come out of this one. Reska went last. Reska stopped at the door.

"You fixed them," Reska said.

"I fixed them."

"That's not nothing."

"No."

"Mm."

"Reska."

"Mm."

"It's not enough."

She looked at him a beat. She nodded the quarter-inch the way Haran nodded the quarter-inch, and Ash registered, in the borrowing, that the nod had become the nod of the district. She went out.

* * *

Haran did not move from the stool.

The workshop was the workshop. The lamp on the front bench was unlit because the morning was bright. The fire was banked. The kettle was cold. Ash stood at the front bench with one hand on the vise the way Davan had stood with one hand on the rail of the cart on Second-day at the cooperage, and he did not, for a long count, say anything.

"So," Haran said.

"You've seen this."

"Mm."

"How long."

"Six weeks."

"Six."

"Maybe seven."

"You didn't say."

"No."

Haran looked at his hands. He looked at his hands the way he looked at his hands when he was about to lay them on a tool, which was a look, and not a look that committed him to laying them on the tool. He laid them, finally, on his knees again.

"You needed to see it," Haran said.

"I needed to see it."

"Mm."

He did not, in the *mm,* soften it. He did not say *I'm sorry I didn't tell you.* He did not say *I would have, if you had been ready.* He let the *mm* be a *mm.* He had, Ash registered, watched the seal go in Building Six on a morning Ash had not been there to see it, which was perhaps Third-day of the second week, and had, on that morning, decided to wait.

Ash sat on the stool by the front bench.

"Tell me," he said.

"Mm."

"Tell me what you saw."

Haran reached behind himself. He took, from the back bench, the small length of brass he had been working in the morning two weeks ago, the blank, finished, smooth, with the small turn at the lip. He held it in his palm. He turned it in his palm once. He set it on his knee.

"You built it too well," Haran said.

He said it the way a man said a sentence he had been practicing in his head, not because he was performing, but because he had walked the sentence around for two weeks and had set it down a great many times against a great many shapes, and had decided, at last, on the shape it was. He said it with the small flat at the end, the way Sera said her sentences, which was, Ash registered, the cadence of a person saying a thing he was sure of and was not sure the listener was sure of.

"Too well."

"You built it the way you think. Elegant. Efficient. Optimized."

"Yes."

"You should have built it the way they can fix it. Rough. Obvious. Redundant."

"Yes."

He paused.

"A pipe a person with a wrench can understand," Haran said, "is worth more than a pipe that works perfectly until it doesn't."

He set the brass blank on the bench beside his stool.

He did not say anything else.

Ash sat with his hands on his knees the way Haran was sitting with his hands on his knees. He looked, for a long count, at the floor between the two stools. The floor was the floor. The grain ran the way the grain ran. He had laid a small fuse at the seam of two of the boards in the second year because the second board had sprung in the heat, and the fuse was, this morning, invisible.

"I built a school," Ash said. "I thought I built a school."

"You built a building."

"Mm."

"With a man in it."

"With me in it."

"Mm."

A pause.

"Reska," Haran said. "She knows the wrap."

"She knows the wrap I taught her to lay. She doesn't know the corner I laid."

"No."

"And the corner I laid is the corner that'll go."

"Mm."

Ash looked at his hands.

The hands were the hands. They were the hands he had at fourteen and the hands he had at twenty-three and the hands he had, this morning, at twenty-four. The fuses he had laid in the year were not visible on the hands. The fuses were in the walls and in the pumps and in the brass and in the seams of the floor of the workshop, and the fuses were the work of these hands.

"I have to tear them out," Ash said.

"Mm."

"All of them."

"Some."

"Which."

"The ones that won't fix without you."

"That's most of them."

"Mm."

Haran picked the brass blank up again. He turned it. He set it down.

"You'll know which when you start," he said.

"Mm."

* * *

He started on Eight-day.

He started at Building Six. He brought the bag and a second bag with him, and the second bag had the longer tools, and Sera was at the cellar door when he came down the steps. He did not, on the way, send word he was coming.

"I'm taking the pump apart," he said.

"You just fixed it."

"I built it wrong."

"It's running."

"It's running because I'm running it."

She did not answer at once.

She looked at him. She looked at him the way she had looked at him on Fourth-day, with the small flat, and the look this morning had a question at the back of it she had not, on Fourth-day, had room to ask.

"What do you want me to do," she said.

"Stand here. Watch. Hand me what I ask for."

"Mm."

He went down.

He opened the casing. He set the lamp on the stone shelf. He took out the lower seal, the one he had fused on Fourth-day. He took out the brass collar. He took out the iron of the pump body where it had been worked to fit the original fitting. He laid the pieces on the stone floor in the order they had come out, which was the order Haran had taught him to lay parts on the bench at the workshop, with the first out at the left and the last out at the right.

He looked at the pieces.

The brass collar was the collar. He had fused it. The fuse was the fuse. He could not, without channeling, take the fuse out. He could cut the collar and replace it with a collar a person with a saw could cut. He could change the fitting of the iron so that the fitting was a fitting a person with a file could fit. He could lay the seal on a gasket a person with leather and shears could cut.

He cut the collar.

He cut it with the heavy snips. He cut it slowly, because the snips were the snips, and the brass was the brass, and the cutting was the cutting of a man who was, in the morning, learning what it was to take an hour to do the thing he had done in twenty minutes with a pour. He cut it. He laid the pieces on the stone. He went up to the workshop and got a length of plain brass tube from the rack, and he cut a new collar from the tube, on the bench, with the snips, and he hammered it round on the lip of the bench-mounted form. He came back. He set it.

It did not, the first time, sit.

He took it out. He filed the inner lip. He set it again. It seated.

He looked at Sera.

"Snips," he said. "Tomorrow you cut the next collar."

"There won't be a next collar."

"There will. The pump on Block Two will go in a month. The pump on Building Four will go in two. They'll go because I built them like this one."

"Mm."

He fitted the new collar. He fitted the new gasket. He did not, in the fitting, fuse. He set the seal mechanically. He clamped it. He set the clamp pressure with the small spring scale Haran had given him in the second year and not, in the year, asked him to give back. He primed the pump.

The pump drew.

It drew at the count Sera knew. The manifold ticked. The pressure came up.

It came up slow.

He registered, in the slow, that the pump was drawing at perhaps eighty in the hundred, which was a draw that would not, in the first ten minutes, fill the basin at the count of two, but at the count of two and a half. The cap on the bench beside the crank, the cap with the small chip in the lacquer, was the cap that had gone on a pump that pulled a hundred. This morning the cap was on a pump that pulled eighty.

His hand came up.

It came up halfway to the seal. He felt it go. He felt the small lift in the chest that came at the back of a pour, which was the lift of a person preparing to spend a thing he had spent in a thousand small spendings and would, this morning, not spend.

He stopped his hand.

He stopped it on the way and he set it on the cold iron of the pump body and he held it there, on the iron, with his palm flat, and he counted under his breath the way Vell counted the prime on the western pump. *One, two, three, prime.*

He took his hand off.

He put both hands on his knees.

"It's slow," Sera said.

"It's slow."

"Will it run."

"It'll run. The basin will fill in two and a half instead of two. The crew will lift forty a minute instead of forty-five, because the seat's a hand under the optimum. The pump will run. It'll need the gasket changed every six weeks instead of every twelve."

"Six."

"Six."

"Mm."

She looked at the pump. She looked at it the way she had looked at the brass on Fourth-day. The looking was a different looking. The pump was, this morning, a pump she had watched come apart and go back together, and she knew the order of the pieces on the stone floor.

"I can change the gasket," she said.

"You can change the gasket."

"In six weeks."

"In six weeks. And when the collar fatigues, you can cut the new collar."

"In a year."

"In a year."

"Mm."

He stood up.

He stood up the way a man stood up after kneeling for an hour on stone, slowly. His knees were not the knees of a man who knelt on stone often. He brushed the dust from the knees of his trousers. He looked at the pump.

The pump pulled at eighty.

He left it pulling at eighty.

It was, he registered, walking up the cellar stair behind Sera, the hardest thing he had done since the earthquake.

* * *

He worked through the second week of the eighth-month at the back of the eastern row.

He pulled out the pin Jora had set at the corner of Building Two on the morning of the cold snap, and he laid a new pin, plain iron, set with mortar and a wedge, with no fuse. He pulled out the lath he had laid in the third month behind the wrap on Building One, and he laid a new lath with a doubled batten and a screw at every joint. He pulled out the small fuse he had laid at the lip of the cap on the chimney of Block Three, and he showed Dael how to set the cap with a flat iron and a hammer and a strip of solder, and Dael set the next cap, on the chimney of Block Four, with the flat iron and the hammer, and the cap held.

The work was slow.

The work was the work of a man unbuilding what he had built. He had, at first, an instinct to do the unbuilding fast, because the instinct of a man unbuilding his own work was the instinct to be done, and to be done he had to have, in the doing, the speed he had had in the building. He had built fast. He had built at the rate of a man with a Wellspring who was building a district, which was a rate.

He could not unbuild at the rate.

He could not, because the unbuilding had to leave, in its place, work a person with a wrench could understand, and the work a person with a wrench could understand was work that took, by the measure of a wrench, a day to do what a fuse had done in a minute.

He spent the days at the back of the eastern row.

He spent them with Reska beside him, at first, and then with Reska on the next building, and then with Tesha at the chimney of Block Four, and then with Jora at the lath of Building One, and the work, on each building, took the length of the day. He did not, on most of the days, channel. He channeled twice. The first was a small fuse at the cracked corner of the southern wall of Building Three, which had pulled in a way that would have been mended with a fuse in the second month and, in the second week of the eighth-month, was a wall whose mending was not, on the day, his to refuse. The second was the wedge of the cellar door of Building Five, which Reska had been trying to fit for the count of an hour, and Ash, against his rule for the day, had, with one finger and a small pour, set the wedge into the masonry, and Reska had said *thank you,* and Ash had said *I shouldn't have done that,* and Reska had said *I know.*

The pump in Building Six pulled at eighty.

He walked past it twice a day on the way to the eastern row and on the way back, and he did not, on either passing, lay a hand on it. Sera did the gasket on the third week, with him standing at the cellar door and watching, and the gasket took her forty minutes, and the pump came back to seventy-five, and Sera said *I think I cut the leather wrong,* and Ash said *the next one will be straighter,* and Sera said *mm,* and went back up the stair.

He stood on the stair behind her.

He stood on the stair with his hand against the cellar wall and he felt, against the palm, the cold of the stone of the cellar wall, and he felt the pull of the pump in the wall as a small even mechanical pull, and the pull was at seventy-five, and the pull would come back to eighty when Sera cut the next gasket straighter, and the pull would not, while he lived in this district, come back to a hundred.

He took his hand off the wall.

* * *

Davan came on the fourth-day of the third week.

He came alone.

He came on foot at the third hour with no cart and no organizers, and he came to the workshop door the way a man came to a workshop door who had been to the workshop door before, which was without ceremony. Haran was at the bench. Ash was at the back, sorting a box of pins. Davan tapped the frame once.

"Davan."

"Ash."

"Tea's hot."

"Thank you."

Haran poured. He poured three cups. He did not, in the pouring, say anything to Davan. He set the cup on the bench at Davan's elbow and went back to the brass.

Davan looked at the bench.

"You're tearing it apart."

"I'm tearing it apart."

"Show me."

He showed him.

He took him to Building Two, to the back, to the eastern wall where the new lath was half-laid and the old wrap was in a pile on the stones with the small fuse at the corner pulled clean of the wall and lying, like a small grey clot, on the top of the pile.

Davan crouched.

He picked up the clot. He turned it. He looked at it. He set it down.

"This was holding."

"It was holding. With me."

"Without."

"Without me, in the cold snap, the corner pulled. The wrap came off. The wall went cold."

"And the new pin."

"Iron. Mortar. Wedge. A second-year apprentice could set it."

"With a wrench."

"With a hammer."

Davan smiled.

He smiled the small half-formed smile he had smiled at the bench on Fourth-day two weeks ago, when he had said *I never know* about the beech, which was a smile he gave the bench more than he gave Ash. He set the clot back on the pile.

"Show me the rest," he said.

Ash showed him.

He showed him the chimney of Block Four where Dael had set the new cap. He showed him the cellar at Building Six where Sera had cut the gasket. He showed him the lath of Building One where Jora had screwed the batten. Davan crouched at each and put his hand against the work the way he had put his hand against the riser at the visit two weeks ago, and he watched the work without commenting on it, and at Building Six he stood on the cellar stair with his head turned slightly to the side and listened to the pump pull at seventy-five for the count of thirty.

"It's slower," he said.

"It's slower."

"By how much."

"Twenty in the hundred."

"And the basin fills."

"In two and a half."

"Mm."

He did not, in the *mm,* say what he had said at the riser two weeks ago about the cost. He did not name a cost. He listened to the pump for another ten counts. He turned and went up the stair.

"Help me with the lath at Building One," Ash said.

"Yes."

He helped.

He helped the way a man who had fitted with his uncle for six years in the mill quarter helped, which was with his hands on the work and his mouth quiet. He held the batten while Ash drove the screws. He cut the leather strip with the knife Reska had given him and Ash had given him and he had, in the first cut, looked at the angle of the strip and recut it without being told. He set the small wooden cleat at the bottom of the wrap with a tap of the hammer that did not, in the tap, mar the lath. He worked beside Ash through the half-hour to the fourth hour and the half to the fifth.

They did not talk much.

They talked the way two people worked together who had, in the day, decided to work together and to let the work be the work. Davan asked, twice, where a tool was. Ash asked, twice, where Davan had set down a piece. Reska, when she came past at the half of the fifth hour, registered Davan and registered, in the registering, that Davan was on the work and was on it without asking to be, and she nodded the quarter-inch and went on.

At the sixth hour, they stopped.

They stopped because the light at the sixth hour was the light at the sixth hour, which was the light a man worked outside walls in until he could not see the work clearly, and they could not see the work clearly. Davan stood. He stretched. He brushed his palms against his coat.

"Eat," Ash said.

"Eat."

"Workshop."

"The workshop."

* * *

Haran was not at the workshop.

The note was on the bench. *In the square. Bread.* The kettle was hot. The brown pot was set out. The two cups were on the bench beside the pot, and the third cup, the cup Haran kept for the boy from the bakery, was on the shelf, where it stayed when Haran was not in the room.

Ash registered it.

He registered, in the registering, that Haran had gone out for a count of an hour the way Haran had gone out for a count of an hour at the cooperage two weeks ago, which was the count of an hour Haran went out when Haran did not want to be in the room.

He took the loaf from the basket on the bench and the cheese from the cold shelf and the small jar of pickle Ash's mother had brought down at the start of the eighth-month and had not, in the bringing, said anything to Ash about. He laid the loaf and the cheese and the pickle on a board. He set the board on the front bench. He poured tea. He sat on the crate. Davan sat on the stool at the back bench, the stool Haran had been on at the meeting on Seventh-day.

They ate.

They ate without talking for a count of two minutes. Davan ate the way a man ate who had worked outside for four hours, which was steadily. Ash ate the way he ate, which was less. He drank tea. He set the cup down. He set the cup down on the bench and he watched the surface of the tea settle in the cup, and he did not, on the cup, see the small skin Davan's tea had had at the seventh hour two weeks ago.

"My sister," Davan said.

He said it without preamble. He said it the way he had said *my mother is a weaver* on the bench two weeks ago, which was the way a man said a sentence he had decided, without making a decision he could later point to, to say.

Ash looked up.

"Younger," Davan said. "She's nineteen."

"Mm."

"Her pool came in at fourteen. It's been settling since. It settled this winter."

"At what."

"At what a person uses to light a candle, if they're patient with the candle."

He said *patient* with the small flat. The flat was the flat Sera said her sentences with. Ash registered it. He did not, in the registering, say.

"She fits at the same shop my uncle had," Davan said. "Since she was fifteen. New owner. He took her on because he was a friend of my uncle and a debtor of my mother. She's good at it. She's better at it than I was."

"Mm."

"She lives at home. She walks to the shop in the morning. She walks back at the seventh hour. She channels twice a day. She channels the kettle in the morning and the lamp in the evening."

He paused.

He set down the bread he had been holding.

"That's her life," he said.

"Mm."

"She's nineteen."

"Yes."

"She'll be sixty if she's lucky. She'll channel a kettle and a lamp for forty years, and the kettle and the lamp will be the kettle and the lamp the city stopped fitting twenty years ago, and when one of them goes she'll go without."

He picked up the cup. He held it. He did not drink. He set it down.

"She doesn't need your proof of concept," Davan said.

Ash did not answer at once.

He registered the sentence. He registered, in the sentence, that Davan had not said *she doesn't need a pump.* He had said *your proof of concept,* which was the phrase the broadsheet had used, and which Davan had not, in the visit at the cooperage and not in the visit to the bench and not on Fourth-day, used to him. He had said it now, this evening, on the stool at the back bench, with the lath of Building One behind his hands.

"She needs the people who still have power," Davan said, "to share it while there's time."

The sentence sat.

It sat the way *it is not enough* had sat on the bench two weeks ago, which was a sentence a man said in the middle of an evening that did not, after the saying, return to the evening it had been before the sentence.

Ash held the cup.

He set the cup down.

He looked at Davan. Davan had not, in the saying, raised his voice. He had said the sentence the way he had said *the boy is not wrong* at the bench two weeks ago, which was a saying without weight on the verb. The flatness of it was the thing.

"I've been watching Haran," Ash said.

"Mm."

"For a year. For longer. Since I was fourteen, but with the count on it for the year. The way he heats the workshop. The way he warms a cup. The way he sets a fuse."

"Yes."

"He uses, in a day, perhaps a hundredth of what most channelers his age use. For the same effect."

Davan looked at him.

He had not, in the looking, registered the sentence yet. He had registered, in the look, the direction of the sentence, and was waiting for the rest.

"He's efficient," Davan said.

"More than efficient. He's, the word is wrong, but the thing is real. He gets ten times what another channeler would get from the pour. Maybe more. Most channeling I've watched, in the year, in council chambers and at the basin and in my own pours, wastes most of what it spends. The pour goes in. A fraction does the work. The rest goes off as heat or wash or nothing."

"You think."

"I've watched it. I've watched him."

"Mm."

Davan picked up the bread. He set it down. He had picked it up, Ash registered, with his right hand. He set it down with the same hand. The hand stayed flat on the bench beside the bread for a count Ash registered without counting.

"You think there's a way," Davan said, "to do more with less."

"I think, watching him, that there might be. I haven't, in the year, run the experiment. I've been building. But the gap I've watched is real."

"How much less."

"I don't know. A fraction. Half. A tenth, in some pours. It depends on what the pour is for."

"And if you ran the experiment."

"I'd start with myself. Then with the workers in this district, the ones who have a small pool. I'd see if the gap closes when the technique changes."

"Mm."

He did not, in the *mm,* say *that's interesting.* He did not say *go on.* He looked at the bench.

He looked at the bench for a count of fifteen.

"You're describing," Davan said, "a solution for people who still have something."

He said it without raising his voice.

"My sister has nothing to be efficient with," he said.

He set the piece of bread down.

He set it down without the smallness of his earlier setting down. He set it on the board the way a man set down a thing he had decided he was finished with, which was, in the table-language Ash had been reading at his mother's table for twenty-three years, the small clean sign of a thing being over.

Ash did not answer.

He did not answer because the answer he had was the answer he had had at the door of the workshop on Fourth-day two weeks ago, which was no answer.

He drank his tea.

Davan drank his.

The kettle was still warm on the stove. The bread was on the board. The lamp was lit because the sixth-and-half hour was the sixth-and-half hour, and the workshop without a lamp at the sixth-and-half hour was a workshop a man could not see the bench in.

"It's not a refusal," Ash said, after a long time. "What I'm building. It isn't."

"I know."

"I know you know."

"Mm."

"And the pour you're asking for. It isn't a refusal either, the asking. I know that."

"Mm."

"I want both."

Davan looked at him.

"I want," Ash said, "the pour that warms your sister this winter, and I want the pump that warms the canal quarter in five. I haven't, on the bench, found the way to want both at once that doesn't, in the wanting, undo one or the other."

"Mm."

"I'm trying."

"I know you're trying."

He said it without weight. He said it with the small flat that was, this evening, against him without being against him, the way Sera had said *thank you* twice on Fourth-day with the small flat.

They sat.

They sat for a long time. The lamp burned. The kettle cooled. Haran did not come back.

At the eighth hour, Davan stood.

He stood the way he had stood on Fourth-day, slowly, with one hand on the bench. He did not stretch. He took up his cap.

"Thank you for the work," he said.

"Thank you for coming."

"Mm."

He went to the door. He stopped at the door. He did not, this evening, turn at the door the way he had turned at the door on Fourth-day. He set his cap on his head. He opened the door.

"Tell Haran I missed him," he said.

"I'll tell him."

"Mm."

He went out.

The door closed.

* * *

The workshop was quiet.

Ash sat on the crate. He sat with his hands on his knees. The lamp burned. The bread was on the board with the cheese half-cut and the pickle in the jar and the two cups on the bench, one Davan's and one his, both empty. Haran's third cup was on the shelf.

He sat for a long count.

He sat the way a man sat at the end of a day in which he had walked, in the day, the length of a thought he had not, on Fourth-day, known the length of, and had, at the end of the walking, found that the thought was longer than the day. He had unbuilt three buildings' worth of work in three weeks. He had asked eleven people one question and had heard the answer in their silence. He had said, on Seventh-day morning, *it's not enough* a second time, to Reska, in the doorway, and Reska had nodded the quarter-inch.

He had said, this evening, to Davan, *I want both.*

He had not, this evening, had an answer for the wanting.

He looked at the under-shelf at the back bench.

The cloth was on it. The shape of the things under the cloth was the shape it had been on Fourth-day, which was the broadsheet and the transcript and the small printed sheet from the platform. He did not, this evening, lift the cloth.

He stood.

He cleared the bread. He covered it. He set the cheese on the cold shelf. He capped the pickle. He rinsed the cups, one and two. He set them on the rack. He banked the stove. He set the kettle on the cold side. He took the lamp off the bench and set it on the hook by the stair.

He went up the stair.

He lay on the bedroll. He did not light the lamp.

The reservoir sat. He registered it. Half a percent in the second week. Two-tenths in the third. He had spent the wedge at Building Five against his rule. He had spent the corner at Building Three against his rule. He had not spent, in the morning, on the pump at Building Six, when his hand had come up halfway to the seal and he had stopped it.

The pump in Building Six pulled at seventy-five.

It would pull at seventy-five tonight. It would pull at seventy-five in the morning. It would pull at seventy-five for six weeks until Sera cut the next gasket, and the next gasket would be straighter, and it would pull at eighty, and the basin in Building Six would fill at the count of two and a half and would, at the count of two and a half, fill.

He thought *I built a school.*

He thought *and I built a building.*

He thought *and the building was the building of a man who thought he could be the school.*

He thought *and the man cannot.*

He thought it the way he had thought, two weeks ago, *I could warm them* and *I have not,* which was the way a man thought two things that were, simultaneously, true.

He did not sleep for a long time.

When he slept, he slept the way a man slept who had spent the day with his hands on a piece of work he had laid in his own first year and had unlaid, deliberately, in the morning, and had stood beside while another person laid it back in a way that did not, in the laying, hold as well as his had held, and had let it stand.

Below, in the workshop, Haran came back at some hour.

Ash heard him on the stair without coming up. He heard the door of the workshop and the stove being unbanked and the kettle being moved. He heard, after a long count, the small sound of brass on the bench.

He did not call down.

The workshop was the workshop.

The pump in the square was warm.

The pump in Building Six pulled at seventy-five.

The dark windows of the canal quarter were dark.
