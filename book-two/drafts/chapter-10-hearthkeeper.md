# Chapter 10

The boy from Building Six came across the square at the second hour.

He was not a boy. Ash registered this on the second look. He had registered him as a boy on the first look because the running was a boy's running, knees too high and elbows out, and the cap was on backwards because the cap had turned in the wind and he had not stopped to right it. He came across the stones at a half-run that broke into a walk at the basin and broke back into a half-run at the workshop door. He was sixteen, perhaps seventeen. He had been on the morning crew for three weeks. His name was Tervin and Ash had taught him the cistern indicator on the first morning of the second week.

"Ash," he said.

He said it from the door. He had not, in the saying, knocked.

"Tervin."

"It's, um."

He stopped. He took the cap off. He held it in both hands.

"Building Six. The pump. Mira sent me."

Ash set the file down.

He set it down without looking at where he set it, which was not the file's slot, and Haran, at the back bench, registered the misplacement with the small turn of the head that was Haran's only comment. Ash did not pick it up. He took his coat from the peg. The coat was the workshop coat, which was the coat for the workshop, and he did not, in the year, often wear it across the square, but the morning was cold and the hand on the door was the hand of a man who had been deciding for two weeks what to wear when he crossed the square to a building he had built.

"Mira," Ash said.

"She said the seal."

"Which seal."

"The one on the pump. The, the one." He turned the cap. "She said you'd know."

"I know."

"She said she's sorry to send."

"Mm."

"She said she tried."

Ash registered the *she said she tried.* It was a sentence Tervin had been carrying across the square in his mouth, in the order Mira had handed it to him, and he had come into the workshop with the sentence intact because the sentence was the part of the errand that was not about the pump.

"Tell her I'm coming."

"She said to come."

"I'm coming."

Tervin nodded. He put the cap on. The cap was still backwards. He turned and went out at the same half-run he had come in by, and Ash watched him cross the square and registered, without saying it, that the boy was running because the running was easier than walking the distance with the message.

"Mm," Haran said.

He said it without looking up.

"Building Six," Ash said.

"I heard."

"The seal."

"Mm."

Haran filed.

Ash stood at the door. He had his coat on. He had the workshop bag in his hand. The bag had a pair of pliers and a small wedge and a length of brass wire he did not need, and the bag was in his hand because a man who walked across his square to a pump did not walk empty-handed even when the thing he was going to use was not in the bag.

"Take the boy," Haran said.

"He's gone."

"Take Reska."

Ash did not answer.

He stood at the door for the count of three. The count of three was the count he had begun running, in the year, when the answer to a thing was not the answer he was about to give. He registered the count. He stepped out into the square. He did not, in the stepping, go and find Reska.

The door closed behind him.

* * *

Building Six was warm.

He came in by the southern door because the southern door was the door the morning crew used and because the southern door was the door that opened on the cellar stair, and the pump was in the cellar. Mira was on the stair when he came down. She had a lamp. The lamp was the lamp Jora kept on the pin at the head of the stair for the morning, and Mira had taken it, which meant she had been down twice already.

Mira was forty. She had run a kitchen in the second terrace before the kitchen closed, and her hands were a kitchen's hands, broad at the knuckle, with the small old burns along the inside of the right wrist a person had who had taken pots off stoves for twenty years. She was on the morning crew because the morning crew paid in coal and the coal was the difference, in her flat upstairs, between the winter and the winter. She had been on the crew six weeks.

"Ash."

"Mira."

"I'm sorry."

She said it the way she had said *good morning* to him on the first morning of the first week, which was the way of a person whose courtesy was not a performance but a piece of furniture she had carried into the room with her.

"Don't."

"I tried the wedge."

"Show me."

She turned. She went down the rest of the stair. He followed. The cellar was the cellar he had built in the third week of the year, with the manifold against the western wall and the pump on the brick plinth he had channeled level, and the pump was the pump, and the seal was the seal he had fused on a morning in the third week with a half-percent expenditure he had not, at the time, registered.

The pump was off.

It was off in the way a pump was off when a person had stopped it because the stopping was the right thing to do, which meant Mira had stopped it before it had failed further, which meant Mira had read the manifold pressure and had registered the drop and had closed the intake valve in the order Jora had taught the third-week crew. The wedge was on the brick by the plinth. The wedge was the wedge from the bag the crew kept on the cellar shelf.

"I read fourteen on the gauge," Mira said. "It should read twenty-two."

"It should."

"I closed the intake. I went up and got the wedge. I tried to set it at the seam, the way you showed Reska." She paused. "I couldn't get the seam."

"It's the inner seam."

"I couldn't see it."

"You can't see it. It's under the cap collar."

"Oh."

She looked at the pump. She looked at the wedge in her hand. She set the wedge on the brick.

"I didn't know it was under," she said.

"I didn't show you."

"You showed Reska."

"I showed Reska on a different pump. This one's the second build. The collar's different."

"Oh."

She said *oh* the second time the way she had said it the first time, which was the way of a person who had registered, twice, that the failure was not a failure of her hands.

He knelt by the plinth.

He laid the bag on the brick. He did not open it. He put his hand on the cap collar. The collar was cool. The seal was below the collar by the width of two fingers, and the seal was, he registered with the part of him that had, in the year, learned to read a fused joint by the small temperature of the metal at its lip, partially failed at the inner edge. He had fused it on a morning in the third week with a quarter-percent expenditure. It had held for nine months. It had failed at the inner edge because the inner edge had taken the brunt of the cold-cycle on the cellar's coldest morning, which had been three days ago.

He could open the collar. He could open it with his hands. The collar was four bolts, and the bolts were sized for a wrench he had in the bag, and he could open the collar and reset the seal by hand, with rag and pitch the way Jora wrapped the riser, and the reset would take him an hour and would hold for perhaps three months.

He could fuse it.

The fuse would take twenty minutes. It would hold for another nine months. The expenditure was a quarter-percent.

He registered the choice.

The choice sat, on the brick, between his hand on the collar and the wedge Mira had set down. It sat there for the count of two. He registered the count. He registered Mira, two paces back, with the lamp lifted, watching, with the courtesy of a woman who had been sent for the Wellspring because the man across the square was the Wellspring.

He fused it.

He laid his palm flat on the collar and he reached, with the small clean reach he had reached with on a hundred mornings in the year, into the pool, and he drew the quarter-percent and he laid it through his palm into the metal, and the metal warmed under his hand the way the metal warmed when the metal was being fused, and he held the warming for the count of twenty, and the seal under the collar, beneath the collar by the width of two fingers, where the inner edge had begun to part, took the heat and re-knit and was the seal again.

He took his hand off.

The collar was warm. He laid the wedge on the bag. He opened the intake valve. He stepped back.

"Try her," he said.

Mira set the lamp on the shelf. She came to the crank. She set her hand on the crank. She ran the count under her breath, *one, two, three, prime,* and the count was the count Jora had taught her, and the pump took on the third stroke, and the manifold ticked once and held, and the gauge moved, in the small slow movement a gauge made when the pressure was returning, from fourteen to seventeen to twenty to twenty-two.

It held at twenty-two.

She stopped the count.

"There," Ash said.

"There."

"Run her for ten and then back to the rotation."

"I will."

"Mira."

"Yes."

"Send Tervin if anything else."

"I will."

She looked at him. She had the lamp back in her hand. The lamp was at her hip. The light from the lamp was on the brick at his foot.

"I'm sorry to send," she said.

"Don't."

"I tried."

"You read the gauge. You closed the intake. You sent. That's the right order."

"I couldn't fix it."

"You couldn't see the seam."

"No."

"That's mine."

She looked at him a beat. She did not, in the looking, take the *that's mine* and lay it down. She held it.

"Mm," she said.

She turned to the crank. She ran the count.

He went up the stair.

* * *

The square was the square at the third hour.

Vell was off the western pump. Reska was on. Jora was at Building Four with the small crew that did the eastern row hinges in the third week of the cold. The basin tap ran. The slate by the bakery had the morning's pressure reading and the count of buckets and the small mark for the cistern indicator, and Reska had chalked, in the corner of the slate, *Bldg 6 - seal,* with a dash, and a blank where the time and result would go.

Ash stopped at the slate.

He took the chalk from the rack. He wrote *3rd hr - reset.* He set the chalk back.

He registered, in the writing, that he had not written *fused.* He had written *reset.* The word was the word Mira had used at the top of the stair. The word was the word the slate would, in the evening, be read by Liss, who came on the third evening of every week now to see the slates. Liss would read *reset* the way Liss read everything, which was the way of a person who registered the gap between the word on the slate and the work it covered.

He stood at the slate for a beat.

He went on across the square.

He went the long way home. The long way was the way past the cellar door of Block Three with the lamp at the inside of the doorframe, and the lamp was the lamp Reska had put there, and he passed it without looking at it. He went past the eastern row. The hinges were on the doors. The wrap on the riser in the western face of the row was the wrap Jora had laid in the third week. He went past Building Four. The southern door was open. Inside, on the back hall, a child was running and the running stopped at the riser and started again on the stair.

He registered.

He registered that *she sent for him.* Not for Reska. Not for Jora. Not for Haran. For the Wellspring. The Wellspring across the square who had, in twenty minutes, with a quarter-percent and a flat palm, fixed the thing the pump's operator could not see the seam of.

He had not, in the building of Building Six in the third week of the year, asked himself who would open the collar when the seal failed.

He registered this on the long way home, between the eastern row and the workshop door, and the registering was the registering of a man who had laid a hand on a wall and felt the wall warm and had known, in the warming, that the warming was not the warming he had built it for.

He came to the workshop. He did not go in.

He stood at the door for the count of five. He looked at the basin. The basin tap ran. The basin held two-fifty. The basin filled a household in two and a half minutes. The basin had been built in the second week of the year with a percent and a half of pool, and it would, on the math he had been running in the year, hold for forty winters.

It would hold for forty winters.

It would not, in any winter, fix itself.

* * *

The chimney flue in Block Three drew poorly on a Sixth-day.

He was on the bench when Reska came. Reska did not run. Reska had been on the morning crew six months and Reska did not run for anything, which was one of the reasons Reska was a crew lead. She came across the square at the walk a person walked who had decided, before the walk, what she was going to say.

"Block Three flue."

"How bad."

"Smoking back. Mrs. Aleth opened the window."

"Mm."

"I went up. The damper's set. The throat's clear. It's something further up I can't get to."

"It's the flue insert."

"I figured."

"I'll come."

He came.

He fixed the flue insert in eighteen minutes. The insert was a piece he had channeled into the brickwork in the fourth week of the year, a small flange that corrected the draft on the upper bend, and the flange had, in the cold-cycle, shifted by a quarter inch. The shift was a quarter inch a man with a chisel and a shim could have corrected in an hour if the man had known about the flange. Reska did not know about the flange. Reska had not been there in the fourth week. The crew that had been there in the fourth week had been Aren and the second boy and Tessa, who had moved to the harbor in the eighth week and had not come back.

He reset the flange with a flat palm and a tenth of a percent.

Reska watched.

She did not, in the watching, perform. She watched the way she had watched him reset the cistern indicator in the third week, which was the way of a person registering a procedure she would not be able to repeat.

"Ash."

"Yes."

"How would I do that."

"You wouldn't."

"Without."

"Without."

He paused.

"Chisel and a shim. The flange is at the upper bend. You'd have to take the brick apart at the bend, set the shim, replace the brick. Half a day."

"Mm."

"I didn't show you the flange."

"No."

"I should have."

"Mm."

She did not say *yes.* She did not say *no.* She said *mm* the way Haran said *mm,* which was not, Ash registered, a thing Reska had said before this morning, and he registered the borrowing the way he had registered Davan's *mm* on the cart side, which was the registering of a tic that traveled, in this district, from the back bench to the morning crew along a path that was the path of attention.

The eastern row insulation panel cracked on a Second-day.

The cold snap was the second cold snap of the season. Jora found the crack at the third hour and sent a child to the workshop with a slate. The slate said *eastern row pnl 3 cracked corner.* Ash read the slate. He took his coat. He went.

The panel was the third panel from the corner. The crack ran from the upper bolt to the seam, six inches, a hairline at the top and a finger's breadth at the bottom. He had set the panel in the fifth week with a half-percent. The cold had moved the bolt. The bolt had pulled the panel. The crack had opened.

Jora was on the ladder.

"Tell me," she said.

"It's the bolt."

"I see the bolt."

"It pulled in the cold."

"I see it pulling."

"The panel's bonded to the bolt. You can't take the bolt without taking the panel."

"Mm."

He went up the ladder.

He laid his palm on the panel. He drew a tenth of a percent. He re-knit the seam, set the bolt with a small inward draw, and brought the panel flat against the wall.

He came down the ladder.

Jora stood at the foot.

"How would I do it," she said.

She said it without looking at him. She was looking at the panel.

"You wouldn't bond the panel to the bolt."

"I didn't bond it. You bonded it."

"Yes."

"So how would I do it."

"You'd take the panel down. New bolt. Set the panel against the new bolt with a strap and a wedge. Pitch at the seam." He paused. "Two hours."

"And it would hold."

"It would hold."

"Less well than yours."

He did not answer.

She looked at him then.

"Less well," she said.

"Yes."

"Mm."

She turned her head and spat, once, sideways, the way she did at the end of a long shift on the morning crew when she was, with the spitting, registering the end of a shift. She did not, in this case, mean the end of a shift.

"I'll write it on the slate," she said.

"Write what."

"Repaired. Fifth-week panel. Bonded bolt."

"Write *Wellspring repair.*"

She looked at him a beat.

"That's the truth."

"Write it."

"Mm."

He went back across the square.

The cistern intake valve stuck on a Fourth-day.

He was at the bench when the second message came. He went. The valve was the valve he had channeled loose in the seventh week of the year, an iron seat with a bronze face he had welded into the pipe with three percent because the pipe had been too narrow at the elbow and the elbow had needed widening, and the welding had been the kind of welding nobody on the morning crew would ever undo because the welding had become, in the welding, the joint.

He channeled it loose.

It took a quarter-percent. It took six minutes.

Nobody asked him, this time, how they would do it without him. The crew on the cistern was Tervin and a woman named Hasta who had come on in the eighth week, and they stood at the foot of the cistern and watched, and Hasta held a wrench in one hand, and the wrench was the wrong size for any bolt on the valve, and Ash registered the wrench the way he had registered Mira's wedge on the brick by the plinth.

He came home at the fifth hour.

Reska first. Jora second. Tesha came with Mira, who was on the morning crew but had not, in the year, been at a leads meeting. Ash had asked her to come. "Mm."

"Thank you."

"Mm."

"My sister," he said.

He held it the way Ash held his.

"No."

"Mm."

"Mm."

"Mm."
