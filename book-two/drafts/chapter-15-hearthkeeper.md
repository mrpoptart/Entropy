# Chapter 15

He went down into the lower district on a Thursday with no tools in his bag.

That was the new thing, and he was still getting used to the weight of it, or the lack of weight, the bag riding light on his shoulder the way an empty bag rode, the strap not biting. He had walked this lane with tools in the bag for the better part of a year. He had walked it with the satchel of clamps and the spool of resonance wire and the small slate he used for sketching the run of a lattice when he was trying to find where it bled. He had walked it with the thing that he was bringing them, and the thing he was bringing them had been the reason he was walking.

Today the bag had a notebook in it and a pencil and an apple, and he was walking it anyway, and the walking was the work.

The lattice ran cold under the boot at the corner of Cattler's bakery. It had run cold there in the autumn, and it had run cold there in the winter, and it ran cold there now in the spring, because the seam under the corner stone was a thumbnail wider than the seam ought to be and nobody had set it, and Ash had known how to set it for two months, and on a Tuesday in the third week of the month he had come down with a clamp and a length of wire and a quarter-hour, and he had set it. The lattice had run warm under the boot at the corner of Cattler's for nine days. On the tenth day it had gone cold again. On the eleventh he had come down to look at it and had stood at the corner with his palm flat to the stone and felt the seam working itself wider in the same direction it had worked itself wider before, and he had understood, slowly, with the quiet humiliation of a man who has been told a thing and then discovers that being told a thing is not the same as believing it, that he had not fixed the seam. He had warmed it. The warming had felt like fixing because he had walked away and the stone had been warm and the warm stone had said *fixed*. The stone had been lying. The stone had not known it was lying. The stone had simply been doing what stone did when you put energy into it, which was hold the energy until it didn't.

He walked past the corner without putting his palm to it.

Ryn had said *you don't fix things. You decorate the breaking with magic.* She had said it on the wall, with the boats below, with her hand on the rail and her shoulder not quite touching his shoulder. She had said it the way she said the thing that had been waiting to be said for fourteen years, which was without raising her voice. She had said it kindly. The kindness had not made it less of a thing. The kindness had made it more of a thing, because the kindness meant she had been holding it for a long time and had decided, finally, that he could be told.

He had been being told for three weeks.

The being-told did not stop when she stopped saying it. The being-told kept happening. It happened when he came down the lane with his bag light on his shoulder. It happened when he stood at the corner of Cattler's and did not put his palm to the stone. It happened in the workshop in the evenings when he sat with the notebook open and tried to write down what a fix was, what a fix actually was, what the difference was between a fix and a warming, and found that the writing came hard, because the writing had to begin with a sentence that said *I have been doing it wrong*, and the sentence was a hard sentence to write, even alone, even with the lamp low and the door shut.

He had written it. He had written it three times. The third time he had not crossed it out.

He came to the gate of the small lane behind the laundry where the children played in the afternoons, and he did not go in. He had been going in. He had been going in for a month, on Thursdays, to set the warmth in the wall that the children put their backs against when they sat to eat. The wall faced north and the sun did not come to it until late and the stone was always the cold stone, and Ash had been warming it on Thursdays the way a man brought bread, and the children had come to expect him, and one of the smaller ones had begun to call him *the warm man*, which had pleased him in a way he had not examined.

He stood at the gate today and he looked at the wall and he did not go in.

If he warmed the wall, the wall would be warm until tomorrow. If he warmed the wall every Thursday for the rest of his life, the wall would be warm on Thursdays for the rest of his life, and the children would put their backs against it on Thursdays, and on the other six days they would put their backs against the cold stone, and on the day he stopped coming the wall would go cold on Thursdays too, and the children would sit somewhere else, and the wall would be the wall it had been before he came.

The reservoir, when he had checked it that morning, had stood at a fraction over a third. He did not have a good word for the fraction. He had been trying out words. *Thirty-seven.* That was the number he had settled on, the way a man settled on a name for a thing he did not quite know. Thirty-seven of what had once been a hundred, and a hundred of what had once been called infinite, and infinite of what was, in fact, a quantity. *Quantity* was the word that had taken him the longest to keep in his head. He had known it for a year. He had not believed it for nine months. He had believed it on the floor of the workshop at the end of the winter when the number had moved a finger's width down the gauge in a single afternoon and he had sat with his back against the leg of the bench and counted his breath until the breath had counted itself.

Thirty-seven.

He stood at the gate of the lane and he did the math the way he had been doing the math for three weeks now, which was without flinching. One district imperfect. One district, which was this district, the lower terrace and a corner of the river quarter and a stretch up the curve as far as the third lift, was perhaps a third done, and the third that was done was the third that he had been told, kindly and with the low voice of a friend who had decided he could be told, was not done. The city had dozens. He had walked, on a different Thursday, up to the upper administrative ring with the notebook in his bag, and he had counted, and the counting had been thirty-one districts on the plateau by the broad reckoning, and another nine on the lower step, and the rural country beyond the gorge, which he had not counted because he had not been there, but which the woman at the records office had said was in the worse condition, the much worse condition, and had said it with the flat face of someone who had been saying it for a long time to people who had not heard her.

Forty districts and the country beyond.

He had come down with forty-four percent and a year of work and had not finished one.

He turned away from the gate and went up the lane.

* * *

Haran was at the bench with the small forge cold beside him and the long forge cold beside the small one, and a length of brass wire across his palm, and he was drawing the wire through his fingers the way a man drew a line of thread through his fingers when he was looking for a snag. He did not look up when Ash came through. He had not looked up when Ash came through for two years.

"Bag's light," Haran said, to the wire.

"Notebook."

"Mm."

Ash hung the bag on the peg. He went to the stove and put the kettle on and stood with his hand on the kettle's handle for a moment after he had set it down, the iron warm under his palm where it had been warm under Haran's palm an hour earlier, the warmth holding, the way a thing held when Haran had set it.

He had been looking at this for a week. He had been looking at it without looking at it for two years.

He turned around. Haran was working the wire. The wire was a piece of brass perhaps the length of a forearm, and Haran was straightening a kink in it, not by pulling it, not by setting it on the bench and tapping it, but by holding it in his fingers and running his thumb along it in a slow, steady draw, and as the thumb moved the kink came out, and Ash, who had stood at this bench for the better part of two years and who had watched Haran do this kind of thing twenty times a day, had until this week understood the drawing-out-of-the-kink as the action of the thumb. It was not the action of the thumb. The thumb was the cover. The drawing-out was happening in the wire, in the brass, in the small heat that softened the metal exactly enough at exactly the place the kink sat, a heat so small that the air above the wire did not shimmer, a heat so small that the bench under the wire did not warm, a heat so contained that Ash, six feet away, could not feel it on his face.

He had been at this bench for two years. He had not seen it.

Haran finished the wire. He set it on the bench. The wire lay straight.

"How do you do that?"

Haran looked up.

The looking up was the rare thing. Haran looked up the way a man at a long task looked up, with the slow unhitching of the eye from the work, and he held Ash a moment with the steady, dry, considered attention that was the only attention Haran had, which was the attention of a man who had decided, a long time ago, that there was no other kind worth giving.

"Do what."

"The wire. The kink."

"Drew it out."

"You didn't pull it."

"No."

"You. You channeled it."

"Mm."

"Haran."

Haran picked up the wire again. He turned it in his fingers. He set it down. He looked at Ash and he looked at the cold forge and he looked at his hands in his lap, and he was not stalling, exactly, but he was the sort of man for whom an answer cost something even when the answer was small, and the cost was being paid in the small turning of the wire and the small look at the forge, and Ash, who had learned to wait at this bench, waited.

"Thirty years of practice," Haran said. "Give or take."

"Show me."

Haran looked at him. The look held.

"Please," Ash said. "Show me."

Haran put his palms flat on the bench. He was a long time doing it. Then he stood up, slow, and he went to the small forge, and he laid his hand on the rim of the small forge the way a man laid his hand on the shoulder of an old dog, and he did not look at Ash when he spoke.

"Light it."

"Me?"

"You."

Ash came to the small forge. He put his palm to the rim where Haran's palm had been a moment before, and Haran's warmth was still in the iron a little, and Ash channeled into the bed of the forge the way he had channeled into a forge a hundred times, the small focused push that brought the kindling up out of cold, and the kindling caught, and the small flame ran along the edge of the wood and curled, and the bed of the forge filled with the slow orange of a fire taking, and the air above the forge began to shimmer, and the rim under his hand grew warm, and the bench beside the forge grew warm, and the tongs hanging on the hook above the forge grew warm, and the whole small region of the workshop around the forge grew warm with the slow halo of his channeling, and the kindling caught, and the kindling burned, and the forge was lit.

"Now me," Haran said.

He doused the forge with the iron lid. He waited until the bed was cold. He laid his palm on the rim again.

The kindling caught.

Ash watched.

He watched for the shimmer in the air. There was no shimmer in the air.

He watched the rim under Haran's palm. The rim did not warm.

He watched the bench beside the forge. The bench did not warm.

He watched the tongs on the hook. The tongs did not move, and the air around the tongs did not move, and the small region of the workshop around the forge was the small region of the workshop around the forge that it had been a moment before Haran put his palm to the rim, and the kindling caught, and the kindling burned, and the forge was lit.

Ash put his hand out, palm down, six inches above the bed of the forge. The heat came up from the bed at exactly the temperature the heat ought to come up from a bed of burning kindling, no more, and Ash drew his palm back and looked at his palm and looked at Haran's palm on the rim, and Haran took his palm off the rim, and the bed burned.

"Haran."

"Mm."

"What did you. You. What did you just do."

Haran sat down on the stool by the bench. He looked at the forge. He looked at Ash.

"Most channelers," he said, "spend roughly six parts in ten of what they spend on heating the room they aren't trying to heat. You have. You have heard this."

"I have heard it."

"Heard isn't seen."

"No."

Haran picked up the wire again. He turned it in his fingers. He set it down. He did not look at Ash when he went on.

"It isn't a technique. Techniques are for things you do once. This is a discipline. You sit with it every day. You sit with it when you light the lamp and when you set the kettle and when you draw a kink out of a piece of brass that nobody is watching you draw. You sit with it when nothing is asked of you. You sit with it especially then. You watch where the energy goes that you didn't mean to send. You catch it. You catch it slower than it goes. So at first you don't catch any of it. Then you catch a thumb's worth. Then a finger's. Then the back of a hand. Then. After a long time. The room stops being warm when you weren't trying to warm the room."

He looked up.

"The traditions were built when there was a great deal to spend. A man could light his forge and warm his neighbor's house at the same time, and the warming of the neighbor's house was a courtesy, and the courtesy was the texture of the day. We did not learn to be careful. We did not need to. The careful is a thing you find when there isn't enough."

"How careful."

"Mm?"

"How careful are you. What. What's the. The fraction. What you spend versus what. What lands."

Haran was quiet.

"I don't know that fraction," he said. "I have never measured. I would say. I would say there is very little I send that I do not mean to send."

"You said sixty percent waste."

"For most. The traditions, as taught."

"And you."

"I would say I waste a little. Not nothing. A little."

He looked at his hands.

"I am not stronger than I was at twenty," he said. "I am much weaker. I have a third the pool I had at twenty, which is what an old man has. But I do more with my third than I did with my whole, because at twenty I was. I was a hose. I'm a needle."

He said it without pride. He said it the way a man named a tool.

"You should know," he said, "what your choices are."

Ash sat down on the floor by the bench.

He sat down because his legs had decided to sit down, and he watched them decide, and he let them, and he sat with his back against the leg of the bench and his hands in his lap, and he looked at the forge that was burning at the temperature of a small forge with a bed of kindling and not a degree more, and he did the arithmetic in his head, and the arithmetic was not arithmetic so much as a door opening.

* * *

"Let me try."

"Mm."

Haran doused the forge. He waited. He stood up off the stool and he made room at the bench and he did not say anything else, and Ash came to the rim of the small forge, and Ash put his palm to the iron, and Ash thought about a needle.

He thought about the place inside himself where the channel started, the small place at the base of the breath, and he thought about narrowing it. Narrowing was the word he had. He did not have a better word. He thought about narrowing the channel the way a man narrowed the bore of a pipe, and he sent.

Nothing happened.

The kindling did not catch. The bed of the forge stayed cold. He could feel that he had sent something, the small signature of it in his own body, the slight tug at the back of the breath that was the going-out of energy, but the going-out had gone. It had not gone into the bed. It had gone into the narrow itself and stopped there, the way water stopped in a kinked hose, the way the thing he had narrowed to a needle had no eye for the thread to pass through.

"Mm," Haran said.

"Too narrow."

"Yes."

"All right."

He widened. He did not know how to widen by a measured amount; he knew only how to widen, and he widened, and he sent, and a flare came up out of the bed of the forge that was not the flare of kindling catching but the flare of a great quantity of energy arriving at a small quantity of wood, and the bed jumped, and a tongue of flame leapt as high as Ash's forearm, and the iron under his palm went hot in a way the iron should not have gone hot, and the bench beside the forge went hot, and a black scorch the shape of a thumb appeared on the wood of the bench at the base of the forge where the bench met the iron, and Ash pulled his hand off the rim and stepped back, and the flame settled, and the bed of the forge burned, and a smell of charred wood came up from the scorch on the bench, the small bitter smell of work that had gone wrong.

Haran looked at the scorch.

"Mm," Haran said.

"I'm sorry."

"Bench has had worse."

Ash put his hand to the scorch. The wood was hot under his palm. The bench beside the forge was warm everywhere, the slow general warm of a region heated by a careless channeler, the warmth he had learned to ignore over two years because it was the warmth of every channeler who lit a forge, and he understood, with the slow understanding that came when a thing already known became a thing seen, that the warmth was not the forge. The warmth was the part of the forge he had not put in the forge. The warmth was the waste.

"Again," he said.

"Mm."

He doused the forge. He waited until the bed was cold. He laid his palm on the rim.

He thought about the difference between the needle and the hose. He thought about a thing in between, a thing that was not the needle and not the hose, and he tried to find the thing in between, and he sent.

The kindling caught.

The bed filled with the slow orange. The flame ran along the edge of the wood. The air above the forge shimmered, but less than it had shimmered the first time. The rim under his palm grew warm, but less. The bench beside the forge grew warm, but less. The tongs on the hook did not move, and the air around the tongs did not move, and Ash watched the small halo of his channeling around the forge and the halo was smaller than it had been the first time, perhaps three-quarters of the size of the halo it had been the first time, perhaps a little smaller than three-quarters, and he had lit the forge, and the lighting had cost him perhaps three-quarters of what the first lighting had cost him, and he stood at the bench with his palm on the rim and his face hot and his breath shallow and he wanted to laugh, and he did not laugh, because the difference was small and the difference was real and the smallness of the difference was the thing.

"Better," Haran said.

"Some."

"Mm."

"How much. How much better."

Haran looked at the forge. He looked at the halo, which was already settling.

"A little."

"How much."

"Ash."

"How much."

Haran was quiet.

"You went from running hot to running. Slightly less hot. You are still running hot. You are running. Hot in the way that any man who has lit a forge in this city in the last hundred years has run, hot. You shaved, I would say, perhaps a finger off the waste. Perhaps less. It is hard to say from where I am standing."

"A finger."

"Mm."

Ash put his palm to the rim again. He was going to do it again. Haran watched him do it again. The kindling caught. The halo came up. The halo was perhaps the same as the second time. The halo was perhaps a little larger than the second time, because Ash had been thinking about it harder and the thinking-about-it-harder had widened the channel, the way clenching a fist made it harder to do fine work, and he saw it, he saw the widening as it happened, and he had no way to undo the widening because the widening had already gone, and the energy had already gone with it, and the bench was warm and the rim was warm and the tongs were warm and the kindling was burning and the forge was lit.

"Thirty years," Haran said.

He said it dry. He said it the way a man read a price tag. He was not unkind. He was not kind. He was the man at the bench, and he had told Ash what the price was, and the telling was the telling.

* * *

Ash sat on the floor by the bench again with his back against the leg of the bench and his knees up and the smell of the scorched wood faint in the workshop.

He did the arithmetic in his head, and this time the arithmetic was arithmetic, and he did it slow.

Most channelers wasted six parts in ten. He was most channelers. He had always been most channelers, in this, the small way that he had not been most channelers in any other thing, because the discipline was a thing that came with thirty years of being unable to afford to waste, and Ash had never been unable to afford to waste, and the not-being-unable had been written into his channeling the way an accent was written into a voice. He wasted six parts. He probably wasted more than six parts. He had built no discipline. He had built only force.

If he could halve his waste. If he could cut sixty percent waste to thirty percent waste. The math did the math. A thing that cost him a hundred today would cost him seventy tomorrow. A thing that cost him a thousand today would cost him seven hundred. The reservoir, which had stood at a third and a hair, would stand at a third and a hair and would stand there longer, because the rate at which the third and a hair came down would be smaller, and the smaller rate would multiply through the months and the years, and the year that had cost him fourteen percent of the reservoir would have cost him perhaps. Perhaps ten. Perhaps nine, if the discipline came in faster than the work grew.

He sat with his back against the leg of the bench and he let the arithmetic open the door.

If he could halve his waste he could last twice as long.

If he could approach Haran's discipline, ten years in, twenty years in, the pool that stood at a third and a hair could last him. He did not know how long. He did not have a clean number. He knew only that the number was longer than the number he had been carrying. Years longer. The reservoir which on the morning's gauge had been nine months from empty at his current rate of work might, with the discipline, with the patient narrowing, with the long sit at the small forge that was the only school there was, stand for. Years. He did not say a number. The number was a door. He stood at the door.

The door said: stay.

The door said: sit at the bench. Sit at the bench every morning. Light the small forge at the small cost. Bend the small wire with the small heat. Sit with the discipline the way Haran had sat with the discipline, hour after hour, year after year, until you were not the hose, until you were the needle, until you had done what no one in the city had asked you to do, which was to stop wasting what no one had asked you to spend. Stay. Stay where you are. Stay extraordinary. Stay the man who has been told the secret of the bench. Stay the man who does not have to be the man with the bag and the notebook and the wall and the children and the corner of the seam at Cattler's that does not stay set. Stay the man who is. The man who is.

The door said: never that person again.

He sat with his back against the leg of the bench and he watched the door.

He had been *that person* for a year. He had been the man with the bag. He had walked down the lane with the clamp and the wire and the small slate and the willingness to be wrong, and the willingness to be wrong had been the wrongness, and Ryn had told him so, and the seam at Cattler's had told him so, and the wall behind the laundry had told him so, and on the wall above the boats she had told him so without raising her voice, and he had sat with the telling for three weeks, and the sitting had been the work. The sitting had been hard. The sitting had been the hardest thing he had done in the year, harder than the earthquake, harder than the dam, harder than the small slow telling of his mother in the autumn, because the sitting had been the slow understanding that he had been doing the work the wrong way, and the wrong way had been the way he knew, and the right way was a way he did not know and might not, in the time he had, learn.

The door said: you do not have to learn it.

The door said: you have a different way. The different way is here, at the bench. The different way is the long sit. The different way is the discipline. The different way means the reservoir lasts. The different way means you are not, at the end of the third year, sitting on the floor of a workshop in a city where the lattice has gone cold under the boot in every district, having spent the last of it on more seams that do not stay set. The different way means there is something left. The different way means the *something left* is the work. Not the building. The keeping.

He sat.

The forge had gone out while he was sitting. The bed had burned through the kindling and Haran had not added a stick, and the room had cooled the small cool of a workshop in the late afternoon when the work was done and the lamps were not yet lit. Haran had moved off, somewhere, the back of the workshop, the small room with the cot, the sink. Ash was alone at the bench.

He looked at the small forge.

The small forge was the small forge. The bench beside the small forge was the bench. The scorch on the wood of the bench was the size of his thumb. The tongs hung on the hook. The wire that Haran had drawn the kink out of lay straight on the bench. The lamp on the wall above the bench was not lit. The light from the high window was the late light, the light of the day going.

He could feel the door he was standing at.

He had stood at doors before. He had stood at the door of the records office and at the door of the council chambers and at the door of his sister's office in the upper ring, and the doors had opened or had not opened, and he had gone through or had not gone through, and the going-through had been a thing that happened in the body, the foot crossing the sill, the hand on the latch, the small commitment of the weight. This door was not in the body. This door was in him. The latch was in him. The sill was in him.

He thought about the wall behind the laundry. The children would put their backs against the cold stone tomorrow. They would put their backs against the cold stone the day after. They would put their backs against the cold stone every Thursday from now until the wall came down or the children grew up or the city found a way to set the seam in the lattice that ran cold under the laundry. Ash, sitting at the bench, with the discipline, with the long sit, with the reservoir held in reserve against an emergency that would never come because he had not gone out into it, would not be the one who set it.

He thought about Davan, who had walked off the dam project in the winter and had not come back, and to whom he had not written, because he had not known what to write, and in the long sit he would still not know what to write, and the long sit was a place from which letters did not get written.

He thought about Tesha in the records office, who had asked him a question in the autumn that he had not answered, and the question had been *what do you want this for*, and the question was on a card on the shelf in the workshop, in his handwriting, where he had copied it down to keep with him, and the answer to the question would, in the long sit, become *I wanted to keep it*, and the keeping would be the answer, and the keeping would, he knew with the slow knowing that came when a thing already known became a thing seen, be a different word for *I did not spend it.*

He thought about his mother, who had said in the autumn that she did not need the kettle warm, that the cold kettle was a fine kettle, that he should not, should not, and he had not understood her, and on the floor of the workshop with his back against the leg of the bench and the late light through the high window he understood her a little more, and the understanding was the understanding that she had been telling him, in her way, what Haran had told him in the afternoon in his way, which was *you should know what your choices are*, and one of the choices was to keep.

The door said: keep.

The door said: the keeping is good. The keeping is wise. The keeping is what a man with thirty years of practice does at the bench when he has understood that the great thing he was given was not, in fact, infinite, and that the careful thing is to make it last, and the lasting is the work, and the work is enough. The keeping is the answer to Tesha's question. The keeping is the letter to Davan you do not have to write because there is no project to walk off. The keeping is the kettle your mother said she did not need warm. The keeping is the wall you do not warm on Thursdays because the children will sit somewhere else and the somewhere else is not your problem and the not-your-problem is the keeping. The keeping is the small forge and the small wire and the long sit. The keeping is the discipline. The keeping is the precise, careful, beautiful thing that thirty years of a man's life had bought him, and you have been offered, here, at the bench, in the late light, the chance to start the thirty years.

The door said: keep.

He sat with his back against the leg of the bench in the cooling workshop and he watched the door.

The door was open.

He could feel the pull of the open door the way a man at the edge of a long drop felt the pull of the drop, the body's small lean toward the thing that would, if he gave it the lean, take him. The pull was a quiet pull. The pull was not a temptation in the way a temptation in a story was a temptation. There was no devil at his shoulder. There was only the bench, and the small forge, and the long sit, and the arithmetic that said *years instead of months*, and the arithmetic was true, and the truth of the arithmetic was the pull.

He thought about the man who had walked down the lane in the morning with the bag light on his shoulder. That man had been a man who was going to do a thing. That man had been a man who had been told he had been doing the thing wrong, and who had been walking the lane to learn what the thing was, and the walking had been the work, and the bag had been light because the tools had not yet been the right tools and he had not yet known what the right tools were.

That man could put the bag down.

That man could come up the curve and hang the bag on the peg and sit at the bench and not, ever, take the bag down off the peg again. The bag would hang on the peg. The peg was a good peg. The bag was a good bag. The bag would be on the peg in a year and in five years and in ten, and Ash would be at the bench, and the reservoir would stand at a third and a hair and would come down slow, the way a candle in a still room came down slow, and he would be the man at the bench, the precise man, the careful man, the man who had been given the great thing and had kept it, and the keeping would be the story.

He sat.

He could not feel his legs. He could feel his back against the leg of the bench, and his hands in his lap, and the small ache at the base of the breath where he had narrowed and widened and narrowed again at the small forge, and the late light on the high window had gone and the workshop was the dark workshop, and somewhere in the back Haran was moving, the small movements of a man putting away a tool, the small clink of a thing on a shelf, and the small clink was the small clink, and the room held.

He looked at the small forge. The small forge was cold.

The reservoir would last years instead of months.

He just had to stop building.
