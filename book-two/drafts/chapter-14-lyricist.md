# Chapter 14

He woke before the lamp.

The room was the cold half-dark that came an hour before the bells, and he lay on his back and listened to it. The reservoir sat in his chest at thirty-seven, the way it had sat for a week, the way a stone sat in a coat pocket a man had stopped meaning to take out. His sister breathed through the wall. His father did not. His father had been up since the third hour, the way his father had been up since the third hour for forty years, and the not-hearing of his father in the kitchen was the morning the morning was.

He looked at the ceiling.

The ceiling had a crack across the plaster he had known since he was nine. He had known it the way a boy knew the face of a thing in his room. It had been a river when he was nine. It had been a road when he was twelve. It had been a crack when he was sixteen. It was a crack now. He looked at the crack and the crack did not become anything else.

He thought about Davan.

He thought about Davan and the not-thinking of Davan, the way a man's mind went around a thing it had decided not to go through, and the going-around was the going-through if a man went around long enough. He had not seen Davan in eight days. He had not been to the meeting house. He had not opened the broadsheet his father had set, three mornings running, on the corner of the kitchen table by his cup. The broadsheets under the brass weight at the workshop had stopped being three and become five and then seven and Haran had stopped saying anything about them and had begun, quietly, to use them, one at a time, to wrap the wire-ends he was sending up the hill to the smith.

He thought about Dorenne.

He thought about Dorenne and the not-thinking of Dorenne, which was a different not-thinking. Dorenne had not come down. Dorenne had sent a clerk, twice, a young man with a folder under his arm and a fixed face, and the young man had stood at the workshop door and read a list of items the Hall would like to discuss, and Ash had said *not this week,* and the young man had nodded and gone, and the second time the young man had said *not this week again,* with the small lift in the voice that was the lift the Hall trained into its clerks, and Ash had said *not this week,* and the young man had nodded and gone.

He thought about Haran.

Haran was a pause and a period. Haran was the bench and the wire and *eat.* Haran had said three sentences in a week that had not been about the work. The first had been *the body is also a tool.* The second had been *grief is a kind of weather.* The third had been *you will know when you know.* Haran's sentences were the kind of sentences a man set on a shelf. They were not the kind of sentences a man took down and used.

His mother could not follow him into this. His father read the moderate sheet at the kitchen table and did not say *Davan,* did not say *Sera,* did not say any of the names, and the not-saying was the kindness his father had to give. Maren had touched his wrist twice in the week and had not said anything, and the touching had been Maren's whole speech, and the speech had been *I am here,* and *I am here* was the speech he could not, this week, take down and use.

He was, he understood, alone in a way he had not been alone in a year.

He had been alone before. He had been alone the year before the Wellspring and the year before that and most of the years he could remember, and the aloneness had been the room he had lived in. The Wellspring had taken him out of the room and put him in a different room, a louder one, with people in it, and the people had been the people, and the people had filled the loudness, and now the loudness was going and the people were going with it and the room he was being put back into was not the room he had left. The old room had a name. He had never given it a name. He had just lived in it.

The new room had no name yet.

He sat up.

He put his feet on the floor. The floor was cold. He pulled on his trousers and his shirt and the wool layer and the coat and he did not light the lamp because the lighting of the lamp would have been a thing his sister woke to, and he did not want her to wake to anything this morning, and he was, he understood, going somewhere, and he had not, until he sat up, known he was going.

He went down the stair.

His father was at the kitchen table. The broadsheet was folded. His father looked at him over the cup and did not say anything. He took a piece of bread from the cloth on the shelf. He put it in his coat pocket.

"I'll be out," he said.

"Mm."

"Tell mother."

"Mm."

He stopped at the door. He looked back. His father had not moved. The cup steamed. The lamp on the table threw the kind of light a kitchen lamp threw when a man had been sitting under it long enough that the light had become the kitchen.

"Da."

"Mm."

"I'm going to Ryn's."

His father looked up. His father looked at him the way his father looked when his father had decided not to say the thing his father wanted to say. He nodded, once, and went back to the broadsheet.

Ash went out.

* * *

The morning was the grey the city did not have a name for.

He took the lower stair down off the curve, not the upper. The upper went past the workshop, and he was not going to the workshop. The lower went past the bakery that opened at the fourth hour and the cloth stall that did not open until the sixth and the corner where, when he was twelve, he had broken his wrist falling off a wall he should not have been on. The wall was still there. The break was still in his wrist when the weather turned, and the weather had been turned for a month, and the wrist was the weather the wrist had been.

He walked.

He walked at the pace a man walked who had not, when he set out, decided he was setting out, and so the walking made the deciding while the walking was happening, and the walking went down the lower stair and across the small square with the leaning post and along the back lane behind the cooper's yard and out onto the long street that ran along the lower terrace toward the bakery quarter where Ryn's family had had the shop and where Ryn, since she had stopped being at the shop, had had her workshop in the side lane behind it.

He had walked this walk a thousand times.

He had walked this walk and not noticed it. He had walked it as a boy on errands and as a teenager going to find her at the end of a shift and as a young man with nothing to do and the whole afternoon to do it in, and the walking had been the walking, and the noticing had not been the noticing, because a thing a man walked a thousand times stopped being a thing and became a length of his own life laid out under his feet.

He noticed it now.

He noticed the leaning post, the grain of it where his hand had gone for years without his hand knowing it had gone there. He noticed the cooper's gate, repainted last summer, the green not quite the green it had been. He noticed the corner where the cobble was loose. He noticed the smell of the bakery quarter before he saw it, the warm yeast smell that came up the lane the way it had always come up the lane, and the smell was the smell, and the smell undid something small in his chest he had not known was tied.

He came around the corner into the lane behind the bakery.

Ryn's workshop was where it had been. The door was open. He stopped at the corner before the door and did not, for a count, go forward.

He could hear her.

She was talking to someone. He could not make out the words. He could make out the cadence, the short lines and the small laugh and the short line again, the rhythm of her at work, and the rhythm went into him the way the smell of the yeast had gone, and he stood in the lane and listened to a cadence he had not heard in nine months and let it be, for a count, the only thing in his head.

Then he walked.

* * *

The workshop was the workshop.

Tools on the wall in the order she put tools on a wall, which was not the order Haran put tools on a wall. Haran's order was alphabetical by use. Ryn's order was by hand. The tool she reached for first was the tool by the door. The tool she reached for second was the tool by the bench. The tool she reached for last was the tool on the high shelf, and the tool on the high shelf was the tool that, when you were reaching for it, you were already in trouble. He had told her once that this was a stupid system. She had said *find me a system that gets me to the wrench faster and I'll switch.* He had not.

She was at the bench with a man.

The man was sitting on a stool with his sleeve rolled up, and Ryn was bent over a brass joint in his lap, a joint that ran from a small portable cistern the man had set on the floor between his boots. She had the channeler's strap on her left wrist. Her right hand was on the joint. She did not look up when Ash came in. She had heard him. He could tell by the small adjustment of her shoulders, which was the adjustment her shoulders had made when he came in for as long as he had been coming in, and the not-looking-up was the looking-up, and the looking-up would come when she had finished what she was doing, and not before.

"Hold this," she said.

She did not say it to him. She said it to the man. The man held the joint.

"Tighter."

The man held it tighter.

She channeled. He saw her pool flicker, the small visible thing it did at the edge of her hand when she was working close, and the joint took heat, and the heat ran into the seal where the seal had cracked, and the seal softened, and she pressed the joint together with her thumb at the angle her thumb went at when she was doing this, and she eased the heat off, and the seal set.

"There," she said.

The man flexed his fingers around the joint.

"Try it."

He tried it. He turned the small valve at the base of the cistern. Water came up the joint and into the small spout at the top and pooled in the cup he had set under it, and the pooling was the pooling, and the joint did not weep.

"Two days minimum before you push it past half-pressure," she said. "If it weeps, bring it back. Don't try to fix it yourself. You'll make it worse and I'll have to charge you twice."

"Yes."

"And tell your wife I said the bread was good."

"I will."

The man stood. He paid her. The paying was three small coins and a folded paper that Ash recognized as a chit from the bakery quarter co-op, and Ryn took the coins and the chit and put them in the tin on the shelf and did not count them and did not write them down and turned, finally, to him.

She looked at him.

She looked at him the way she had always looked at him, which was straight on, with the small sideways angle of her chin she did when she was deciding whether the thing in front of her was the thing she had thought it was. She did not smile. She did not, also, not smile. The man with the cistern picked up his cistern and went past Ash in the door and said *morning* without looking and was gone.

"You look terrible," she said.

It came out of him before he had decided to let it.

It was a laugh. It was a short rough laugh that came up through his chest and shook in his shoulders and stopped, and his face did the thing his face did when a laugh had gone through it, which was to be wet at the corners of the eyes, and he put the back of his hand to his eye and took it away and looked at the back of his hand and did not look at her.

"There he is," she said.

"Ryn."

"Hold this."

She handed him a tool.

It was a small clamp. She handed it to him without looking, the way she had handed him tools when they were sixteen and he had been hanging around her father's shop because there had been nowhere else to be, and the handing-over was a thing her hand did before her mind was finished thinking about whether to do it, and the clamp was in his hand and she had turned back to the bench.

"What are we doing."

"I am finishing a repair. You are holding the clamp. When I tell you to squeeze, you squeeze. Not before."

"All right."

"And not too hard. The casing is old."

"All right."

She bent over the next piece on the bench, which was a small valve assembly with the housing taken off, and she set the clamp into his hand at the angle she wanted and she pointed at the place she wanted it, and he held the clamp at the place she wanted, and she worked.

She worked for a count.

He held.

She worked for another count, and another, and the rhythm of her at the bench was the rhythm of a woman who had been at this bench for a year, who knew the bench the way Haran knew his bench, who had built the bench up out of the small daily acts of working at it until the bench was a place that worked her hands as much as her hands worked the bench. She did not narrate. She did not explain. She did not ask him how he was. She did not say *it's been a while.* She said *squeeze,* and he squeezed, and she said *ease,* and he eased, and she said *squeeze,* and he squeezed, and the assembly went together at the rate it went together, and at the end of it she straightened and pressed the heel of her palm into the small of her back and said:

"Good. Set it down."

He set it down.

"Tea."

"Yes."

She went to the small stove in the corner. The kettle was on. She had channeled the kettle warm, he saw, while she had been working on the joint with the man, the small economy of a woman who did not waste a channel. She poured. She handed him a cup. She sat on the stool the man had been on. She pointed at the other stool. He sat.

"You haven't been by," she said.

"No."

"Nine months."

"Yes."

"Mm."

She drank.

He drank.

The tea was the tea she made, which was the tea her mother had made, which was the tea the bakery quarter made, which was rough and over-steeped and tasted like the inside of a cupboard if a man let it cool. He did not let it cool. He drank it hot and the drinking did the thing the drinking did, which was to put a small warmth into his chest where the not-channeling sat, and the warmth and the not-channeling were two things in his chest, and the two things did not, today, argue.

"Walk with me," she said.

"Now."

"After this. I'm done for the morning. I want to walk."

"All right."

* * *

They walked.

She locked the workshop with the key her mother had given her, which was the same key her mother had used for the bakery side door for thirty years, and she put the key in her coat pocket, and they walked out of the lane and along the lower terrace and up toward the corner where the lane gave on the long street that ran past the building they had both grown up in.

The building was the building.

It had a new sign on the door of the lower flat, which was the flat Ryn's family had had until her father had moved out to the sister's house in the country in the second autumn of the decline, and the new sign was the name of a family Ash did not know. The window above had the green shutter that had always been the green shutter, repainted twice in his life and not yet a third time. The step had the chip out of the corner where the boys had run into it with the cart the year a boy two streets over had broken his collarbone and they had all stopped running with carts for a season.

She did not stop at the building.

She walked past it. He walked past it. He looked at it as he passed and she did not, and the not-looking was the look she had at the building, which was the look of a woman who had walked past it every day of her life and had decided some time ago that the walking-past was the relationship.

"I have been," he said, "doing badly."

"Mm."

"With Davan. With the work. With my reservoir. With, I don't know, with a lot of things."

"You said you'd been doing badly with a lot of things. Pick one."

"Davan."

"All right."

He told her.

He told her about Sera. He told her about Edda coming up the hill with the broadsheets, about the square with the brass cap, about the lane behind the meeting house and the long street under the warming circuit and *you're asking the drowning to be patient while you decide which lifeboat to build,* and the saying of it to her in the lane, on the walk, with her boots beside his on the cobble, was a different saying than the saying of it to himself in the workshop, because in the workshop he had been the man it had been said to and out here he was a man saying a thing to a friend, and the friend was walking, and the walking was the room, and the room was a room he could put things in and not have them stand on him.

He told her about Dorenne.

He told her, more carefully, because Dorenne was not Davan, because Dorenne was the room he had lived in for a year and he had not yet figured out the shape of the door he had used to walk out of it, and the telling of Dorenne was the telling of a half-shape, but Ryn did not push at the half-shape. She walked. She let the half-shape be a half-shape. She kicked a stone twenty paces and stopped kicking it and did not pick a new one.

He told her about Haran.

"Pause and a period," she said.

"Yes."

"Mm. He sounds useful."

"He is."

"Useful isn't the same as company."

"No."

She walked.

She walked the way she walked, which was with her hands in her coat pockets and her shoulders set the way her shoulders set, and at a pace that was a step faster than his because she was a step faster than him at most things and always had been, and he had to lengthen his stride a small amount to keep with her, and the lengthening was familiar in his calves the way the smell of the yeast had been familiar in his chest, and the familiarity was a thing he had not known he was going to feel and now was feeling.

"My family," he said.

"Mm."

"My family can't follow me into this."

"No."

"Maren tries. Mam tries. Da reads the paper."

"Da reads the paper."

"Yes."

"Da reads the paper because Da is Da and Da has been reading the paper since you were six and the paper is the room he sits in. Don't ask Da to come out of the paper. Don't ask Mam to come where she can't go. They love you. They are not your council."

"I know."

"Do you."

"I know."

She walked.

He walked.

They came up out of the bakery quarter and along the back of the lower market, which was closed at this hour, the stalls covered with the rough cloth, and the cloth was wet with the morning, and a cat went under one stall and out the other side and looked at them and went on.

* * *

She stopped at the corner.

She stopped at the corner where the lane turned up toward the small square with the bench under the locust tree, the square where they had sat as kids on the bench when the bench had been too high for their feet to reach the ground and they had swung their legs and argued about whether the moons were going to come back and whether the man who lived on the third floor of the green building was a thief or just rude.

She did not sit on the bench.

She stood at the corner and looked at the bench and did not sit on it, and he stood at the corner and looked at the bench too, and neither of them sat, and the not-sitting was a thing.

"Ash."

"Mm."

"You became someone else. You left. I didn't know if you were coming back."

He did not answer.

He looked at the bench. The bench had the iron slats it had always had. The iron had rust along the back slat where the rain came under the locust. The wood underneath, the cross-piece their feet had not reached, had a name carved into it from before either of them had been born, and the name had been the name when he was six and was the name now, and the name was *Telman,* and he had never known who Telman was.

"Ryn."

"I'm going to say what I came out here to say. You asked me to walk and I came out here to say it, and if I don't say it now I'll go home and I won't say it tomorrow either, and I've been not saying it for nine months and I'm done."

"All right."

"I missed you."

She looked at the bench while she said it. She did not look at him. Her hands were in her coat pockets and her chin was at the angle it went at when she was setting a thing down and had decided it was going to stay set down.

"Not the Wellspring."

"No."

"You."

"Mm. You."

He did not, for a count, have anything in his throat that was going to come out as a word.

He looked at the bench. He looked at the carved name. He looked at the iron slat with the rust along the back. He looked at her boots beside his, scuffed at the toe where her boots had always scuffed because she walked with her toes turned a little out, the way she had walked since she was nine and had started walking in her brother's hand-me-down boots and had never stopped walking like that even after the boots had stopped being her brother's and had started being hers, and the scuff on the boot was the scuff on the boot, and the scuff was a thing he had not seen in nine months and had not known he had been not seeing.

"I'm sorry," he said.

She nodded.

She nodded, once, and the nodding was not acceptance. The nodding was the small motion a woman made when she had heard a sentence and was setting it next to other sentences and was waiting to see what kind of sentence it turned out, in the end, to be.

"I know," she said.

"Ryn."

"I know. You were sorry the last time too."

"Yes."

"And the time before that."

"Yes."

"Mm."

She turned, then. She turned and looked at him, and her face was the face he had known since he was four, and the face was tired, and the tired was a tired he had not put there and had also, he understood, partly put there, and the partly was a thing he had to hold without letting the holding become a thing that took the looking-at-her away from him.

"Being sorry isn't the same as being here, Ash."

"I know."

"Do you."

"I'm here now."

She watched him say it.

She watched him say it the way she had watched the man with the cistern test the joint at her bench, with her chin at the small sideways angle, and she let the saying of it sit in the air between them for a count, and then she said:

"Are you."

"Yes."

"Or are you here because the other places didn't work out."

He felt it in the chest.

He felt it the way a man felt a finger pressed to the place the bruise was, the small precise pressure that did not hurt unless you were already hurt, and he was already hurt, and the pressure was the pressure, and the answer he had put together on the walk down was not, in front of the question, the answer.

"Both," he said. "I think both."

She did not move.

She watched him a count more. Then her shoulders, the small set of them, eased a quarter-inch, and she said:

"All right."

"All right."

"That was the right answer."

"Was it."

"It was the true answer. I'll take it."

She took her hand out of her pocket. She put it on his sleeve, at the wrist, the small flat press she had done since they were ten, the press she put on his wrist when she was telling him without saying so that they were going to keep walking. She pressed. She took the hand back. She put it in the pocket. She walked.

He walked.

* * *

They went up the small square and past the locust and along the upper lane that gave on the long street that ran above the lower terrace and down toward the canal at the second bridge, which was not the way home for either of them, but was the way a person walked who was not yet ready to be home.

"Tell me your work," he said.

"My work."

"Yes."

"You want me to tell you my work."

"Yes."

"You don't want to tell me yours anymore."

"I told you mine. Tell me yours."

She looked at him sideways. Then she looked ahead.

"All right."

She told him.

She told him about the joint she had fixed for the man with the cistern, who was the third man this month who had brought her a cistern joint that had cracked because the building's main cistern was not being kept warm enough at night and the joints were freezing micro-freezes that the man and his wife did not see in the morning until the joint cracked at the third week. She told him about the woman in her own building, on the second floor, whose hot-water cap had stopped warming three weeks ago and who had been heating water on a stove for three weeks because the channeler the building paid for had taken on two more buildings and was now coming once a week instead of every day, and the once-a-week cap warming did not last, and Ryn had been going up on the second floor every other evening and warming the cap herself, for free, because the woman was sixty-eight and had been the woman in her building since Ryn was a child, and Ryn was not going to charge the woman in her building.

"She's named Sirth," she said.

"Sirth."

"You don't know her. You met her once. She gave you a piece of bread when you were seven because you had fallen on the step. She does not remember it. I do."

"Mm."

"And she isn't doing well."

"How."

"Cold. Tired. The flat is cold even when I warm the cap. The cap warms the room next to her but not the room she sleeps in, because the run was put in fifty years ago and the sleeping room got the smaller branch, and it would take a day's work and three good channelers to re-do the run and she doesn't have three good channelers and she doesn't have a day's work and she doesn't have the coin. So she sleeps in the room with the cap."

"Ryn."

"Don't. Don't fix it. I'm telling you what it is. I'm not asking you to fix it."

"All right."

"There's a man in the next building. He has two daughters. Both of them have small pools, smaller than mine, and his pool is gone, mostly, the way a man's pool goes when he's been working a heating run for thirty years and the run has started taking more than it gives. His daughters are doing the channeling for the building. Both of them. At fifteen and thirteen. And they're tired. And they're going to be tired for a long time. And he is, I think, ashamed about it, in a way I don't know how to talk to him about. So I don't. I just bring bread on a Tuesday."

"Tuesday."

"Tuesday because he gets paid Monday and the bread is gone by Tuesday and his wife will not ask anyone for bread but she will eat bread that has appeared on the step."

"Mm."

She walked.

"And I do not want," she said, "what the Levelers want."

"No."

"I do not want my pool to be a public pool. I do not want a young man at my door with a polite invitation to come stand on a platform. I do not want to be Sera in a square. I do not want to be in any square. I want my pool to be my pool and to use it on the woman on the second floor and on the cistern joints of men whose wives bake bread, and I want to be paid in coin and in chits and in the small things people give me, and I want to go home tired in a way I chose."

"All right."

"And."

"And."

"I do not want, also, what your friend Haran wants. Or what you want. I don't want a hand-pump in my yard. I don't want to be told to insulate my walls with fiber. Maybe I will. Maybe I will need to. But I do not, Ash, I do not want to be a person who has been moved off her channeling by a clever man with a workshop who has decided that the clever thing is the thing for me. I do not want to be a problem you have solved."

"Ryn."

"I'm not done."

"All right."

"What I want. What I want is small. I want the world to stop getting harder. I want my mother's flat in the country to be warmer in the winters than the winters keep being. I want Sirth's sleeping room to be warmed. I want the man's daughters to not be tired at thirteen. I want to do my work and be paid for it and have enough at the end of the day. I am not asking for the redistribution of pools. I am not asking for hand-cranks in courtyards. I am asking for the world to stop getting harder, which is, I know, the one thing nobody is offering."

She stopped.

She stopped walking. She had not raised her voice. She had said the whole thing at the pace of a woman walking, and the saying of it had been a saying, not a speech, and the not-speech of it was the thing that landed.

"All right," he said.

"All right."

"Ryn."

"Yes."

"So what do I do."

She looked at him.

She looked at him the way she looked at a joint she was about to test, with her chin at the angle and her hand half out of her pocket and her weight on her back foot. Then she looked away.

"I don't know."

"All right."

"I don't know, Ash. I am not the woman who knows what you do. I am not on the council. I am not in the Hall. I have not been in any room with a banner."

"All right."

"But I know what you don't do."

"All right."

"You don't disappear again."

"All right."

"You don't go back up the curve and not come down for nine months. That is the thing you don't do. That is the only thing I am sure of in this conversation."

"All right."

"And you don't, Ash, you don't go off and find the smart answer, and build the smart answer, and put the smart answer in front of people who didn't ask you for it, and then sit in your workshop and wonder why nobody's grateful."

"That isn't, I didn't, I."

"Don't."

"All right."

"You built a water system for people who didn't ask for a water system."

"They needed water."

"They needed water. Yes. They also needed a hundred other things. They needed someone to ask them which of the hundred things to do first. They needed someone to sit down on their step and say *what is the thing you are most tired of,* and listen to the answer, and not have the answer already in their pocket. Maybe the answer would have been water. Maybe it would have been the run that doesn't reach the sleeping room. Maybe it would have been the thirteen-year-old daughter. You didn't ask. You built. And you built well, Ash. I'm not saying you built badly. I am saying that maybe they needed someone to listen first."

He looked at the cobble.

He looked at the cobble for a count. The cobble was the cobble. There was, at the corner of his eye, a poster pasted to the wall with the corner come up, and the poster did the small lift the wind made it do, and lay back down. He did not read it.

"Ryn."

"Mm."

"That's, that's hard."

"I know."

"It's also true."

"I know."

"How did you, how do you, when did you decide to say that to me."

"On the walk."

"On this walk."

"Yes."

"You didn't have it ready."

"I had pieces."

"Mm."

"I had the piece about disappearing. I have had that piece for nine months. The other pieces I had this morning when you came in looking the way you looked."

"How did I look."

"I told you."

"Terrible."

"Yes."

He breathed.

He breathed in and out, and the breathing was the thing the breathing was, which was a man breathing on a corner with a friend, and the friend had said the thing the friend had to say, and the friend was standing beside him, and the standing-beside was the thing the friend had to give, and the friend was giving it.

"All right," he said.

"All right."

"Walk."

"Walk."

* * *

They walked the long way back.

They did not talk much on the long way back.
