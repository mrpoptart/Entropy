# Chapter 9

The note came up the stair at the second hour, on cheap paper, folded twice.

Haran brought it in the way Haran brought a thing. He laid it on the bench beside the brass blank he had been finishing the night before. He did not unfold it. He said, "From the Cooperage," and then he said nothing else, and then he picked up the file and went back to the blank.

Ash unfolded the note.

The hand was small, even, deliberate. Six lines. *We would like to come and see what you have built. Three of us. An hour, perhaps two. No press, no platform. You may say no. If you say yes, name the day.* The signature was a single name without ornament. *Davan.*

Ash read it twice.

He set it on the bench beside the file. The file moved. The brass shaving curled. Haran did not look up.

"He wrote it himself," Ash said.

"Mm."

"You know the hand."

"I know the paper."

"Mm."

A long beat. The file moved. The shaving lengthened, broke, fell, lay.

"You'll say yes," Haran said.

It was not a question. Haran did not, on the second blank, look at him to see whether the not-question had landed where he had aimed it. He aimed it the way he aimed all his not-questions, which was at the bench. The bench took it.

"I'll say yes," Ash said.

"When."

"Third-day."

"Mm."

"Third-day, fourth hour. The square. Not the workshop."

"Mm."

Haran filed.

Ash sat on the crate and watched him file, and the watching was the watching it had been a year, which was the watching of a man who had taught him how to watch a man work without filling the watching with talk. The filing had a tempo. The tempo had not changed, the year, although Ash had changed, although the workshop had changed, although a folded note from a man whose voice carried across eighty paces of yard now lay on the bench between the file and the brass.

The note lay flat.

"You'll want the square clear," Haran said.

"I'll tell Reska."

"Mm."

"Vell will be at the pump."

"She will."

"That's all right."

"It is."

Haran set the file down. He picked up the brass blank. He held it to the lamp. He turned it once. He set it down. He picked up the file.

The note lay on the bench through the rest of the morning, unfolded, the small chalk-clean rectangle of it, the six lines facing up. Ash did not move it. He went out to the square at the third hour and helped Reska move a length of pipe and came back at the fourth and the note was where he had left it. He read it again. The hand was the hand it had been. *You may say no.* He folded it once and put it on the under-bench shelf, and the shelf was the shelf it had been the night before, and was not.

* * *

Third-day came up cold and clear.

He did not, in the morning, change his coat. He wore the coat he wore. He had Reska shift the morning crew's water test from the basin to the eastern pump so the basin would be open at the fourth hour, and he asked Vell to run a long primer at the third so that the manifold would be at temperature when the visitors came, and he did not, in the asking, tell her why. Vell did not ask. She nodded, the small precise nod she had begun to nod since the rally, the nod of a girl who had come back from the Cooperage and had not, on coming back, said where she had been. She primed the pump. She wiped the cap with the rag. She waited.

The visitors came up through the square at the fourth hour exactly.

Ash heard them before he saw them. Three sets of boots on the cobbles, not hurried, not loud. He was at the basin tap, his hand on the iron of it, the small clean weight of the lever in his palm. He turned. He did not stand up straight any faster than he would have stood for anyone.

Davan was in the middle.

He was tall in the daylight the way he had been tall on the cart, and the coat was the coat from the cart, and his hands were at his sides the way his hands had been at his sides on the cart. The man on his left was older, perhaps forty, broad through the shoulders, a beard cut close, a scar at the line of the jaw on the right side that ran down into the collar. The woman on Davan's right was younger, Ash's age perhaps, dark hair pulled back, a satchel across her body, a notebook in her hand that she was not, at this moment, opening.

Davan stopped four paces off.

"Ash," he said.

"Davan."

"This is Korrel. This is Sema."

Korrel inclined his head, the small inclination of a man who had inclined his head at many doors. Sema said "Good morning" in a voice that had the quick clipped vowels of the eastern districts, the canal-quarters past the basin works, where the speech ran a half-beat faster than the central city's. Ash registered the vowels. He registered the satchel. He registered the notebook closed.

"Thanks for coming up," Ash said.

"Thanks for having us."

"It's a working square. I can't promise quiet."

"We didn't come for quiet."

The corner of Davan's mouth moved. Not a smile. The shape a smile would have been, in a man who had not, in this hour, decided to spend one. Ash noticed. He noticed the way he noticed a tolerance on a fitting: present, measured, not yet engaged.

"I'll show you the pump," Ash said. "Then the cistern. Then the block, if you want the block. The workers can speak for themselves, when we get to them. I'd rather they did."

"Good," Davan said.

They went.

* * *

Vell was at the eastern pump.

She had the cap off. She had her sleeve rolled to the elbow on the right arm and her hand on the stroke-arm and her eyes on the manifold gauge, and she was, Ash registered, performing none of this. She was running the primer she ran every Third-day at this hour, and the visitors were a thing in the periphery of her attention, and the primer was the work.

"This is Vell," Ash said. "She runs the morning shift on the eastern pump. She's fifteen."

Sema opened the notebook.

Davan did not look at the notebook. He looked at Vell. He looked at her hand on the stroke-arm, and at the manifold gauge, and at the small bright bead of water at the seal where the seal sweated when the line was at temperature, and then he looked at Vell's face.

"What does the bead tell you," Davan said.

Vell's hand did not stop moving on the arm.

"That the line's hot."

"Hot enough?"

"Not yet."

"How do you know."

"It hasn't run."

"Run?"

"Down the side. When it's hot enough it slips. Right now it's holding. So it's not."

Davan nodded. Once. The nod was the nod of a man who had been told a thing and had filed the thing in the place the thing went.

"How long does it take to get to slip," he said.

"Four minutes. Sometimes five. Today it'll be five. Cold morning."

"You count."

"In my head."

"Which count."

"Ones and twos. Reska taught me ones-and-twos for the long ones. They're slower. You don't lose them."

"Show me."

Vell glanced at Ash. She glanced quick, the glance a girl glanced at the man who had given her the pump when a stranger asked her to demonstrate. Ash did not move his face. Vell looked back at Davan. She said, low, "One-and. Two-and. Three-and." She said it the way she said it in her head, which was the way she had been taught, which was Reska's way, and Reska had got it from a stevedore at the harbor, and the harbor stevedores had got it from somewhere older than any of them.

Davan listened.

He listened the way he had listened to the older man on the cart at the beginning of the rally, which was the way a person listened who was not, while listening, building the next thing he was going to say. He listened to *one-and, two-and, three-and* the way Haran listened to a kettle. When Vell stopped, he said, "Thank you."

"Mm," Vell said.

It was Haran's *mm.* Ash registered the borrowing. Vell had not, until this minute, said *mm* in the workshop register that he had heard. She had picked it up off Haran the way Haran had picked up *fair* off Ash and the way Reska had picked up *line's hot* off Vell. The square was, in its small ways, a vocabulary.

Davan moved to the manifold.

He did not touch it. He looked at the pipe-runs. He looked at the brass coupling at the riser. He looked at the wood frame of the pump-house and the iron lag-bolts at the base, and at the way the run came out of the cistern wall and turned twice before it reached the riser, and at the cleanout cap on the low side. He looked at it the way an engineer looked at a thing, except that he was, Ash registered, not an engineer.

"Why two turns," Davan said.

"The cistern wall. The exchange has to clear it."

"At the height?"

"Below the height. The pressure drop is what we wanted."

"Why."

"So a hand crank could match it."

"And the second turn?"

"Slows it before the riser. Otherwise the seal at the riser wears in a season."

"Is that yours?"

"Haran's."

Davan looked at him. The look was not long. It was a half-second, the half-second a man took when an answer arrived a half-step earlier than he had planned for it.

"Haran's at the workshop?"

"Yes."

"Mm."

Korrel had been at the cistern indicator. He came back. He had a pencil in his hand and a slip of folded paper, and he had written something on the paper and put the pencil back in his coat pocket. He did not show Ash the paper.

"The indicator's hand-built," he said. To Davan, not to Ash.

"It is," Ash said.

"Whose."

"A boy on the second crew. He left."

"Why."

"Personal."

"Mm."

Korrel folded the paper smaller. He put it in his coat. The not-asking was, Ash registered, a thing Korrel did the way Haran did the not-questions: by aiming them at the bench. Korrel had no bench. He aimed them at his coat pocket, and the coat pocket took them.

"Show me Block Three," Davan said.

* * *

They went the long way around.

Ash took them along the eastern wall of the square, past the bakery where Othen had slept the week of the second cold snap, past the cellar door of Block Three with its small lit lamp on the inside of the doorframe. He did not narrate the lamp. He did not narrate the bakery. He let the square be what the square was on a Third-day at the fourth hour, which was a square, with a basin, and a pump, and a row of lit windows, and the small carrying sounds of people doing what people did in their kitchens at the fourth hour of a cold morning.

Davan looked at the windows.

He looked at them the way he had looked at Vell's pump, which was the way a man looked at a thing he wanted to understand. Not at the row of them. At one. The third from the left, second floor. The lamp behind the curtain was a small steady amber. Davan looked at it for the time it took for the curtain to move once and settle.

"Who's in the third from the left," he said.

"Tesha," Ash said. "And her daughter. Her daughter's seven."

"Tesha was without heat last winter."

"She was."

"You know that."

"I asked."

"Mm."

The block opened. The cellar door was the cellar door. Ash took them into the basement, where the run came in off the cistern through the wall and into the manifold, and the manifold split, and the splits ran up through the chase to the four flats above. The chase was lit by a single lamp at the top. The lamp had a clean glass. Reska kept it clean. Ash had not asked her to keep it clean. She had decided.

Korrel ran his hand along the chase.

"Insulated."

"Wool batt. Tar paper. The wool came from the harbor."

"How long."

"The batt? Five years, if it's dry. Three if the chase sweats."

"Does it sweat."

"Not yet. We'll know in spring."

"Who installed it."

"I did. Two of the workers. The boy who left."

"You did."

"I did the channeling on the seal at the manifold. The batt was hand work. Anyone can do the batt."

"Who's anyone."

"The crew. New crews, when we train them."

Korrel's hand stayed on the chase.

"The seal at the manifold," he said. "If it goes."

"Then I come back and reseat it."

"You. Personally."

"Yes."

"How often, you think."

"Once every two years. Maybe three. The fuse is good."

"Good's not forever."

"No."

"And when you stop coming."

A silence in the chase. The lamp at the top of the chase moved a small flicker the way the lamp moved when a draft came down off the upper floor. Ash did not, in the silence, look at Davan. He looked at Korrel. Korrel was looking at the batt. The not-looking was, Ash registered, also a question.

"Then someone learns to reseat it without channeling," Ash said. "Or we redesign the seal."

"Have you."

"Started. Not done."

"Mm."

Korrel moved his hand off the chase.

* * *

They went up to the workshop at the fifth hour.

Ash had not planned to take them up. He had planned to walk them to the western edge of the square, thank them, and let them go. But the woman, Sema, asked, in the small tight vowels, whether she could see the bench, and Davan looked at Ash, and Ash heard himself say "Come up" before he had decided to say it.

Haran was at the bench.

He was working a brass blank. Not the one from the morning. A different one, smaller, a thing for the sliding mount of a hand-lamp, and the file was the small file, the one Haran used on brass when he wanted the surface to come up clean without tooth. He did not, on the door opening, look up. He did not look up when Ash said *Haran, this is Davan, Korrel, Sema.* He took the blank off the bench. He held it to the lamp. He turned it once. He set it down. He picked up the file.

"Mm," he said.

Davan stood at the threshold a beat. The beat was the beat a man took at a threshold he had been invited across by one householder when a second householder was at the bench. Then he came in. He came in the way he had come into the yard at the Cooperage, which was, Ash registered, the same way: chest open, hands at sides, his weight on the back foot.

"Master Haran," Davan said.

"Just Haran."

"Haran."

"Mm."

Davan went to the bench. He did not stand close. He stood at the corner of the bench across from the file. He looked at the blank. He looked at the wall above the bench, where the small tools hung in their order, and at the under-bench shelf, where the broadsheet and the transcript and the platform were folded in the order they had come in, and at the kettle, and at the brown pot with its chip.

He did not, in the looking, ask.

Haran filed.

Korrel and Sema stayed at the door. Sema had her notebook closed under her arm. Korrel had his hands in his coat pockets. They did not, Ash registered, come in. They had, in the small calculus of the threshold, decided that the workshop was a room for two, and that the two who had been invited across were Davan and Ash, and that they would be the ones who waited.

"Tea?" Ash said.

"Please," Davan said.

"Korrel. Sema."

"Please."

"Please."

Ash put the kettle on the stove. The stove was warm. The kettle took its time.

"You can sit," Haran said.

He had not looked up. He said it to the file, which was the way he said most things, and the file took it, and Davan crossed to the crate by the stove and sat. He sat the way a man sat on a crate, which was to say he sat without ceremony, the way Ash had seen him stand on the cart, the way he had seen him stand at four paces in the square: as if his body did not, in the moment, ask for anything more than the surface under it would give.

Korrel and Sema took the tea at the door.

Ash poured. He poured for Davan into the brown pot's matching cup, the one Haran used for visitors who were not, that day, the kind of visitor a chipped cup would insult. He gave Davan the cup. He poured his own into the chipped one. He sat on the second crate, the low one, and the two crates were the two crates, Ash on the low and Davan on the high, and the bench was beside them, and Haran was across the bench, filing.

"How long has the workshop been here," Davan said.

"Forty-two years," Haran said.

"You opened it."

"Mm."

"Before the second cistern's failure."

"Long before."

"You knew it would fail."

"I knew the exchange was wrong. I didn't know the year."

"Mm."

Haran filed. The shaving curled.

"You have a pool," Davan said.

It was not a question. Haran did not, on the not-question, look up. He said, "I do."

"You don't use it."

"I use it some days."

"Not many."

"No."

"Why."

The file moved. The shaving curled, broke, fell. Haran picked up the brass blank and held it to the lamp and turned it once and set it down.

"It wasn't the work," Haran said.

He said it the way he said *mm,* which was to the bench. Davan listened to it the way he had listened to Vell, which was to say without preparing the next thing. The kettle made a small sound on the stove. Ash watched Davan listen. Davan's face did not move. He took the answer the way he had taken Vell's *one-and, two-and, three-and,* which was the way a man took a thing he had not been given before in quite that shape.

"Mm," Davan said.

It was not Haran's *mm.* It was, Ash registered, a different *mm,* a *mm* of a man who had registered a sentence and would, later, take it out and look at it again. Haran did not, on the not-borrowing, react. He filed.

"You'll want the bench by the window," Haran said.

He said it to Ash.

Ash looked. The bench by the window was the long bench, the one Haran used for laying out a job that was going to take more than a morning, and the bench was clear at this hour because Haran had not yet started the job he would start at the sixth. Haran was, Ash registered, giving them the bench. He was clearing the room for them by suggesting they move to it.

"The bench by the window," Ash said to Davan.

"Yes."

They moved.

* * *

The workbench by the window held the long light.

It came in low through the small panes at this hour, an angle the light took only in the cold months, when the sun was south of the workshop's ridge and the panes broke the light into clean parallel bars across the bench and the wall behind. The bench was scarred. It had been scarred by forty-two years of brass and iron and copper and wood, and the scars were the scars of work, not damage, and Ash had spent enough hours at the bench to know which scar was the chisel-slip from the year before he came and which was the fire-mark from the kettle's first stand.

He sat on the stool.

Davan took the second stool.

Between them on the bench were a wrench, a small wooden box of brass washers, a length of pipe Ash had been planning to cut at the seventh hour, and a pencil. The pencil was Haran's. It was the short one.

"You said the seal," Davan said.

"At the manifold."

"In the chase."

"Yes."

"Show me how you'd reseat it. Without channeling."

Ash looked at the wrench.

He picked up the wrench. He picked up the pipe. He laid them on the bench in the relation they would lie in the chase, the wrench in the position he would hold the wrench in, the pipe at the angle the pipe came out of the wall. He did not, in the laying, talk. He laid them, and then he showed Davan, with the wrench's lower jaw in his left hand and his right thumb at the place the seal-collar rode the pipe, the rotation the seal would take to clear the lip.

"You wedge a brass pin here," he said. "The pin holds the collar against the lip while you back the lock-nut off. Then you pull the collar."

"Pin where."

"In the lip. There's a pre-drilled seat. I put it in when I sealed it."

"You knew."

"I knew the seal would have to come out one day. So I gave the lip a place to take the pin."

Davan picked up the wrench.

He did not weigh it the way men weighed wrenches. He held it. He moved it the way Ash had moved it, the way a person moved a tool when the person was, in the moving, learning the shape of the hand that had moved it before. Then he set it down. He picked up the pencil. He sketched, on a corner of bench-paper Ash had been using to track the morning's draws, the shape of the seal. He sketched it well. The lines were clean. The pin-seat was where Ash had put the pin-seat. The lock-nut was where the lock-nut was. Davan's hand drew the way the hand of a man who had drawn many drawings drew, which was to say he drew without looking up at his own hand.

"You did this," Davan said. "On every seal in the block."

"Yes."

"How long did it take."

"On each? Twenty minutes. I did sixty-two seals. Not in a row."

"What was the channeling."

"Catalytic. Heating the brass to the temperature where the fuse takes. Holding it. The fuse itself's mechanical. The temperature's mine."

"How much. Total."

"Three quarters of one percent. Across the block."

"Mm."

Davan put the pencil down.

He put it across the sketch, the short pencil along the line of the lock-nut, the way a man set a pencil down when he had finished the drawing he had been making for himself. Then he sat. He sat for a long count, and he did not, in the count, look at Ash.

"It's good," Davan said. "What you've built."

"Thank you."

"I want to be honest with you."

"All right."

"I came here ready to find a thing I could explain. I didn't. The work is real. The pump is real. The girl can run the pump. The block is warm. The man at the workshop is who he is, and the seals are what you said they are, and Korrel will tell me, on the way down, that the ledger is the ledger. I came up the stair willing to walk down with whatever I found. What I found is that you have built a thing that works."

He paused. The pause was not the cart's pause. It was a smaller pause, the pause of a man who had decided what he was going to say and was, for a half-beat, taking the temperature of the room before he said it.

"And I want to ask you something," Davan said.

"Ask."

"You lived without magic for twenty-three years."

"Yes."

"You know what's coming for everyone."

"I do."

"Why aren't you angry."

The window held the light.

The bars of light lay where they had lain. Across the wall, a bar fell on the small framed sheet Haran had pinned there the year before Ash came, a rough sketch of a flue diagram, and the bar lit the corner of the sketch the way the corner had been lit any morning Ash had been in the workshop at this hour. The bench was the bench. The wrench was the wrench. Davan was waiting.

Ash heard himself.

He heard the breath go in. He heard it go out. He heard, the way a man heard his own pulse in a room that had gone too quiet, the small steady sit of the reservoir at the back of his attention, the held weight of it, the *thirty-eight* of it, the number Haran had taught him to feel without naming. He felt his hands open on his knees the way he had felt them open at the eastern wall of the Cooperage four nights before, and he did not, in the opening, name what was in them.

"I was angry," Ash said. "For twenty-three years."

He said it slow.

"Being angry didn't build anything."

The bars of light did not move. Davan did not nod. Davan did not, in the moment, do any of the things a man did when a man took an answer. He held it. He let it sit between them on the bench, beside the wrench and the box of washers and the pencil and the pipe Ash had been planning to cut at the seventh hour.

The kettle had stopped making the kettle's sound.

"Mm," Davan said.

It was not Haran's *mm,* and it was not the *mm* he had given Haran. It was a third *mm,* the *mm* of a man who had been given a sentence and had, in the giving, recognized that the sentence was the answer and was not the answer. He looked, for the first time in the conversation, at the window.

The light was the light.

"I was angry," Davan said, "for fifteen."

He said it the way a man said a thing he had not, in this room, said before. Not the way a man said a thing he had said often, in many rooms, on many carts. He said it the way the rally had not been said.

"I was angry from nine to twenty-four. I was angry the way a child is angry, which is the way a child makes a fist at a wall that is not, in the next minute, going to be moved by the fist. I was angry at fourteen when my mother lost the back of her pool and didn't tell us for six months because she didn't want us to have to look at her differently. I was angry at sixteen when the schoolmaster gave the channeling exam to the boys with the strong fathers and didn't give it to us because we weren't, in his view, going to need the certificate. I was angry at twenty-one in a Council gallery, watching a councillor explain that the redistribution petitioners had not yet, in his estimation, demonstrated that the question was a question."

He paused.

"And then I built a thing."

He looked at the wrench.

"I built it the way you built yours. I built it because the anger was a fire that warmed the room when I lit it and the room when I stopped lighting it was the same room, and the room was cold. So I started carrying coal. I started laying a fire that would burn without me at it. I built the movement the way you built the seals: in pieces, with a fuse at every joint, with a pin where the pin would have to go when the seal had to come out."

Ash watched him.

"You and I are not so different," Davan said. "We both stopped being angry. We both started building. The difference is the thing we built."

"Yes."

"And the difference matters."

"Yes."

"You built a system that works without channeling, in one square, with one workshop, on the back of one pool. I built a movement that asks the people who have channeling left to share it. Your system, if the city had ten of you, would warm the city in twenty years. My movement, if it carries, will warm the city in two."

"And then?"

"And then we have two more winters."

"After that."

"After that we have what we have. You build, in the warm. I level, in the warm. Neither of us, in the winters before the warm, asks the canal quarter to die."

The pipe lay on the bench.

Ash did not pick it up. He did not pick up the wrench. He did not pick up the pencil. He sat on the stool with his hands open on his knees and he heard, at the back of his attention, the small steady sit of the reservoir, and he heard, in the chase below, the manifold breathing the way the manifold breathed, and he heard, beyond the wall, the low square sounds of Reska teaching a girl the count.

"I disagree with the prescription," he said.

He said it the way he had thought it, on the towpath, beside the dark canal. He said it knowing it was the sentence the powerful man wrote in the upper-terrace journals at twenty-one. He said it because he could not, in the bench's light, not say it.

"I know," Davan said.

"You know."

"I came up the stair knowing. I am sitting on the stool knowing. The disagreement is why I came. I did not come to be persuaded. I came to see whether the man I disagree with is the man I thought he was."

"And?"

"You are. And you aren't. You're better than the man I rehearsed against. You are also, exactly, the man I rehearsed against. Both of these are true."

"Mm."

"Mm."

The bars of light moved, a little, the small slow drift of bars of light across a bench through the panes of a workshop window in the cold months, and the moving was the moving of the morning, and the morning was the morning, and the wrench lay where it lay, and Haran filed across the room.

Korrel came in from the door at the sixth hour.

He came in the way he had come in: hands in his coat pockets, the small inclination of the head, the not-question aimed at his own coat. He stopped at the corner of the bench.

"Hour," he said.

"Mm," Davan said.

Davan stood.

He stood in the way he had sat, which was without ceremony, his weight settling onto the back foot, his hands at his sides. He picked up the pencil and put it on the bench-paper sketch, across the lock-nut, the way he had put it down. He left the sketch.

"Thank you," he said. "For the morning."

"Thank you for coming."

"We'll come again, if you'll have us."

"You're welcome to."

"I'd like to talk about the seal."

"Any time."

"Mm."

He turned.

He was at the door when he stopped. He did not turn his head all the way back. He turned it to the angle a man turned his head when he had remembered a thing he had decided not to forget.

"Ash."

"Yes."

"The pump works."

"It does."

"It's also a year away from helping anyone who isn't in this district."

The light on the bench did not move.

"What do they do until then."

He said it quiet. He said it the way he had said his name at the cart, without ornament, without weight he had to add, the way a man named a fact and laid the fact on a table and stepped away from the table. He did not wait for the answer. He had not asked for the answer. He had asked for the question, which he had laid on the bench between the wrench and the pencil and the unfinished sketch of the seal, and the question lay there, and Davan went out of the door.

Korrel inclined his head.

Sema did not look up from the satchel she was, at the threshold, closing. She closed it. She nodded once. She went.

The door closed.

* * *

Haran filed.

The file moved. The shaving curled. The brass blank on the bench across the room caught the lamp and gave it back, the small bright bead of light at the edge where Haran had taken the oxide off in a clean line. Haran did not look up. He did not say *mm.* He did not say *you'll go down to the basin.* He filed.

Ash sat on the stool.

The wrench was on the bench. The pencil was on the bench. The pipe was on the bench. Davan's sketch was on the bench-paper, the lines clean, the pin-seat where Ash had put it. The bars of light had moved a finger's width.

He did not, in the moment, name what he was sitting in.

He sat in it.

He sat for the time it took the kettle to come back to the small pre-boil sound, and Haran took it off, and Haran poured, and the brown pot took the water the way the brown pot took the water, and the pour was a thing the pour was. Haran did not, on the pour, look at him. He set the pot on the bench. He went back to the file.

"Mm," Haran said.

It was, Ash registered, the *mm* he had been waiting for. It was the *mm* of a man who had been at the bench across the room through the whole conversation and had not, in the through, looked up, and was not, on the after, going to say the thing he was, at the back of his filing, thinking. The *mm* held it. The *mm* was the holding.

Ash got up.

He went down the stair to the square and stood at the basin for a long minute, and the basin was the basin, and Vell was at the eastern pump finishing the morning's last draw, and Reska was at the cistern indicator chalking the mark, and the lamps in the windows of Block Three were on the small irregular pattern a building's windows made on a cold morning at the sixth hour.

He did not turn the tap.

He stood at the basin.

Across the square, past the lower stair, past the curve of the western edge, the streets ran down toward the canal. The buildings on those streets were the buildings of those streets. The windows on the upper floors were dark, mostly, at this hour, the way the windows of a quarter that did not warm in winter were dark in winter.

He counted, by tens, the dark windows.

He stopped at sixty.

* * *

Davan came back on Fifth-day.

He came alone. He came at the third hour, in the same coat, with no notebook and no satchel, and he stood at the gate of the square the way a man stood at a gate when he had been told he was welcome and was, in the standing, not yet using the welcome. Ash saw him from the workshop window. He went down.

"You came up."

"I said I would."

"You did."

"The seal," Davan said. "I want to see the redesign."

"It isn't done."

"That's why I want to see it."

They went up.

Haran was at the bench. He filed. He said *mm* on the door opening, and that was all he said for the hour Davan was at the bench, and the *mm* was, Ash registered, the *mm* it had been on Third-day, which was the *mm* of a man who had decided, the year before, what he would say in this room and was, for as long as the room was the room, going to say it.

They worked at the bench by the window.

Ash showed him the sketch he had begun in the night. Davan asked. Davan asked the way he had asked Vell about the bead at the seal, which was to say without preparation, without the next thing, and the asking made Ash's answers come in shapes Ash had not, before the asking, found for them. He found, in the answering, three things he had not known about the seal until Davan had asked them, and he wrote them on the paper, and Davan watched him write, and Davan did not, in the watching, write anything down himself.

The pencil moved. The shaving did not.

At the end of the hour Davan stood. He thanked Haran. Haran said *mm.* Davan thanked Ash. Ash said *come back.* Davan said *I will,* and he went down the stair, and Ash watched him cross the square and go out the western stair, and he did not, in the watching, name what he was watching.

* * *

The letters started after that.

Davan wrote in the same hand, the same paper, the same six-line economy. He wrote about the seal. He wrote about a tap design Korrel had seen in a harbor-quarter shop. He wrote, once, about a question one of his speakers had asked him, which was whether the canal quarter would, this winter, take the heat the platform was promising it, and what *take the heat* would, in practical terms, mean, and Ash sat with the letter at the bench through the morning and did not, by the afternoon, have an answer he could write back without writing the sentence about disagreeing with the prescription, and he set the letter on the under-bench shelf.

The shelf was fuller, by the second week, than it had ever been.

Ash wrote when he wrote. He wrote about the seal. He did not write back about the canal quarter. Davan, in the next letter, did not ask again. The not-asking was a thing. The thing sat on the shelf, with the platform and the broadsheet and the transcript and the two letters with the blue wax, and the shelf was the shelf it had been the morning of Chapter Eight, and was not.

Haran watched.

He watched the way Haran watched, which was by not watching. He filed. He held the brass blanks to the lamp. He took the kettle off at the small pre-boil sound. He poured into the brown pot. He did not, in any of the days Davan came up the stair or the days the letters arrived, ask Ash about the visits or the letters or the shelf. He did not ask. The not-asking was, Ash registered, also a watching. It was the watching of a man who had decided, decades before, that some questions a person had to find on his own bench, and that the bench was the bench whether the person found them today or tomorrow, and that the kettle, at the sixth hour, had to come off.

Once, on a morning Davan had gone, Haran said, "He listens."

"He does."

"Mm."

"He's good," Ash said. "At what he does."

"Mm."

"He's not wrong about the diagnosis."

"No."

"He's wrong about the prescription."

A beat.

"Mm," Haran said.

He filed.

The file moved. The shaving curled. The lamp's small bright bead at the edge of the brass held.

* * *

On the eighth day Davan came at the second hour.

He came alone. He carried, this time, a pamphlet, folded, which he did not bring to the bench. He set it on the under-bench shelf with the letters, without comment, and he came up to the bench by the window and he sat on the second stool.

They worked an hour.

The seal had moved. Ash had a second sketch. Davan asked about the pin-seat, and the asking moved the sketch, and Ash wrote in the corner *try hardened brass for the pin,* and Davan said *yes,* and they sat for a moment looking at the sketch, and Davan looked, then, at the window.

The light was different. It was later in the morning. The bars were narrower. The angle was higher. The bench held a different geometry.

"There was a death in the canal quarter last night," Davan said.

He said it to the window.

"A woman. Sixty-one. Two flats above the chase that closed when the second cistern failed. Cold. She had been cold a week. The neighbor went in this morning. The neighbor told one of our organizers. The organizer told me at the second hour."

He did not, in the saying, look at Ash.

"I am not telling you this," Davan said, "to ask for the pool. I am not telling you this to make a sentence on a cart. I am telling you because I came up the stair this morning and we worked an hour on the seal, and the seal, when it is finished, will be a good seal. It will be a seal a worker can pull without channeling. It will keep a chase warm in five years and ten and twenty. You are right about the seal. I want you to know I think you are right about the seal."

The bars of light on the bench held.

"And," Davan said, "the woman."

He said *and.* He said it small. He laid it the way he had laid the other things, without weight he had to add, the way a man named a fact, and the fact was the woman, and the woman was sixty-one, and the woman had been cold a week.

"The problem with building for the future," Davan said, "is that people are dying in the present."

He said it to the window.

"Your pump works. It is also a year away from helping anyone who is not in this district. What do they do until then."

He did not turn his head.

The pencil lay on the sketch.

The wrench lay on the bench beside the box of brass washers.

The light on the wall lit the corner of Haran's flue diagram the way the light lit it on any morning at the third hour in the cold months, and the file moved across the room, and the shaving curled, and the kettle had not yet been put on for the third pot of the morning.

Ash did not, in the moment, answer.

He sat on the stool with his hands open on his knees.

He sat with the question on the bench between him and the man he had agreed with, point by point, for an hour and a half a day for nine days, and he sat with the small steady sit of the reservoir at the back of his attention, the held weight of it, the *thirty-eight* of it, the number that had not, in nine days, moved a tenth, and he sat with the dark windows of the canal quarter that he had counted, on Third-day, and stopped at sixty.

The wrench did not move.

The bars of light did not move.

Outside, in the square, Vell was running the morning's primer, and the manifold was coming to temperature, and the line was holding, and the bead at the seal had not yet, by the count, slipped.
