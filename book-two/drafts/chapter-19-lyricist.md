# Chapter 19

The broadsheets came in on the morning carter, and Haran read them at the long bench.

He read them the way he read the letters, which was in the order they had come, and he set them in a row at the iron plate at the side of the lamp the way he set the country letters in a row, and he did not, in the reading, say what was in any of them. He read the first. He set it. He read the second. He set it. He read the third, which was the broadsheet of the printers' guild and which came once a week and which carried, on the back page, the small flat list of grid notices that the criers had been crying in the streets. He set it. He read the fourth, which was a single folded sheet on the cheap stock the Levelers used for their own broadsheet, and he held this one a count longer than the others, and he set it down at the corner of the bench at the going-in place.

He did not say anything.

Ash came to the bench. He picked up the broadsheet at the going-in place. He sat at the stool.

The page was a list.

The list ran down the left column, and at the head of it was a sentence, and the sentence said, in the plain unornamented type the Levelers' printer used, *Grids at reduced output, week of the eleventh*, and the list under it was a list of districts. He read the list. He counted, in the small accounting he had begun doing without quite meaning to, the number of districts on it. He counted nineteen. The week before, on the broadsheet that had come the week before, he had not counted, because the week before he had not been counting. He set this one against last week's, which Haran had also set in the row, and he counted last week's. He counted fourteen.

Five more districts.

In a week.

He read the right column. The right column was a notice from the council faction the broadsheet's masthead claimed, which was Davan's faction, the redistribution faction, and the notice was a call. The call asked the elder channelers in three named quarters to come to the contribution hall on the seventh and pour into the spring grids for the autumn. The call was worded the way Davan worded a thing, which was direct, and unembarrassed, and built on the assumption that the people reading the call would do the thing being asked for, because the thing being asked for was the right thing. The call did not, in any of its sentences, acknowledge that the people reading it had been pouring into the spring grids in the spring, and into the summer grids in the summer, and that what they had to pour with now was the thinned thing of a year of pouring.

The call was the call of a man who had not, in his arithmetic, allowed the number to keep going down.

Ash set the broadsheet at the corner of the bench.

He sat at the stool a count.

He looked at Haran.

Haran was at the next letter. Haran did not look up. After a count, without looking up, Haran said, "Mm."

"Mm," Ash said.

"Nineteen."

"Yes."

"Last week."

"Fourteen."

"Mm."

Haran read his letter. The forge ticked. Ash, at the stool, took the cheap paper book out of his inside pocket, and he opened it past the page with Tesha's name underlined and past the page with the list of things to do at the second basin and past the page with *plate seven, breathing, slow*, and he opened it to a fresh page, and he wrote, in the careful hand, *Levelers, week of the eleventh, nineteen districts on the list*, and under that, *the call asks for more*, and under that, *there is not more*.

He underlined *not more*.

He set the pencil down.

He sat with the book open on the bench a count, and the lamp was the lamp at the corner, and the grey at the high window was the colorless grey of a morning that had not yet decided whether it was going to rain, and Haran, at the letters, set the next one in the row.

* * *

He went to the south block at midmorning.

He went the way he had been going for a season, which was on foot, with the cheap paper book in the inside pocket and the pencil stub at the same pocket and his hands in his coat against the late-autumn cold, and the curve was the curve, and the lower terrace was the lower terrace, and at the gate of the south block he did not, this morning, stop. He went up the lane. He went past Building Six, past the chalk mark at the back wall where the second basin would go, past the heating row where Pell, even at this hour, had the cover off plate seven and his ear an inch from the iron, listening. Pell did the small lift of the chin he did. Ash did, in return, the small lift he had learned to do back. He went on.

He went to Tesha's workshop.

The door was open.

The door had been open more often since the autumn, and the small social geometry of the door had changed, in the slow way the geometry of a door changed in a district. The door, in the spring, had been Tesha's door, which had been the door of a woman doing a private work in a converted storage room. The door, in the summer, had become a door at which a neighbor stood with a thing in her hand and Tesha at the bench, and the neighbor was shown the thing. The door now, in the autumn, was a door at which more than one person stood at any given time, and the bench was rarely empty of hands besides Tesha's, and Tesha did not, on the days he came up, have the look of a woman who had been alone in the room.

She had, this morning, the look of a woman who had not been alone in the room since before he had come up the slope.

There were four people at the bench.

Tesha was at the head. The woman with the pressure valve from the week before was at her left, and the valve was no longer in the woman's hands but in the hands of a man Ash did not know, who was a man of about thirty in the patched coat of a grid worker, and the man had the valve open the way Tesha had had the valve open, and his hands were doing the small ungentle thing one did to the seat. At the woman's other elbow, watching the man, was a second woman, younger, who Ash did not know. And at the back of the bench, with her arms folded and the thoughtful unhurried standing of a person taking the room in, was a fourth.

The fourth was the one he had not seen before.

She was a woman of perhaps thirty-five, in a dark coat without a guild mark, and her hair was pulled back in the plain way a person pulled it back to keep it out of work, and at her throat was a small grey scarf knotted twice in the way the Levelers' organizers knotted a scarf when they had been at it long enough to have made the knot a small flat habit. She was not wearing the scarf, this morning, as a sign. The knot was the sign. She had her arms folded. She was watching the man with the valve. She was watching, Ash saw after a count, not the valve but the way the man's hands moved on it, and she was watching the way a person watched a thing she was, in her head, taking notes on.

Tesha looked up when he came in.

She did the small flat acknowledgment she did, which was not a smile and not a greeting but the registering of his arrival, and she said, in the flat voice she used at the bench, "Ash. This is Vanya. Vanya's at the contribution hall on the lower terrace. She come up with Renik this morning. Renik's got the valve."

"Morning," Ash said.

"Morning," Vanya said.

Vanya did not unfold her arms.

She looked at him the way she had been looking at the valve, which was with the close evaluating attention of a person who was, at any given moment, more interested in the person than in the introduction. Her eyes were dark and quick. Her voice, when it came, was lower than he had expected from a woman of her build, and the rhythm of it was the rhythm of a person who had spent a great deal of time talking to rooms with twenty people in them, which was a rhythm with a small carrying weight at the end of each sentence, the weight of a person used to making sure the back of the room had heard.

"You're the builder," she said.

"I draw," Ash said. "I don't build much, lately."

"Mm. I've heard that, too."

She did not, on saying it, look away. She did not, on saying it, smile. The look was the look of a woman who had been told a thing in a meeting and was, now, comparing the thing told against the man at the door.

"Who from," he said.

"From a few. From Renik, mostly. Renik's been coming up with the maintenance crew on the lower terrace. He's the one who said you'd been up here without paper."

"Mm."

"Said you'd been writing names down."

"Yes."

"Said you'd written his."

He looked at the man with the valve. Renik had not, at the introduction, looked up. Renik was at the seat, with his thumb on the give. He had the small absorbed look of a man who had a thing in his hands he had been wanting to have in his hands for a while.

"I didn't know his name," Ash said, "until this morning."

"You know it now."

"Yes."

Vanya nodded.

The nod was a small flat nod, like the knot at her throat, and it was, he saw after a count, the nod of a person who had taken in a piece of information in the way she had been taking in the man's hands on the valve, which was the way of a person who would, later, put the information to a use.

Tesha, at the head of the bench, did the small impatient thing she did with the rag at her belt, which was the wiping of her hands on it whether or not her hands needed the wiping, and she said, "Vanya. Show him the list."

"All right."

Vanya unfolded her arms.

She took, from the inside pocket of her coat, a folded sheet of the cheap stock the Levelers' printer used, and she opened it on the bench at Tesha's elbow, and she smoothed it with the flat of her hand, and Ash came up to the bench and stood at her shoulder.

The sheet was a list of names.

The list was in two columns. The left column was a column of names with addresses next to them. The right column was a column of skills. Some of the skills were channeling skills, and some of the skills were not. *Plate maintenance*, Ash read, on the third line. *Pressure seat, intermediate*, on the fifth. *Coordinating, spring grid, two seasons*, on the seventh. *Reads, writes, keeps the book at the contribution hall*, on the ninth.

The list was twenty-one names long.

"This is the lower terrace contribution hall," Vanya said.

"Yes."

"These are the people who came up to the hall this season. Some come for the grid. Some come because their neighbors come. Some come because the hall is warm and the soup's on and a person can sit with a person."

"Mm."

"The grid is where it is. It's down a third on the spring. It'll be down a third again by the spring after this one. The math is the math. The hall, though, is a hall. The hall is what we have when the grid is gone. The people in the hall are the people."

She tapped the list at the seventh line, the line that said *Coordinating, spring grid, two seasons*.

"I'm at the top of the list," she said.

"Mm."

"Not because I'm the best channeler. I'm not. I'm a middling channeler. I'm at the top of the list because for two seasons I've been the one who knew which family on the lower terrace had a sick child and which family had an extra pot of soup, and I matched them. That's what I'm bringing up the slope."

She looked at him.

"The grid'll go," she said. "The hall won't."

He looked at the list.

He looked at it the way he had looked at the broadsheet at the going-in place that morning, which was with the small accounting he had begun to do without meaning to, and the list was twenty-one names, and twenty-one names was a thing, and the skills column was a column with channeling at the top and not channeling at the bottom and a great deal in between, and the column was, he saw, a column he had not, in any of the drawings he had done at the long bench in the spring, drawn.

He took the cheap paper book out of his inside pocket.

He opened it to a fresh page.

He wrote, in the careful hand, *Vanya. Lower terrace contribution hall. Twenty-one names. Coordinating.*

He underlined *Vanya*. He underlined, after a count, *Coordinating*.

Vanya watched him write.

She did not say anything.

When he set the pencil down, she said, "You write people's names down."

"Yes."

"What for."

"So when the thing works, I know whose idea it was."

A count.

She did the small flat nod again.

"All right," she said.

Tesha, at the head of the bench, picked up the rag from her belt and wiped her hands on it once more, and she said, in the flat voice, "Renik. Show him the seat."

Renik looked up.

Renik was a man with a long jaw and the careful slow speech of a man who had been a slow speaker since he was a child, and he turned the valve in his hands and he set it on the bench in front of Ash, and he did not, in setting it, hurry.

"Here," he said. "And here. You feel the give."

"Yes."

"Tesha showed me. I been at the seat a week. The seat's where it goes. Not the wrap. The seat."

"Mm."

"I'll have it on by noon."

"All right."

Renik went back to the valve.

Vanya stood at the bench a count, and she did not, in the count, say more. She watched Renik. Tesha watched Renik. Ash, at the bench, with the cheap paper book in his hand and Vanya's list folded on the bench under his elbow, watched Renik, and the woman at Tesha's left and the younger woman at her elbow watched, and the workshop, in the small social geometry of a workshop with five people in it where there had been one in the spring, was the workshop it was now.

Outside, the lane went on.

Inside, the seat took.

* * *

He came down the slope at noon for tea at Tesha's stove, and on the way down he passed a thing he had not, in the morning's coming up, passed.

It was at the side of the heating row, in the small open patch where the row turned and a low stone wall ran along the inside of the curve. Three people stood at the wall. Two were residents of the south block he knew by face, and the third was a man in a dark coat without a guild mark whose face he did not know. The man was speaking. He was speaking in a low even voice, the way a person spoke when he was explaining a thing that mattered to him to two people who had not yet decided if it mattered to them, and the residents were listening, and on the wall between them, weighed at the corners with two stones, was a folded broadsheet on the cheap Leveler stock.

Ash slowed.

He did not stop.

He walked past the wall the way he walked past a door he did not need to go through, and as he passed he caught the shape of what the man was saying, which was that the contribution hall on the lower terrace had been at it two seasons, and had got the grid through the autumn last year, and had a place at the table for a person who came up to it with a pot, and the residents of the south block, the man was saying, were welcome, and the two residents were nodding the small careful nodding of people who were taking a thing in but had not, yet, decided.

Ash walked on.

He walked on the way Dorenne had walked on, in the spring, on her formal walks, which was without comment, but he walked on with the small unsettled feeling of a man who had, until this season, been the only kind of organizing happening on the slope, and who was, this morning, walking past a second kind, and the second kind was not a kind he had been asked to comment on, and the not asking was, he saw after a count, the thing.

He came down to Tesha's door.

Tesha was at the stove with the kettle.

"Tea," she said.

"Mm."

She poured. He took the cup. He stood at the bench. Vanya was no longer at the bench. Vanya, Tesha said, without looking up, had gone up to the heating row with Pell, because Pell had said the breathing on plate seven was, this morning, not breathing the way it had been breathing yesterday, and Vanya had said she had a cousin at the contribution hall who had been a smith's apprentice and who, in the spring, had named a sound at a forge that turned out to be the bellows seat, and Tesha had said that this was a thing worth bringing up, and Pell had nodded the small Pell nod, and they had gone.

"Mm," Ash said.

"She's on the slope a lot, lately."

"I saw her cousin," he said. "Up at the wall. Speaking."

"Mm."

"On the broadsheet."

"Mm."

Tesha poured a cup for herself. She drank.

"They been coming up," she said. "Two weeks now. Three. They don't ask. They stand at a wall. They put a sheet down. They speak to whoever stops."

"Mm."

"The residents listen."

"Yes."

"The residents come back here after, and they ask me a question."

"What question."

She looked at him over the rim of the cup.

"Whether they can do both."

He set the cup down.

He looked at the bench. The pressure valve was on the bench. Renik's hands were on it. Renik was at the seat. The valve was the valve it was, and the seat was the seat, and the question Tesha had answered, by the way she had answered it, which was by saying the word *both* in the flat voice without elaboration, was not a question that needed his answer.

He took the cheap paper book out.

He opened it to the page where Vanya's name was underlined.

He wrote, under it, *the residents are asking whether they can do both. Tesha says they can.*

He underlined *Tesha*.

He drank his tea.

* * *

The broadsheets that week ran a long column.

The long column was not a list. It was a piece of writing in the unornamented type, and the piece of writing was signed at the bottom *D.*, and Ash knew the *D.* the way a person knew a hand, and he read it at the bench in the lamplight after Haran had gone up to the back room, and he read it twice.

It was Davan's piece.

It was a piece on the spring grids. It was a piece written by a man who had spent four years at the spring grids, and who had built the contribution model that powered them, and who had, in the years since, been the voice of the model in the broadsheets and in the council and at the contribution halls in seven districts, and who was, now, watching the model do what the model was doing, which was thinning. The piece did not, in any of its paragraphs, name the thinning. The piece named, instead, the obstacles to redistribution, and the names of the obstacles were the names Davan had been naming for four years, and the obstacles were, on the page, the obstacles they had always been, and the call at the end of the piece was the call to double the contribution from those who still had it.

The piece was a good piece.

The piece was the piece of a man who knew his arithmetic, and whose arithmetic had, until this autumn, worked, and whose arithmetic was, this autumn, no longer working, and who was answering the failure of the arithmetic by asking the arithmetic to do more.

Ash read it twice.

He set it on the corner of the bench at the going-in place where Haran had set it.

He went to the stove.

He warmed the kettle.

He poured a cup.

He sat at the stool with the cup in his hands a count.

He thought, at the stool, about the bench paper in his inside pocket, and the names in the cheap paper book, and Vanya's list, and the man at the low stone wall, and the residents who had come back to Tesha and asked whether they could do both, and the thinned grid and the warm hall, and the column on the broadsheet at the corner of the bench, and he thought, after a count, about the man who had written the column.

He had met Davan twice.

He had met him at a hall in the second spring after the earthquake, at a meeting Ash had gone to because Haran had told him, in the *Mm* way Haran told him a thing, that he ought to go, and he had stood at the back of the hall and watched Davan speak. Davan had been a man of perhaps forty, with the broad shoulders of a man who had worked a quarry in his youth, and a voice that did not need a hall, and the small contained anger of a person who had been right about a thing for a long time and was, at last, in a room of people who agreed. Ash had not, at the back of the hall, been able to fault any of the sentences. He had not, at the back of the hall, been able to take the next step Davan was taking, which was the step of asking the people in the hall to give what they had to a grid, and to take, from those who had more, what those who had more would not give. He had not, then, known why he could not take the step. He had not, then, known what was wrong with the arithmetic.

He knew now.

He read the column once more, and the column was the column it was, and he set it down, and he drank his tea.

* * *

It was a Friday near the end of the month when he saw Davan in the lane.

The Friday was the Friday after the council had voted on Dorenne's authority, and the vote had gone the way Dorenne had said it would go, which was that the office was dissolved and the function had passed to the council, and the council voted by district, and the districts voted by pool size. The notice had run in the broadsheets that morning. Haran had set it in the row. Ash had read it at the bench. He had not, at the reading, said anything. Haran had said, after a count, "Mm."

He was walking up the lower terrace at midafternoon when he saw him.

Davan was at the corner of the lower terrace and the cross-lane that ran east toward the next quarter, and he was alone, and he was standing at the small open patch where the lower terrace turned, and he was looking up the slope toward the south block, and his hands were in the pockets of his coat, and his coat was the coat Ash had seen him in at the hall in the second spring, which was a heavy work coat with a torn cuff that had been mended and was, in the autumn, mended once again at the same place.

Davan saw him at the same count Ash saw Davan.

He did not, on seeing him, move.

He stood at the patch and watched Ash come up the cross-lane, and he watched the way a man watched a thing he had not, on the morning's setting out, expected to see, and his face was the face of a man who had been at meetings that morning and at meetings the night before, and his eyes were the eyes of a man who had not slept the way a man slept who slept.

Ash came up.

He stopped a pace from him.

Davan did not, on his coming up, take his hands out of his pockets.

He looked at Ash a count.

He looked, after the count, past Ash, up the slope toward the south block, where the heating row ran and the chalk mark at the back wall of Building Six had become, that week, the cut line for the second basin, and where the communal plate at the end of the lane sat warm under the hands of whoever the hands belonged to that hour, and where, at the side of Building Four, the door of the workshop stood open in the lane, and there were, when the wind shifted, the small carrying voices of people at a bench.

He looked at the slope.

He looked at the slope a long count.

He looked back at Ash.

"This isn't what I built," Davan said.

"No," Ash said.

A pause.

"It's what they built."

Davan did not, on his saying it, answer.

He looked at Ash a count more, and his face was the face it was, which was the face of a man who had heard the sentence and had not, in the hearing, been able to put the sentence down. He took his right hand out of his pocket. He put it, after a count, back. He turned, the small slow turn of a man who turned because turning was the thing left to do, and he walked down the cross-lane the way he had come, and he did not, walking, look back.

Ash stood at the corner.

He stood with his hands in his coat pockets and the cold of the late autumn afternoon at his face, and the slope behind him with the heating row and the chalk and the plate and the open door and the carrying voices, and the lower terrace in front of him with Davan walking down it, and he did not, for a count, move.

Davan went around the bend at the bottom of the cross-lane.

He went out of sight.

The cross-lane was empty.

A cart came up. A cart went down. A woman with a basket on her hip came across the patch, and she did not look at Ash, and she went on. The wind, when it came up the cross-lane, was the wind of late autumn, which was a wind that had thinned, in the way the wind in this city thinned by the end of the month, to the wind that ran ahead of the first hard frost.

Ash took the cheap paper book out of his inside pocket.

He opened it.

He did not, for a count, write.

He stood at the corner with the book open in his hand and the pencil in his other hand and the page blank, and he held the pencil at the page, and he did not write Davan's name.

He closed the book.

He put it back in the inside pocket against Maren's note and the bench paper and Joren's letter.

He went up the slope.

* * *

He went up because the basin was being cut.

He went the way he had gone in the morning, which was on foot, with his hands in his pockets, and he went up the slope past Building Six and around to the back wall, where the chalk mark Tesha had drawn that week had become a line scored into the dressed stone by the cutter, and the cutter was a man from the lower terrace whom Vanya had brought up two days before, who had a name Ash had written in the book under Vanya's, and the man was at the line with the small short hammer and the cold chisel he used for a clean break in dressed stone.

He was not alone.

Tesha was at his shoulder. Pell was at the line a pace down with his hand on the collar where the line came down from the ridge. Vanya was at the corner of the building with a folded sheet in her hand, and the sheet was a sheet from her contribution hall, and the names on the sheet were the names of three people from the hall who would be on the slope that afternoon. Renik was at the side of Vanya, with the pressure valve in his hands, because the pressure valve was, Renik had said the day before, the kind of thing a man kept in his hands when he was not, at any given moment, doing anything else with them.

There was a fifth person.

She was standing a pace back from the work. She was the elder woman from the day the communal plate had gone in, the woman with the grey at her temples and the mended scarf, who had, that day, said *like the kettle*. She had, this afternoon, the same scarf and the same mending. She was watching the cutter. She was watching the way a person watched a thing she had not, when the thing had first been proposed in the spring, expected to live to see done.

Ash came up.

He stopped at the corner of the building.

Tesha did not look up. Pell did not look up. Vanya looked up. Vanya did the small flat nod she did. Ash did the same nod back. The cutter set the chisel. He struck. The hammer rang. The dressed stone took the break in the small clean way dressed stone took a break when a cutter had set the chisel right, and a flake came off, and the cutter set the chisel again.

The elder woman, after the second strike, turned to Ash.

She did not, in turning, smile.

She said, "It's at the top."

"Yes."

"The basin."

"Yes."

"It should have been at the top."

"Yes."

"You been told."

"I been told."

She looked at the chisel.

"Mm," she said.

She turned back to the work.

Ash stood at the corner a count, and he did not, for the count, take the book out of his pocket. The book was in the pocket. The names were in the book. The cutter struck. The flake came off. Vanya, at the corner, said something low to Renik, and Renik said something low back, and Tesha, at the line, said, in the flat voice, *Half a finger more*, and the cutter said, *Mm*, and set the chisel again.

The work went on.

He stood at the corner a count more.

He did not, in the count, do anything else.

* * *

He came down the slope at evening.

He came down the way he had come down for a season, which was on foot, with the cheap paper book in the inside pocket and the wind at his back, and the lamps were going on at the doors, and the cooking smells came out of the doors, and the heating row at the north edge was running, and the communal plate at the end of the lane sat warm under a man's hand, and at the back of Building Six, where the cutter had been at it all afternoon, the line had become a cut, and the cut had become the start of a basin, and a length of pipe had been laid in the lane against the wall the way a length of pipe was laid before it was run.

He passed Tesha's workshop.

The lamp was lit. The door was open. The bench inside had four people at it, and Tesha was at the head, and Vanya was at her left, and Renik was at the bench with the valve, and the man whose stove had not caught at noon a month ago was at the end of the bench with a heating element in his hands, learning. Tesha was speaking. Vanya was listening. The two streams were, at the bench, the same stream.

He did not stop.

He came down to the lower lane. He came out through the gate. He walked the curve toward the workshop with his hands in his pockets and the inside pocket warm against his ribs and the empty afternoon behind him at the corner where Davan had stood, and he did not, in walking, take the book out, and he did not, in walking, take Maren's note out, and the wind was the late-autumn wind, and the city above him was the city, and behind him the south block was the south block.

He came in at the workshop.

Haran was at the long bench.

The lamp was at the corner. The forge was banked. The letters were in the row. There was a folded broadsheet at the going-in place. Haran did not, on his coming in, look up.

"South block," Haran said.

"Mm."

"Basin."

"Started."

"Mm."

A count.

"Davan."

Ash stopped at the peg.

He held his coat a count.

"Yes."

Haran did not, for a count, say anything more. He set the letter he had been reading in the row. He picked up the next. He opened the seal with the small paring knife.

"Mm," he said.

"Mm," Ash said.

Haran read the letter. The forge ticked. Ash hung the coat. He came to the bench. He sat at the stool. He took the cheap paper book out of the inside pocket. He set it on the bench. He took the pencil. He opened the book past the page where Tesha's name was underlined and past the page where Vanya's name was underlined and past the page where the list of things to do at the second basin was, and he opened it to the page where, that afternoon at the corner of the cross-lane, he had not written.

The page was blank.

He held the pencil at the page.

He did not, for a count, write.

He sat with the pencil at the page and the lamp at the corner and Haran at the long bench with the letters and the forge ticking in the small ticking of a forge cooling, and he sat with the page blank, and he did not, for the count, fill it.

He set the pencil down.

He closed the book.

He put it back in the inside pocket.

He went to the stove.

He warmed the kettle.

He poured a cup for Haran. He poured a cup for himself. He drank.

The forge ticked.

He did not, for a count more, do anything else.
