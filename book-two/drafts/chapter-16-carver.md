# Chapter 16

The letter came on a Tuesday, in the second post, and Haran was at the door when it came.

Ash had been at the bench with the small set of fittings he had laid out the night before, four of them in a row, brass and tin and the one steel cap that had begun to pit at the threads. He had not, in the morning, channeled. He had warmed the kettle with his palm a finger's width from the ring and had felt for the thread and had not found it, and he had let the kettle warm at the slope it warmed at, which was the slope of an ordinary morning's work, and he had drunk the tea, and he had gone to the bench. The folded paper was in his coat pocket. The coat was on the peg. The peg was where the peg was.

Haran took the letter at the door.

He took it and turned it in his hand the way a man turned a thing he had been waiting some time for and had not, until he saw it, known he had been waiting for. He looked at the seal. He did not break the seal at the door. He carried the letter past the bench and set it on the corner near the lamp, and he took his stool, and he did not, for a count, open it.

Ash looked up from the fittings.

Haran's hand was on the letter. The hand was loose on the letter the way a hand was loose on a thing the man holding it was deciding whether to read in front of another man or not, and Haran sat with the hand a count, and then he broke the seal.

He read.

He read at the speed Haran read, which was not fast. He read with his head bent and his thumb at the corner of the page where his thumb went when he was reading, and his face did not, in the reading, do anything. The page turned. The second page was shorter than the first. He read the second page. He set the letter on the bench and put his palm flat over it and looked at the lamp.

"Mm," he said.

Ash waited.

"Ardenmere," Haran said.

"Ardenmere."

"Three days east. Farming. It's the place I told you about in the autumn."

"The pools."

"The pools."

Haran did not, for a count, say more.

He picked the letter up and read a passage from the middle of the first page, not aloud, with his thumb on the line. He set the letter down again. He looked at Ash and did not look at him, in the way a man looked at someone when he was deciding what part of a thing to give them first.

"It's gone dark," he said.

"Dark."

"Not dimmed. Dark. Pools so weak the carriers can't keep the irrigation moving. Healing's stopped. The crystals went silent six weeks ago. The fields are coming up thin and the harvest will not, this year, be a harvest."

"How many people."

"Four hundred. Give or take."

"Four hundred."

"It's not a small place. It's a place the city sends the wheat-buyers to in summer. Or sent."

He turned the letter over.

"Read it," he said.

Ash took the letter.

The hand was a hand he did not know, large and even and pressed hard, the way a hand was when the writer had been writing at a kitchen table with the lamp guttering. The opening was a name, *Haran*, without endearment, in the way a man wrote to another man he had been writing to for thirty years.

*The wheel went last week. I'm writing from Sennet's house because the lamp at mine won't take a flame from a channeler anymore and I'm not yet good enough at striker and tinder to bother in the dark. Sennet has a striker. Sennet has bread.*

*Tovan died on the seventh. He was eighty-one and he had been holding the south irrigation since spring, and the holding was killing him, and we knew it was killing him, and we let him hold because there was nobody else. Mira tried after he went down. Mira lasted four days. The last channelers in this village are children and old women, and the old women are tired in a way I cannot put on this page, and the children do not have the pool for the work the work needs.*

*The fields in the south quarter are dust. The north quarter is alive because the stream still runs and we have been carrying buckets, all of us, the way our grandfathers' grandfathers carried buckets, and we have learned in three weeks of carrying that we are not the men our grandfathers' grandfathers were. We are softer. Our hands split. The carrying is the carrying.*

*Pell's daughter took a fever on the third. There was no one to take it out of her. She died on the fifth. I am writing this and I am not soft about it, Haran, you know I am not soft, but I want you to know what it is to bury a child you could have, ten years ago, channeled the fever out of in an afternoon. I want you to know it because I think the city does not know it. I think the city thinks dimming is a thing that happens slow.*

*It does not happen slow. It happens slow and then it happens at once.*

*Write me what you can. Send me, if you can send me, anything that is not channeling. Send me a tinkerer. Send me a book. Send me the names of the villages east of you that have done it without us, because we have heard there are villages, and we did not, when we should have, listen.*

*The crystals are silent. This goes by horse.*

*Edrin*

Ash set the letter on the bench.

He did not, for a count, speak.

He looked at the lamp on the corner of the bench. The lamp was the lamp Haran had been at when he read. The lamp was lit because Haran kept it lit in the mornings when the high windows did not yet take the sun, and the lamp burned oil, and the oil came from the press at the lower terraces, and the press ran on a wheel a man turned with his back, and the wheel had been, in his thinking, a small thing, and it was not, in this morning, a small thing.

"Edrin," he said.

"Tinkerer. He took my correspondence ten years ago when his shop was still in the city. He went out to Ardenmere because his wife's people were there, and he stayed because the work was honest, and he has been writing me twice a year since. He is not a man who writes for nothing."

"Pell's daughter."

"I don't know her. I know Pell. Pell's the man who gave us the brass for the fittings two years ago. The brass under your hand."

Ash looked at the fittings.

The brass was the brass. It had a small mark at the rim where Pell's mark went, three lines crossed, and Ash had, in the laying out of the fittings the night before, not looked at the mark, because the mark was the mark a man stopped looking at when he had laid out the fittings a hundred times.

He looked at it now.

"I'm going."

He said it the way a thing came out of him when the thing had been in him before he had gone looking for it, and Haran did not answer, and Ash heard the not-answering, and he said it again.

"I'm going to Ardenmere."

"Mm."

"I can be there in three days."

"Mm."

"Two, if I go light."

Haran picked the letter up.

He folded it on its creases. He folded it slow. He put it back into the envelope and laid the envelope on the bench at the corner near the lamp, and he set his palm flat on it, and he looked at Ash, and he did not, in the looking, do anything.

"What would you do when you got there."

"What."

"What would you do when you got there, Ash."

"I would, "

"Pour the reservoir into their fields until you left."

He said it without heat.

He said it the way Haran said a thing that was the thing, and Ash heard it the way he heard a thing that was the thing, and the tea cup that had been at his elbow was at his elbow, and his hand had not, in the saying, gone to it.

"I would, " he said.

"And then what."

"What."

"You leave. After the pour. You leave on the same horse you came on, and the fields are wet for a week, and the wheat is in the ground, and Ardenmere is what it was the day before you got there, only with a story about a man who came once."

"I, "

"What's the morning after, Ash."

He stopped.

He stopped because the sentence he had been about to make was a sentence he could not, with Haran's eyes on him, finish. He looked at the letter on the bench. He looked at the lamp. He looked at his hands, which were on the bench, which had been laid on the bench the way a man laid his hands on a bench when he was about to stand, and he had not stood.

"I don't know," he said.

"Mm."

"I haven't, "

"No."

"I haven't thought past the going."

"No."

Haran did not, in the *no*, make it a small thing. He let the *no* be the size it was, and he let Ash sit with it, and the sitting was a sitting Ash did at the bench with his hands flat and the brass under one of them and the steel under the other, and the steel was pitted, and the pitting was the pitting.

"You'd spend everything," Haran said.

"Yes."

"You'd spend it in a week."

"Yes."

"And the line."

"The line moves."

"The line ends."

"Yes."

"And then there are four hundred people in Ardenmere where there were four hundred and one, and there is a man on a horse going home, and the man on the horse going home is you, and you are not, on that horse, anything Ardenmere can use the next time the wheel breaks."

He said it slow.

He said it slow because the slowness was the way he meant Ash to hear it, and Ash heard it slow, and his hand went, finally, to the cup, and the cup was cold, and he held it.

"What do I do," he said.

"You sit."

"Sit."

"You sit, and you read what the post brings, and you wait, and you do not, this week, get on a horse."

"People are dying."

"Yes."

"I have, "

"I know."

"I have what they need."

"You have a piece of what they need for a week. You do not have what they need for a year. The man who has what they need for a year is, somewhere, a man who is not you, and the work, this week, is to find that man."

"Find."

"Through the post."

He set his hand on the envelope.

"I have correspondents," he said. "I've had correspondents for thirty years. I have not, in those years, made much of them. They were a thing I did at the bench in the evening when there was nothing at the forge and a candle was lit. They were a thing my wife, when she was alive, called my hobby. They were not a hobby. They were not, then, a network. I am not certain they are, now, a network. But they are letters, and they are men and women in places, and the places are the places that are going dark, and the men and women in them are the men and women who are, in some of those places, not going dark, and we are going to find out, this week, by post, which is which."

"This week."

"It will take longer than this week. We start this week."

"And Ardenmere."

"Ardenmere has Edrin. Edrin is writing me. I will write Edrin, today, and I will tell him, today, what I know about the villages east of him that have done what he is asking about, and I will tell him, today, the names. I will send him, by horse, by the courier the city's tinkerers use when the crystals are bad, three names. The names will reach him in five days. The names are men he can write to. The men he can write to have done the things Ardenmere is going to need to do. That is what I have for Edrin this week."

"That's, "

"That's not nothing."

"No."

"It is also not a man on a horse."

He let the *also* sit.

Ash sat on the stool with the cold cup in his hand and the brass under his other hand and the letter in its envelope at the corner of the bench, and the lamp burned, and the morning was the morning.

"All right," he said.

"Mm."

"All right."

"You'll write too. You'll write what you can. You have hands and you have a pen and you have, in the back of your skull, a year and a half of work the rest of us have not done, and you will put what is in your skull on paper, and we will send the paper where the paper goes."

"Yes."

"This week."

"This week."

Haran took his hand off the envelope.

He stood. He went to the small chest at the back of the workshop that Ash had, in the months he had lived here, never seen opened, and he opened it. The chest held paper. It held paper in stacks tied with twine, and the twine was old, and the stacks were many, and the stacks had names written on the topmost sheet in Haran's hand, and the hand was a hand thirty years deep, and the names were names of places.

Ash watched.

He watched because he had not, in his life, seen Haran open this chest, and the not-seeing had been a not-seeing he had made nothing of, and the chest was now open, and the chest was a chest of letters.

Haran lifted out a stack.

He lifted out a second.

He carried them to the bench and set them down beside the lamp.

"We start," he said, "with these."

* * *

The morning went into reading.

Ash read at the corner of the bench with the lamp pulled close and the stack at his elbow and the brass fittings pushed to the edge to make room for the paper, and Haran read at the other end with his own stack and his pen and a fresh sheet on which he was, slow, writing the letter to Edrin. The shop was quiet in the way the shop was quiet when two men were at work that did not include hammer or fire, and the only sound was the rasp of paper turned and the small dry sound of the pen.

Ash read what Haran handed him.

The first letter was three years old. The hand was small and slanted, and the writer was a woman called Bess at a place named Ifford, and Ifford was a hill country settlement that had, Bess wrote, *put in the iron wheels last spring, because the channelers were down to two, and the two were tired, and we did not, this year, want to ask them for the irrigation*. The wheels were not, Bess wrote, very good. *They break. They break in a way the channels did not break. But when they break, my husband fixes them, and my husband did not, before, know how to fix anything, and now my husband knows how to fix the wheels, and so do my husband's brothers, and so does the boy who lives across the lane, and the boy who lives across the lane is the one who is best at it, and he is twelve, and we did not, before, have a twelve-year-old boy who could fix the irrigation.*

Ash read the letter twice.

He read it the second time slow.

The slow reading was a reading he did because the first reading had passed over a thing in the letter and he had felt, after the passing, that there was a thing he had passed over, and the second reading was the going back to find it, and the thing he found was the boy. The thing was that Bess had, without making anything of it, said that the village had, in two years, made a twelve-year-old boy who could fix the wheels. The making was the thing. The making had not, in Bess's letter, been a project. The making had been what happened when the wheels broke and a boy was nearby and the boy was curious.

He set the letter down.

He took the next.

The next was from a man named Tobel at a coastal village called Mell, and Tobel wrote in a hand that was big and round and slow, the way a man wrote when he was not, by trade, a writer, and the letter was about boats. *The fleet went out last spring without channels for the first time in my grandfather's grandfather's grandfather's time, Haran, and I want to tell you we lost a boat. We lost the Helga because the wind took her sideways at the headland and the man at the tiller did not know the wind because the man at the tiller had been, his whole life, a channeler, and channelers do not, when they fish, watch the wind. They watch the channel. Now we watch the wind. We are slow at it. We are getting less slow at it. The catch is half what it was. The catch is enough for the village if the village is careful, and the village is, this year, careful. We will not, next year, lose the Helga.*

The letter was four pages.

The four pages were about sails. They were about sails the way a man wrote about sails when he had spent eighteen months relearning a craft his grandfather's grandfather had let go, and the craft was coming back to his hands the way a thing came back to a hand that had once known it through blood, and Tobel did not, in the four pages, name what he was feeling, but the feeling was in the way he wrote about the rake of a mast.

Ash set the letter down.

He sat with his hands on the bench.

He sat for a count he did not count.

"Haran."

"Mm."

"Ifford put the wheels in two years ago."

"Mm."

"Mell rebuilt the fleet eighteen months ago."

"Yes."

"Who taught them."

Haran finished the line he was writing. He did not look up.

"Ifford," he said. "A traveler. A man named Cull who had been at the bench with me in the city when we were both young, and who went to the hill country when his wife took the cough, and who learned the iron wheels off a book I sent him, and who taught Ifford the wheels because Ifford asked him to teach them. He taught for a season. He went home in the autumn. The wheels were in the ground when he went."

"And Mell."

"Tobel taught himself. Tobel had a grandfather who had fished without a channel, and the grandfather was alive, and Tobel listened. Tobel is who he is."

"And the others."

Haran set the pen down.

He looked at the stack at his elbow. He looked at the chest at the back of the room, which was still open, and which still held stacks. He looked at Ash.

"There are others."

"How many."

"I don't know."

"You don't, "

"I don't know, Ash. I have not counted. I have, in this chest, letters from forty-some places over thirty years. Not all of them are still places. Some of the writers are dead. Some of the places are Ardenmere now. Some of the places are Ifford. The ones that are Ifford did not write me about being Ifford. They wrote me about wheels, or about a fleet, or about the way they were heating their houses with a thing made of straw and clay that the youngest son of the cooper had figured out one winter. They wrote me about work."

"Forty places."

"Some forty. I have not counted."

"They were here."

"They were."

"In the chest."

"In the chest."

Ash looked at the chest.

He did not stand. He did not, in the looking, do anything. He looked at the chest the way a man looked at a room he had walked past every day for a year and was, this morning, seeing for the first time, and the seeing was a seeing in which the room had been there the whole time and he had not put his eye on it, and the not-having-put-his-eye-on-it was a thing he was sitting with.

"Why didn't you, "

"Tell you."

"Yes."

"You were building. You were, when I met you, building the south block. You were, after the south block, building the gauges. You were, after the gauges, building. I do not, when a man is building, hand him a chest of letters. I let him build."

"And now."

"Now you've stopped."

He said it without inflection.

He said it the way Haran said a thing he had observed at the bench and had decided was the case, and Ash heard it, and he heard the *now you've stopped*, and the stopped was a word that was a word he had not yet, in his thinking about himself, used.

He had not, in three weeks, started a new thing.

He had finished old work. He had walked the south block at Ryn's hours. He had sat at the bench with the fittings, and he had drawn the line on the bench paper, and he had, in the drawing, not started anything new, and Haran had, evidently, watched him not.

"Mm," Ash said.

"Read," Haran said.

He read.

* * *

The next letter was a letter from a place called Threll.

Threll was a highland settlement east of the city, three weeks' walk in summer, a month in winter, on a road that did not, after the second week, go to many other places. The hand on the letter was a young hand, careful and even, the writer a woman named Sela who had taken over her father's correspondence three years ago when her father had gone deaf and could not, after the deafness, follow the talk at the door when the post came.

*Haran, the houses are warmer this winter. We did the inner walls in the way you wrote about, with the straw thick between the timbers and the lime over the straw, and the lime cracks but the wall does not let the wind, and the houses are warm at the level a person sits in them, which is the level we have decided is the level to keep warm. We do not, this winter, heat the level a person stands at. The standing level is cold. We have learned to sit. We have moved the work to the floor.*

*The old people complain. The old people complain because the old people are cold, and because the work at the floor is hard on knees that are old, and because they remember the houses warm at the standing level, and the warmth at the standing level was the warmth a channeler made, and the channeler is my grandfather, and my grandfather has not, for two winters, been able to make the warmth at the standing level. The old people complain at my grandfather, who is older than they are, and who does not deserve the complaining, and I have learned, this winter, to take the complaining away from him and put it on me, because I can carry it and he cannot.*

*The children do not complain. The children sit on the floor and play at the floor and the children do not remember the warmth at the standing level and the children are, this winter, the warmest people in the village.*

*Tell the city, Haran, that the children are warm. The city does not know this. The city thinks dimming is a sad thing. It is a sad thing. It is also a thing the children, after, are warm in.*

Ash read the letter.

He read it twice.

The second reading was a reading in which his eye stayed on the line about the children. The line about the children was a line that was, in the structure of the letter, a small line, set at the end of a paragraph that was about the old people. The line was four sentences. The four sentences were the work of the letter. The rest of the letter was around them.

He set the letter down.

He looked at the lamp.

He did not, for a count, do anything else.

Haran was at his pen.

"Threll," Ash said.

"Mm."

"Threll did the walls."

"Yes."

"Two winters."

"Three. The first winter was the bad one. They made mistakes. The second winter the lime did not crack as much. The third is this one."

"Sela writes."

"Sela writes since her father went deaf."

"Her grandfather is the channeler."

"He is."

"He's older than the people complaining."

"Yes."

"They complain at him."

"They do."

"And Sela, "

"Sela carries it."

He said it the way Haran said a thing about a person who had, in the writing of letters to him over years, become a person he had a small clear sense of, and the small clear sense was a sense Ash did not have, because Ash had not read the letters, but he had, in the count of his reading of this one letter, begun to have the start of it.

"How old is Sela."

"Twenty-two. Twenty-three by now."

"She runs the village."

"She does not run the village. She runs her grandfather's correspondence and she has, in running the correspondence, become the person the village writes through. There is a difference."

"Is there."

"There is. The difference is that the village does not, when Sela makes a decision, do what she has decided. The village does what the village does. Sela writes me what the village does. Sela has, in the writing, become the person who knows what the village does. The knowing is a thing, in a village that is dimming, that turns out to be useful."

"Useful."

"To the villages around her."

He set the pen down.

"Threll has been writing to two other villages this last year. They sent a man last summer who taught the lime work to a place west of them. He went home before winter. The place west of them did the walls in the autumn and is, this winter, also warm at the level a person sits."

"Sela arranged that."

"Sela wrote the letter that started it."

"And you, "

"I sent the letter that told Sela the place west of her was asking. I am the post."

He said it without weight.

He said it the way a man named a thing he had been doing for thirty years and had not, in the thirty years, found grand, and Ash heard it without weight, and he sat with the not-weight, and the not-weight was a thing in him that was not the weight of his last year.

"Haran."

"Mm."

"How long has this been happening."

"Mm."

"The villages. Adapting. How long."

Haran considered.

He looked at the chest. He looked at the lamp. He looked at a place above the lamp that was the place his eye went when he was counting back through years he had not counted in some time.

"Eight years," he said. "Seriously. Things were happening before that, but the early things were small. Eight years ago Cull went to Ifford. Seven years ago Tobel rebuilt the first boat. Six years ago Threll did the first wall, badly. Five years ago there were three villages doing it. Four years ago there were eight. Last year I counted eleven that I knew of. I have not counted this year."

"Eleven."

"That I knew of. There will be more I don't know of. There will be villages that did not write me because they did not know me, or because the man who wrote to me died, or because the village did the work without telling anyone because the work was, to them, just the work."

"Eleven that you know of."

"Yes."

"And Ardenmere."

"Ardenmere did not."

"No."

"Ardenmere had Tovan. Ardenmere had Mira. Ardenmere had a strong elder generation and good land and, from time to time, a buyer up from the city who paid well for the wheat, and Ardenmere did not, in the years the writing should have started, write."

"They held."

"They held."

"They held until they couldn't."

"Yes."

"And then it went."

"In a season."

"In a season."

"The model did not bend. When the holding gave, the holding gave at every point at once. The fields, the heating, the healing, the crystals. There was no second system. There was no boy at the lane who could fix the wheel because there was no wheel. There was Tovan. Tovan died."

Ash looked at the letter.

He looked at the letter from Edrin which Haran had set, again, at the corner of the bench beside the lamp. The handwriting on the envelope was the same hand the letter had been in, large and even and pressed hard, and the address was a single line, *Haran, at the workshop, behind the fourth gate*, and the line had been written by a man at a kitchen table at Sennet's house with a striker and bread, who had buried Pell's daughter on the fifth.

He looked at the letter from Sela.

The two letters were on the bench three feet apart. They were on the bench in the same morning. They were the same kind of paper. They had come, the both of them, by horse, because the crystals were silent in one of the places and there had never been crystals to begin with in the other.

The two letters were the chapter.

He sat with that.

He sat with it in the cool of the shop, with Haran at the pen, with the lamp burning low because the morning was opening into the high windows, and the shop was light enough that the lamp was not, now, needed, and Haran had not put it out.

He sat with the recognition.

The recognition was a thing he did not, for a count, name. It was the shape of a thing. The shape was that the answer had not come from him. The answer had come, in the ordinary way an answer came, from people who were not him, in places he had not been, doing work he had not done, with no claim on any room they walked into and no name a city looked up at, because the work the work needed was not a thing a name did. The work was wheels and walls and sails and a twelve-year-old boy who was good with a wrench. The work was Sela carrying her grandfather's complaining for him so the complaining did not, in the carrying, kill him. The work was a fleet that had lost the Helga and would not, next year, lose her again.

The work was ordinary.

He had been, for a year and a half, looking for a thing that was not ordinary.

He had been looking for it at the south block. He had been looking for it at the lattice. He had been looking for it in the gauges. He had been looking for it, in his pool, at thirty-seven, give or take, in a slope he had drawn on a sheet of bench paper that was in his coat pocket. He had been looking for the answer in himself.

The answer had been in Haran's chest.

The chest had been at the back of the workshop the whole time he had lived here.

He let his breath out.

He let it out the way a man let a breath out who had been holding it without knowing, and the let-out was the let-out, and the shop was the shop, and Haran did not, at his pen, look up.

* * *

By midmorning Ash had read seven letters.

He had read Bess at Ifford and Tobel at Mell and Sela at Threll. He had read a man called Hor at a village whose name he could not, after, remember, who had taught his neighbors to bank a stove with peat in a way that held the heat overnight without channel. He had read a woman called Anet who wrote about her two daughters making a winnowing fan out of stretched hide and a wheel they had taken off a broken cart. He had read a letter from a man whose name was missing because the corner of the page had been wet at some point, and the man had written about salt. He had read a short letter from a place called Vell that was three years old, and Vell had, he understood from Haran, been Ardenmere a year ago, and Vell was now a name on the list of places that no longer wrote.

He set the seventh letter down.

He looked at the stack. The stack had, when he had started, been a stack. It was, after seven, still a stack. The stack had not, by the seventh letter, become smaller in any way the eye would have seen.

He stood up from the stool.

He went to the stove. He put his hand a finger's width from the kettle ring and warmed the kettle and felt for the thread and did not find it, and he poured the tea, and he brought a fresh cup to Haran, and he set Haran's at his elbow.

"Mm," Haran said.

"How many letters today."

"Yours, mine, and the post."

"How many."

"Eight that I'll start. Twelve that I'll finish if I'm fast. The rest tomorrow."

"Twelve."

"I have been writing letters for thirty years, Ash. I am fast at writing letters."

"Yes."

"You'll write three."

"Three."

"You'll write to Edrin. You'll write to Bess. You'll write to Sela. Edrin because he asked. Bess because she did the wheels, and she has been doing them for two years, and what she did for Ifford she may do for Ardenmere if she is willing, and we will ask her if she is willing. Sela because she is the post for the country east of her, and the country east of her is the country Ardenmere is in, and Sela will know things we do not know."

"What do I write."

"What you know."

"I don't, "

"Mm."

"Not, "

"Not the pour."

* * *

He wrote Edrin in the afternoon.

He wrote at the corner of the bench, with the lamp re-lit because the high windows had passed the angle of best light by the second hour, and Haran across from him at his own letters. The fourth start began *Edrin*.

He wrote about Bess.

He wrote about how to set the bearings so the wrong was small.

He wrote about Sela.

He wrote about straw and lime and walls. He wrote that the lime cracked the first winter and less the second and that the cracking did not, in the cracking, ruin the wall, and that a man at Ardenmere who was not a builder could, with the work of an autumn, do enough wall in his own house to know whether the wall was a thing his village wanted to do, before the village committed to doing it. He wrote that he did not, himself, know how to do the wall. He wrote that Sela would, if asked, write back about it. He wrote Sela's name and the name of her village and the road to it.

He wrote about names.

He wrote a list of names. The list was Bess and Sela and a man called Cull who was, possibly, not in Ifford anymore and might be reachable through Haran. The list was three names. The list was, he understood as he wrote it, the only thing in the letter that mattered, because Edrin could write to three names and three names were three places and three places were three ways the world had been done, and a man at Ardenmere could pick one of the three, or two, or all three, and the picking was the picking, and the picking was not Ash's.

He did not, in the letter, offer to come.

He held the pen at the line where the offer would have been. He held the pen there a count. He wrote, instead, *Haran has my pen on this. Whatever Haran sends, I will be behind. Write back to Haran with what you need that the names have not given you, and we will, between us, find what we can find.*

He signed the letter *Ash*.

He looked at the signature.

The signature was a name he had been, for a year and a half, signing on bench papers at the south block and on the gauges and on the orders Dorenne had passed him for review. The signature was a name people in the city looked up at when it came down a curve in the morning. The signature on this paper was not, he understood, going to be looked up at by Edrin or by anyone in Ardenmere, because Edrin had not asked him to come and was not going to be impressed by a name from the city, and would, in the reading of the letter, look at the names of Bess and Sela and Cull, and would write to them.

The signature was, on this paper, a small thing.

He set the pen down.

He sat with that.

* * *

He put the letter in an envelope at the end of the afternoon.

He did not seal it. Haran had said to let it sit. He put it in the envelope and laid the envelope in the corner of the bench beside the lamp, in the place Edrin's letter had been, and he looked at the two envelopes side by side, the letter that had come and the letter that would, tomorrow or the day after, go.

The shop was dimming.

The high windows had gone past the angle that took the late sun, and the lamp at the corner of the bench was, for the second time that day, the only light at the bench. Haran had, an hour ago, gone up the stair for a count Ash did not know, and Ash had, in the count, finished the letter. Haran had come back down with a piece of bread and cheese, and had eaten it standing, and had gone back to his pen.

The bell at the door rang.

It rang the small dry ring of the bell at Haran's door, which was a bell with a cracked rim that did not, when struck, make the full ring of a whole bell, and the cracked ring was the ring Haran had left it at because Haran had, some years ago, decided he liked the cracked ring, and Ash looked up.

Ryn was at the door.

She was at the door the way she was at the door, which was with her coat unbuttoned at the collar and her hair pulled back and a small loaf wrapped in cloth under her arm, and she came in without waiting for the *come in*, the way she had been, the last weeks, coming in without waiting.

"Bread," she said.

"Mm."

"Tessaly's mother. Don't ask."

She set the loaf on the bench. She looked at the stack of letters. She looked at the chest at the back of the room, which was still open, and which Ash had not, in the day, closed because Haran had not, in the day, closed it. She looked at the envelope at the corner of the bench.

She did not, for a count, ask.

She pulled the high stool from the corner and sat at the end of the bench in the place she had, the last weeks, taken to sitting in, and she looked at Ash.

"Ardenmere," she said.

"You heard."

"The schedulers heard. The schedulers always hear. There was a courier through the third gate this morning and the courier had a face on him and Tessaly asked the courier and the courier told. By midday the office knew."

"Yes."

"Four hundred."

"Yes."

"Mm."

"No."

"No."

"Show me."

He pushed Sela's letter across the bench.

"Three."

"Yes."

"Without anyone from the city."

"Mm."

She set the letter back.

"Eleven."

"Yes."

He looked at her.

He looked at her a count.

He did not, in the count, speak.

He looked at the list of names in it. The list he had not, when he wrote it, called a list. The list of three names that were not him. He looked at the chest at the back of the room. He looked at the place where his own name would have been if he had offered to come to Ardenmere and had put his name beside the offer, and the place was empty, because he had not, and the not-having was a not-having he had made, this afternoon, with his pen, in the small choice of a paragraph he had not written.

The choice had been a real choice.

It was the second real choice he had made in three weeks. The first had been the second line on the bench paper. This one was on a different scale. This one was a paragraph he had not put in a letter to a man whose daughter, three weeks ago, had been a man's daughter, and who, on the seventh, had buried his neighbor, and who was, tonight, eating Sennet's bread by the light of a striker.

He had not offered to come.

He had given names.

"Mm," he said.

Ryn looked at him.

"Mm what."

"Nothing. You said a thing."

"I did."

"It landed."

"Mm."

"Eat," she said.

"Mm."

"All right."

"Tomorrow."

"Tomorrow."

"Ash."

"Yes."

"It's a good letter."

She had not, in the count she had been at the bench, opened the envelope at the corner. She had said the thing, and the thing was a thing about the letter that was a thing about the way he had been, when she came in, sitting at the bench, and she had read the sitting, and the reading of the sitting had been the reading of the letter, in the way Ryn read things, which was sideways.

He looked at her.

"Thank you," he said.

She went out.

The door closed.

The second post came late.

It came at the hour the lamps in the lane were being lit, and the courier was a boy Haran knew by name, and the boy handed the bundle through the door and went on, and Haran took the bundle to the bench and unwrapped it and laid the letters out in the row he laid letters out in.

There were five.

Two were for the workshop's working trade and Haran set them aside. One was from a place called Hett that Ash did not know. One was from a man called Brane, whom Haran said with a small *mm*, in the way he said the name of a man whose letter he was glad to have. The fifth was a small folded note, not in an envelope, sealed with a single drop of red wax and no impression in the wax, and the hand on the front said *Ash* and nothing else.

Haran set it at his elbow.

"Yours."

Ash took it.

He knew the hand.

He had, in his life, seen the hand four times. He had seen it on a card at a dinner two summers ago. He had seen it on a list at a meeting last winter. He had seen it on a brief note left at the workshop in the autumn, asking for a meeting that had not, in the end, happened. He had seen it on the receipt for a thing he had ordered through her office, and had not noticed until later that she had signed the receipt herself.

The hand was Maren's.

He held the note a count.

He held it without opening it. The note was small. The fold was tight. The wax was the dark red of a small jar Maren kept on the corner of her desk, and the wax had been pressed by a thumb, not by a seal, and the thumbprint was visible at the edge of the drop. He looked at the print.

He broke the seal.

The note was three lines.

The first line was *Ash*. It was *Ash*, without endearment. The second line was the body. He read the body, and the body was a list of infrastructure failures at Ardenmere. It was clinical. It was clinical in the way Maren was clinical when she was naming systems, and the naming was the naming of a person who knew, exactly, what each system required, and what its failure cascaded to, and which next system would, in the cascade, fail. *Channeled irrigation: failed. Healing capacity: nil. Communication: silent six weeks. Granary heating: failed (consequences for seed stock unknown). Mill: not channel-dependent, intact. Ovens: traditional, intact. Forge: dependent on a single elder; status unconfirmed.* The list ran half the note. It was the list of a woman who had heard about Ardenmere through her professional channels and had, on hearing, sat down with a pen and named, system by system, what had broken.

The third line was not clinical.

He read it.

He read it, and his hand on the note did not move, and his other hand on the bench was open, palm down, on the brass fitting Pell had marked, and the fitting was cool under his palm, and the lamp at the corner of the bench burned, and Haran at his pen did not look up.

The third line was four words.

*This is what it looks like.*

He read it again.

He read it the way a man read a thing on a page that was a thing about the person who had written it, that had not been said before, in any of the four times he had seen the hand. The thing was that Maren had not, in writing the note, written about Ardenmere. She had written about Ardenmere for two and a half lines, and the two and a half lines were the cover, and the thing she had written was the four words at the end, which were not about Ardenmere. The four words were about her city. They were about the lattice in the upper terraces and the channeled mains and the press where Hira channeled eight hours and the south block pump where Branner stood at the handle of a system Ash had built. They were about the office Maren ran and the office Dorenne ran and the meetings and the receipts and the dark red wax.

They were about her.

This is what it looks like.

She meant: this is what is coming.

She meant: this is what is coming for me.

He held the note.

He did not, for a count, do anything.

He looked at the bench. He looked at the brass under his other hand. He looked at the letters laid out in their row, and at the envelope to Edrin in the corner with its three names, and at the chest at the back of the room with thirty years of paper in it, and at the lamp, and at Haran's hand on the pen, and the room was the room.

He folded the note.

He folded it on its single crease. He set it on the bench beside the envelope to Edrin, and the two of them were, on the bench, two pieces of paper. They were two pieces of paper from two people who had heard about Ardenmere on the same day and had, the both of them, written. Edrin had written. Maren had written. They were not the same letter. They were not for the same person. They were, on the bench, side by side.

He looked at them.

He sat with the looking a count.

The shop was quiet.

He thought, slow, the slow thinking of a man at the end of a day in which he had not, in the morning, expected the day to be the day it had become. He thought about the chest. He thought about Bess and the boy at the lane and Tobel watching the wind for the first time and Sela carrying the complaining for her grandfather and the children at Threll who were warm at the floor. He thought about the eleven. He thought about the eleven that Haran knew of, and the more that Haran did not, and the work that had been going on in a country he had not, in his pouring, looked at.

He thought about the letter to Edrin.

He thought about the names in it, and about the paragraph he had not written, and about the way the letter, when it went, would go to a man at a kitchen table at Sennet's house, who would read it by the light of a striker, who would write to Bess and to Sela and to Cull, and the writing of Edrin to Bess would be, in some month he could not yet draw on a page, the start of a thing that was not, in any part of it, his.

He thought about Maren.

He thought about the four words. He thought about the fact that she had written him, and not, in the writing, asked anything of him, because there was no thing to ask. There was no system he could pour into that would, on the morning after, be there. There was no quiet hour at the office at which he could say, *I will hold this for you*. The thing she was telling him was a thing that did not, in the telling, ask. The telling was the telling.

She was telling him because he was the person she told.

He sat with that.

He sat with it a count.

He thought about what a catalyst was.

The word was not, when it came up in him, a word he had reached for. The word came up the way a thing came up at the edge of a thought, in the space between thoughts, and he had not, for a count, looked at it, and then he looked at it. Haran had used the word once. Haran had used it last spring, in a thing he had said about a fitting, in which a small metal had made a reaction happen at a temperature the reaction would not, without the small metal, have happened at, and the small metal had not, after, been changed. The metal had been there at the start of the reaction and at the end of the reaction and the reaction had passed through it, and the metal had been the same metal, and it had let the reaction happen, and the reaction had not been the metal's reaction.

The metal had not been consumed.

The reaction had been the reaction of the things it acted on.

He looked at the chest.

He looked at the eleven places he could not, by name, list, and the more he could not name, and the names of the people at them. He looked at the boy at the lane in Ifford who was twelve and was good with a wrench and would, in some year he could not yet imagine, be a man. He looked at Sela who was twenty-two and ran her grandfather's correspondence and had made of the running a thing that joined Threll to a village west of it that was, this winter, also warm at the floor. He looked at Edrin in his kitchen at Sennet's house with the striker and the bread, who would, in five days, have three names.

The eleven had not, any of them, been waiting for him.

They had been at the work the whole time.

He had been pouring the reservoir into a city, and around the city, in the dark country he had not looked at, there had been a thing happening that was not his thing, and the thing was the thing. The thing was the work the work needed. The thing was wheels and walls and sails and a boy at a lane and a young woman who carried complaining for her grandfather. The thing did not need him. The thing needed names, and the names existed, and the names were in a chest, and the chest was at the back of the room he had been sleeping in for a year and a half.

He could not, alone, save the city.

He had spent a year and a half learning that.

He could, possibly, be the thing that joined the chest to the city. He could be the thing that took what Bess knew and wrote it where Hira could read it, and took what Sela knew and wrote it where the upper terraces could read it, and took what Tobel knew and wrote it where the lower terraces could read it, and the writing would not be his writing but the carrying of writing, and the carrying would let what had been happening in the country happen also in the city, in the way Cull had let Ifford happen, in the way the traveling tinkerer had been the thing that had let the wheels go in.

He could be the post.

He could be the post in the way Haran had been the post, only at the scale he was at, with the name he had, with the time the line gave him.

He sat with that.

He did not, for a count, name what was happening in his chest. He did not name it because he had, in the day, named enough things, and the chest did not need a name. The chest was open and the chest was not, in the opening, the relief that had come at the forge when Haran had said *you should know what your choices are*. The chest was a different kind of opening. The chest was the opening of a man who had, for a year and a half, been building, and who had, on a morning at a forge, decided he would stop building, and who had, on an afternoon of reading letters, understood that the stopping was not, in itself, the end of his work.

The stopping made room for a different work.

The different work was, possibly, smaller.

The different work was, possibly, the work the work needed.

He folded Maren's note again.

He put it in his coat pocket beside the bench paper with the two lines. He put his palm on the pocket a count, the way a man put a palm on a pocket when he wanted to know that the thing in it was, still, in it. He took his palm away.

He looked at Haran.

Haran was at the pen.

He had been at the pen the better part of the day, and he was at it now in the lamp light, and his hand on the page was the hand of a man who had been writing letters from this bench for thirty years and was, this evening, writing one of them. He was not, in the writing, performing anything. He was writing.

"Haran."

"Mm."

"How did you start."

"Start what."

"The letters. The post. The chest."

Haran finished the line. He set the pen down. He looked at Ash a count.

"I had a friend," he said. "He went to a place. He wrote me. I wrote him back. He wrote me again. After a while there were two friends. After a while there were five. After a while a friend died and there were four. After a while there were eight. I did not, in any year, decide to make a chest. The chest is where the letters went."

"That's it."

"That's it."

"Thirty years."

"Mm."

"And you didn't, "

"I didn't know it was a thing, no. It was letters. I wrote letters. The letters were, to me, what I did at the bench in the evening when there was nothing at the forge. They became a thing. They were not, when they started, a thing."

"Yes."

"You will not, Ash, decide tonight to make a chest. You will write Edrin. You will write Bess. You will write Sela. The letters will be the letters. After a while there will be more. After a while there will be a chest. The chest will not be your chest. It will be the chest the letters made."

He picked the pen up again.

"Bank the forge," he said. "I'll be at this another hour."

"Yes."

"And eat."

"Yes."

* * *

He banked the forge.

He covered the firebox the way Haran covered it. The cover settled on the brick. The warmth at the level of his ankles began, slow, to go. He stood at the forge a count and did not, in the standing, channel.

He went to the stove.

He ate a piece of bread from Tessaly's mother's loaf. He ate it standing. He did not, in the eating, sit, because the eating was a thing he was doing while the rest of him was at the bench, and the bench was where the letters were.

He went back to the bench.

He sat at the corner. He took a fresh sheet of paper. He took the pen. He drew the lamp closer.

He wrote *Bess* at the top.

He held the pen at the line under it.

He thought about the boy at the lane. He thought about what a wheel did when it failed, which Bess had not, in her letter, explained, because Bess did not, when she wrote, explain things she could see and had not put a hand on. He thought about what he, at this bench, in this city, in the lamp light of an evening, knew about wheels that Bess might, on her end, find useful.

He started to write.

He wrote slowly. He wrote badly. He wrote a paragraph that was, when he read it back, half the things he meant and half things that did not, in the saying, belong to Bess, and he set the paragraph aside, and he started again, and the second start was less bad than the first, and he wrote on.

The lamp burned.

Haran's pen scratched.

The shop was the shop, and the chest at the back of it was open, and the two letters on the corner of the bench beside the lamp were the letter from Edrin and the letter to Edrin, and the folded note in the coat on the peg was the four words from Maren that were not, in the four words, asking, and Ash sat at the bench and wrote the bad letter to Bess that would, by the end of the week, after the sitting Haran had said to do, be a less bad letter, and at the end of the month would be a good one.

He wrote the line.

He wrote the line on the inside of his thinking, where the bench paper's slope had been redrawn that morning at the forge, and he wrote it without putting the pen to it, in the way a man wrote a thing that did not, yet, belong on a page.

The line was that the answer had not, for any of this, come from him.

The line was that the work was already in the country.

The line was that he was, possibly, the small metal at the edge of a reaction that would, when the reaction happened, not be the metal's reaction.

He did not write that on the page to Bess.

He wrote about wheels.

The lamp burned.

He wrote.
