# Chapter 6

The message was on the workshop bench when Ash came in at sundown. Folded once. His mother's hand on the outside, the same hand she'd written his school slips with, a hand that hadn't changed in twenty-three years.

He stood with the door open behind him. Read it.

*Dinner Thursday. Maren is coming if work permits. Bring your appetite. Your father has been asking what a pressure tolerance is.*

He read it twice. The second time for the last sentence. He folded it back along its crease and set it on the bench beside Haran's curl of brass shaving and a drawing of a flue collar Tesha had given him for review.

Thursday was tomorrow.

He had been sleeping in the back room of the old bakery for nine nights. Eating from the district kitchen in Stonecurve, where a woman named Petra's cousin ran a pot at the south end of the square and fed the workers at midday. The reservoir sat at thirty-nine, plus or minus. The eastern run was holding. Block Three was warm. Tesha had lapped two flues that afternoon without supervision and one of them had taken the seal on the first try.

The work was good. The work was the first thing in his life that had been only good.

He thought about the apartment. About the cracked bowl on the shelf. About his mother arranging the displaced pieces of their old life on the surfaces of a new one until the rooms felt familiar.

He picked up the pencil. He found a scrap on the back of an order sheet. He wrote *coming* and folded it and set it aside for the morning runner.

He stood a moment longer at the bench. Then he turned the lamp out and walked back across the square to the room where his bedroll was, and he slept the way he had been sleeping lately, hard and dreamless, the rhythm of the day still beating in his arms.

* * *

The lift up to the post-quake terraces was working again.

It hadn't been, the week after the quake. Ash had walked the stairs then, six flights, his mother's spare key in his pocket and his hands still shaking from the morning's expenditures. Tonight the lift hummed and hauled him up the way it had been built to. Channeled. Smooth. He stepped out onto the landing and looked at the door that wasn't the door of his childhood and stood a moment composing his face.

He knocked.

His mother opened it before the second knock landed.

"Ash." She didn't say it loud. She said it the way she always had, her voice a half-step quieter when she was glad. She stepped back to let him in. "You're early."

"I'm on time."

"Early for you."

He kissed her cheek. She smelled of the same soap. The kitchen behind her smelled of stew and bread and the particular brown smell of onion taken just past translucent. The cracked bowl was on the shelf above the stove. The dented kettle sat on the back burner. The singed curtain hung at the bedroom doorway, drawn back with a piece of twine.

She had put the apartment together the way a person reassembles a song from memory. The notes weren't in the same order. But it was the song.

His father was at the table. Rendell Torren, in the cardigan with the elbow patches that Ash's mother had been patching since Ash was twelve, stood up too quickly and put a hand on the table to steady himself.

"There he is."

"Hi, Dad."

His father came around the table. The hug was short and full. The cardigan smelled of pipe smoke from a pipe his father had not owned for fifteen years; it was the cardigan that remembered, not the man. His father stepped back. Looked at him. Looked at his hands.

"You've got bench dirt on your knuckles," his father said.

"I scrubbed."

"You scrubbed where you looked." His father smiled. "There's a basin."

He went to the basin. He scrubbed his knuckles. The water came out of the tap clean and cold and steady; channeled, up here, the lattice in the wall doing what lattices did. He looked at the tap a moment too long. His mother saw him looking. She didn't say anything. She turned the heat down under the pot.

"Maren?" he said.

"Working." His mother's voice had no edge on the word, which was itself an edge. "She'll come if she can."

"She said she'd come."

"She said *if work permits*. Which is what she says."

He dried his hands on the towel by the basin. The towel was new. The hook the towel hung from was the same hook from the old apartment, the iron one his father had bent in a vise thirty years ago because the hardware shop was out of brackets. His father had carried it out of the rubble and screwed it into this wall.

He sat down at the table.

His mother put bread in front of him. Then butter. Then a glass of the pale beer his father liked, although Ash had not asked for it and his father was already drinking water. He looked at the beer.

"You'll drink it," his mother said. She wasn't looking at him. She was at the stove. "It's been in the cold box since the quake."

"I'll drink it."

"Your father can't anymore. The doctor."

"The doctor's a fussbudget," his father said.

"The doctor is correct," his mother said, and brought the pot to the table.

The stew came out steaming. Bowls. Spoons. The spoons were his grandmother's, the ones with the worn places on the handles where four generations of thumbs had pressed. His mother sat. She folded her hands. She didn't say anything that resembled a grace; the Torrens had never said grace. But she looked at the table for the breath of a moment, the way she always had, and then she picked up her spoon.

"Eat."

He ate.

* * *

His mother asked first about the project the way she always asked first about the most important thing. She didn't begin with it. She began with whether he was sleeping. Whether he was eating. Whether the apartment, the workshop, the bedroll. Whether his hands were holding up. Then, when he had answered each of those with the smallest true sentence he could make, she said:

"Tell me what you're building."

He told her.

He told her about Vell. About the cistern fill time. About the eight flues in Block Three, six of which were Tesha's and two of which were Dael's. About the insulation, the compressed fiber, Petra's man in hill country, the bales the size of a man's torso. About the heating stoves the size of a kettle that burned a pellet a chemist he had never met had developed. He talked the way he hadn't talked to her in months, maybe in a year, and as he talked he watched her listen.

She listened with fierce attention.

She listened the way she had listened when he was nine and could not channel, when other mothers had stopped listening and his mother had gone the other way, had become a forensic instrument of her son's smallest wins and losses, had remembered what he had said on a Tuesday and quoted it back to him on a Thursday so he would know that someone in the world tracked his existence at full resolution.

She listened to him now the same way.

She asked: "How many workers."

"Sixteen, full-time. Another eight rotating in for training."

"And the foreman?"

"Haran runs the workshop. I run the construction crews. There isn't a foreman in the way you mean."

"Then who decides what gets built first."

"Haran and I."

"Just the two of you."

"Just the two of us."

She put her spoon down. She rested her chin on the heel of her hand. He watched her file the fact, the way she filed everything, in whatever inner ledger she kept her son in.

"Could I help," she said.

He said yes before he had thought about it.

He said it the way you say yes to your mother when she has asked something at the edge of her courage and you have heard the edge in her voice and the answer has come up out of you before the considering machinery had a chance to start.

"Yes."

She didn't smile. She nodded once. Slowly. The nod was the receipt for the answer; she had asked, he had said yes, the transaction was logged. But her eyes stayed on him a beat longer than the nod required, and in that beat he saw her notice that he had not paused before saying yes, and he saw her note the not-pausing, and he saw her decide not to mention it.

He had no role for her. He hadn't thought about a role. He had said yes because she had asked and because the asking had been costly.

His father had been quiet at the other end of the table. He had been listening with his whole body, the way Rendell Torren always listened, the cardigan still and the spoon down and the eyes following Ash's mouth as if reading there as well as hearing.

Now he cleared his throat.

"This pipe joint," his father said. "The one you said carries the upstream pressure. That's threaded?"

"Threaded and soldered. Lap-soldered at the seam."

"Lead-tin?"

"Lead-tin."

"Hm." His father reached for the bread, tore a piece, didn't eat it. He turned the piece in his fingers as if the bread were the joint. "I patched a flue at the brewery in eighty-four. Lead-tin solder, lap seam, draft pulled through it morning and night for nine years. Held."

"Nine years is a long run on a lap."

"It's a long run on anything." His father set the bread down. "What does your draft look like. On the chimney stack."

"Strong. Haran built the stacks on the exterior."

"Brick?"

"Brick. Iron-strapped to the wall."

"Strapped how. Lag bolts or hooked into the masonry?"

"Hooked."

"Good. Bolts work loose." His father nodded once. "Old buildings cycle. Summer to winter. The wall moves a hair. A bolt'll back out a quarter turn a year and you don't notice till the strap's slack and the stack's leaning. A hook moves with the wall."

"Haran said the same thing."

"Haran would. Haran's seen it."

His father picked up his spoon again. Ate. Chewed. Swallowed.

"What about your pressure tolerance," his father said. "On the hand crank."

Ash told him.

He told him the cylinder dimensions and the stroke length and the ratio of strokes to volume. He told him the way the priming valve sat above the inlet so the cylinder couldn't dry out if a worker stopped mid-fill. He told him the lever ratio and the load on the operator's shoulder at the bottom of the upstroke. He told him about Vell, although he didn't use her name, only that there was a girl who could fill the cistern faster than a man twice her size because her shoulders moved from the hips.

"Good crank," his father said.

"Haran's design."

"Better than the brewery's. The brewery had a wheel. Wheel was a mistake."

"Why."

"Weight's wrong on a wheel. Crank lets the body do what it knows. Wheel makes the arm do what the back should." His father wiped his mouth with the back of his wrist. "Whoever designed your crank was paying attention."

"Haran was paying attention."

"Haran was paying attention," his father agreed.

For a few minutes they were two men talking about how things worked. The Wellspring wasn't in the room. Ash's mother sat between them with her chin in her hand and watched them talk and did not interrupt, although she could have. She had patched the cardigan elbow that was at this moment leaning into the table; she had bent the hook the towel hung on; she knew exactly what a pressure tolerance was. But she let his father have the conversation. She watched her husband's face fill back out the way she had been watching it fill back out for twenty-three years whenever Ash and his father found a thing to talk about that wasn't Ash.

Ash watched her watching, and felt the old guilt arrive, and did not push it away.

He was going to leave again after dinner. He was going to leave again the way he had been leaving, the form different now and the distance familiar. He saw it before he had decided it. He saw it in his mother's eye, which had already seen it, and which was, in its forensic way, building the file.

He kept eating. The stew was good.

* * *

The knock at the door came when his mother had cleared the bowls and was setting out the second pot, the small one with the dark tea his father liked.

"That'll be her," his mother said, and went to the door.

Maren came in on a draft of the cold from the corridor. She brought the corridor with her for a moment, the smell of stone and the slight metallic note of the lift's channeled cable, and then she shut the door and the smell went away and she was just his sister, in the gray coat she had owned for three years, her hair pinned up the way she pinned it for work.

She hugged their mother. She hugged their father. She came around the table to Ash.

She was thinner.

He saw it before she touched him. He saw it in the way the gray coat hung from her shoulders, which had always filled it; he saw it in the angle of the collarbone above the coat's collar, sharper than he remembered; he saw it in the cheekbone when she turned her head, the small new shadow under the eye. Not dramatic. Not a thing a stranger would see. The kind of thinning a brother sees because he had spent a childhood mapping his sister's face.

She hugged him.

The hug was the same hug. The arms were the same arms. But the arms felt lighter against his back, and he held her a half-beat longer than usual, and she let him, and when she stepped back her smile was the smile he knew.

"Sorry," she said. "I'm sorry. They held us late."

"Doing what," their mother said.

"Work."

"Doing what at work."

"Mother."

"I'm asking."

"Reservoir consult. Two of them. The second one ran long."

His mother looked at her a beat. She didn't ask the next question. She set a bowl in front of Maren and ladled stew into it from the pot she had kept warm on the back burner.

"Eat."

"I ate at the office."

"Eat again."

Maren ate.

She ate the way a person eats who has remembered, only when food was placed in front of her, that she was hungry. The first three spoonfuls went down fast. Then she slowed. Then she set the spoon down and took the cup of tea their mother had poured and held it in both hands.

"Tell me about the district," she said. To Ash.

He told her. Shorter version this time, because his mother had heard the long version and his father had heard the technical version and Maren wanted, he could see, the version that was about the project as a project, the design and the scope and the arc.

She listened. She asked sharp questions. *How are you tying the new flues into the older stacks. What's your fuel logistics look like. What's your training pipeline.* She asked the questions the way a senior channeler asks a junior channeler about a system, without condescension and without indulgence, the way two people in the same trade compared notes.

He answered her. He felt the pleasure of being asked.

"I want to see it," she said.

"Tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow. Morning."

"Come down to the square. I'll meet you at the workshop."

She nodded. She drank her tea. Their mother watched her drink it and did not, this time, say anything about how thin she was, although Ash could see her wanting to. Their father was talking again about the brewery flue, a different flue this time, and Maren turned her listening toward him with what Ash recognized as the deliberate kindness of an oldest child, and their father expanded into the listening and went on.

They stayed at the table another half hour.

Maren did most of the staying. She didn't get up when their mother started clearing. She sat with the tea cup empty in front of her and let the warmth of the room hold her in the chair. Ash watched her without looking at her. The lamp light caught the side of her face and showed him, again, the new shadow under the cheekbone.

He filed it under *Maren works too hard.*

He would not, that night, file it under anything else.

* * *

He left when the building's outer bell rang the half-hour before the lift's last shift.

His mother walked him to the door. His father stayed at the table because his hip wasn't good in the evenings. Maren stayed at the table because she had said she would help her mother with the tea things, although Ash suspected she had said it so her mother would have her for another hour.

At the door, his mother held the latch a moment without lifting it.

"Have you seen Ryn," she said.

He felt the question land. The way he had felt his father's hook-versus-lag-bolt land. A small precise weight, applied at a point chosen with care.

"Not lately."

"How long is lately."

"A while."

"Six weeks?"

"Probably."

"More than that."

He didn't answer. He could not, on a moment's count, have said how long. He had not been counting. Ryn had drifted to the edge of the project the way Ryn drifted to the edge of most rooms, present at the start and then somewhere else by the middle, and the project had filled in the space he had occupied. Ash had not noticed the filling in.

His mother looked at him.

She did not say *you're doing it again.*

She said it with the angle of her head and the way the light from the corridor caught the flat plane of her cheek and the small patient dropping of her gaze. She had said this to him before and would say it again, and the syntax of the saying without the words was a syntax she had perfected in the years he had been magicless and unreachable inside his own life.

He felt the saying land.

"I'll go see him," he said.

"Mm."

She lifted the latch. She kissed his cheek. The kiss was the kiss; she did not make it a punishment.

"Tomorrow," she said, "with Maren. Be gentle."

"Why."

"Because she's your sister."

He went out. The corridor was cold. The lift came when he called it, the cable thrumming with the channeled draw of it, and he rode down to the street alone and walked the long way back to Stonecurve through the lower city, his hands in his pockets, because he wanted the walk.

The reservoir sat at thirty-nine. The wall was there when he reached. He didn't reach.

He thought about Ryn. He thought about the last time he had seen him. He could almost remember. Almost.

He filed it.

* * *

Maren came down the south stair into Stonecurve at half past nine the next morning.

The light was the high winter light the lower city got in the second hour after sunrise, when the upper terraces had thrown their shadow across the eastern blocks and the western blocks were lit white. She stood at the foot of the stair in her gray coat and looked at the square the way a person looks at a thing she has heard described and is now adjusting the description to what she sees.

Ash met her at the basin.

"Smaller than I pictured," she said.

"Most things are."

"It's clean."

"Petra's people sweep it."

She walked the perimeter once, slowly, the way he had watched Felden walk it. She stopped at the tap. She stopped at the basin. She stopped at the workshop door and looked through it without going in. Haran was at the bench. He looked up. She nodded once. He nodded once. She moved on.

She stopped at the east square pump.

Vell was on the crank. Vell did not look up when Maren stopped at the pump. Vell was halfway through the morning fill and her count was at three hundred and something, and Vell did not break a count for a stranger.

"This is the famous pump," Maren said.

"This is Vell's pump."

"Vell's?"

"The girl. She's claimed it."

Maren watched Vell for a half-minute. Vell's shoulders moved from the hips. The crank went round. The water came up.

"Can I try it."

Ash looked at Vell. Vell did not look up. The cistern bell was a hundred strokes away. He waited.

The bell chimed. Vell stopped on the upstroke, locked the brake, stepped back. She wiped her palms on her trousers. She looked at Maren for the first time.

"The crank's heavy on the third quarter," Vell said. "Don't fight it. Let it come down."

"Thank you."

Vell stepped aside.

Maren took the crank. She set her feet. She took the first stroke.

Ash watched her.

She was strong. She had always been strong. She had outpaced him on the stair-climbs into the upper terraces when they were children; she had carried the heavier of the two grocery sacks home from the market for a decade before he had grown into it. She knew how to use her body for work. She set her feet correctly. She let her shoulders carry the load. She turned the crank.

She did the first dozen strokes well.

By the twentieth her breath had gone audible. By the thirtieth there was a flush at the side of her neck. By the fortieth her shoulders had begun to tip forward, the pelvis dropping its support, the arm taking what the back should have been giving.

She stopped at fifty.

She didn't stop because she had finished. The cistern bell was nowhere near. She stopped because she was breathing hard and the breath was at the edge of her control, and Ash watched her notice the edge and decide not to push it.

She stepped back. She kept her face composed. She wiped her palms on her trousers the way she had seen Vell do. The flush was high on both cheeks now. Her gray coat hung from her shoulders the way a coat hangs from a hook.

"It's harder than it looks," she said.

"It's the third quarter," Vell said. Vell had stepped back to the pump and was watching the crank and not Maren. "It catches everyone."

"Does it."

"It caught Corren. Corren is twice your size."

"That's kind of you to say."

"I wasn't being kind."

Ash watched Maren's face in the half-second the words landed. The face composed itself. The face had been composing itself, he saw now, for the whole of the fifty strokes; it had not so much composed itself just now as it had not yet finished composing. There was a flicker. A small bright edge of frustration, banked behind the eye, present and then not present, the way a candle in a draft will lean and right itself.

She mastered it. She smiled at Vell.

"Then thank you for the warning about the third quarter. I'll know next time."

"Mm," Vell said, and took the crank back.

Maren stepped away from the pump.

She walked with Ash toward the workshop. She didn't say anything for ten paces. She didn't say anything for twenty. He didn't fill the silence. He had learned, somewhere, not to fill his sister's silences when she was thinking; she came back faster on her own.

"Show me Block Three," she said.

He showed her Block Three.

* * *

She asked the right questions in Block Three.

She asked about the cavity work. She asked how he had bonded the plaster. She put her palm on a sealed seam and held it for the small unfocused breath that meant she was reading the work. She nodded. She didn't say anything for a while. Then she said: "Clean."

"Haran called it clean."

"Haran was being generous."

"You're not?"

"I'm being accurate." She lifted her palm off the wall. "It's clean. The fusion's tight. You used more than you needed in three or four spots. Most of it is better than I would have done at your time-served."

"That's a compliment, I think."

"It's a compliment."

She walked through the second building. She watched a training session in the front room, where Tesha was teaching a new worker how to lap a flue. She didn't interrupt. She watched for ten minutes. When Tesha glanced up she nodded at her, and Tesha nodded back, and Maren turned and walked out.

At the door she said, quietly: "She's good."

"She's the best of them."

"How long has she been doing it."

"Three weeks."

"Three weeks."

"Yes."

Maren absorbed that. Her face did not move. But she looked back through the doorway at Tesha for the duration of one mallet stroke, and then she walked on.

They walked the perimeter of the block. The day had warmed into the middle hours of pale winter light. The buildings of Block Three stood with their walls sealed and their flues lapped and their stoves burning the small contained fires that Petra's chemist had developed. Smoke rose from the chimneys in thin straight lines. The wind, when it came, did not pull the smoke sideways.

"You're going to scale this," Maren said. Not a question.

"I'm going to try."

"How many districts."

"Forty, probably. The lower half of the city."

"At what rate."

"One per quarter. If we can train fast enough."

"You can't train fast enough."

"I know."

She walked another half-block in silence.

"I want to help," she said.

He looked at her.

"How," he said.

"I don't know yet. I'll think." She put her hands in the pockets of the gray coat. "I have ideas."

"Mother said yes too."

"Mother said yes to what."

"Helping."

"Mother will help by having you over for dinner once a week. Which is not nothing."

"It's not nothing."

"It's not enough either."

"No."

They walked.

* * *

They came back to the square at midday and Ash made tea in the workshop.

The workshop kettle was a tin one Haran had brought down from The Splits, dented on one side from a fall a decade ago, the spout a hair off-true. Ash filled it from the basin. He set it on the small combustion stove in the corner, the test stove Haran had been running for tuning purposes, and he opened the damper. The flame took. The kettle began, slowly, to mumble.

Haran was at the bench. He didn't look up. He had said good morning to Maren and offered her a stool and gone back to whatever he was filing. Maren had sat on the stool. She had her coat open now, the warmth of the workshop having reached her, and her hands were folded in her lap.

"You're heating it on the combustion stove," she said.

"It's faster than waiting for the kettle hook."

"Of course."

The kettle came up to the boil. Ash poured. Two cups. The cups were enamel, chipped at the rims, the kind of cups that lived in workshops. He carried the cups to the bench. He set one in front of Maren. He sat on the upturned crate by the door with his own.

Maren wrapped her hands around the cup.

She held it a moment without drinking.

The tea was hot but not yet drinking-hot; he had not let it steep. She lifted the cup to her face. She breathed the steam. Then she set the cup down on the bench in front of her, and rested her right hand flat against its side, the way she had been doing since she was twelve years old when their father had taught her the trick of warming a cup the last increment with a touch of channeling, the way most channelers warmed tea, the way Maren had warmed her tea every day for fifteen years.

Her hand sat on the cup.

Ash drank from his own cup. The tea was hot enough. He watched her over the rim.

Her hand sat on the cup.

It sat there for the breath of a beat longer than it should have. The smallest pause. The kind of pause a man would not see if he were not sitting six feet from his sister with nothing to do but watch her drink her tea. Her brow did a thing. A small thing. A drawing-in at the inner edge, the way a person concentrating frowns without knowing they are frowning. The hand stayed flat on the enamel. The pause held. The pause held a fraction past the point at which the tea should already have been warm.

Then the warmth went into the cup.

He saw it go. He saw her shoulders ease a hair. He saw the brow release. The whole event, end to end, took perhaps two seconds; the pause inside it took perhaps a half-second; the half-second was inside a domestic gesture she had made ten thousand times.

She picked up the cup. She drank.

"Good tea," she said.

"It's the workshop tea. Haran's tea."

"It's good."

She drank again. Her face was easy now, the brow smooth, the hand around the cup steady. She talked about Block Three. She talked about scaling. She talked about the rural inquiry Felden had relayed, which Ash had told her about on the walk, and her ideas for a documentation pipeline that could send designs out without sending channelers.

She didn't mention the pause.

She talked the way she talked when she was excited about something. The reductive efficiency of her, the way she could compress a complicated thought into a sentence and a half and move on. She did all of that. She did it well. She was excited for him. She was excited for the work.

Ash listened. He drank his tea. He filed the pause under *Maren is tired*. He did not file it under anything else, because he was not, that day, a man who could afford to file it under anything else. The reservoir sat at thirty-nine. The flues were lapped. The training pipeline was three weeks old. He had a project that was working and a sister who was excited about it, and he wanted, for the first afternoon in a long time, to want only the things he was wanting.

Haran did not look up from the bench.

But the file paused on the brass at one point, for the breath of a beat, and resumed. Ash registered the pause without thinking about it. Files moved across brass. Sisters drank tea. The square held its small new sounds, the crank's pulse, the cistern bell, the woman at the basin filling a jug.

* * *

She left in the early afternoon.

She had to be back at the Hall for a consult at three. She walked with him to the south stair. At the foot of the stair she stopped and turned and looked at him.

She hugged him.

He hugged her back.

Her shoulders, under the gray coat, were thin in his arms in a way they had not been thin a season ago. He felt the shoulder blade through the wool. He felt the smaller circumference where her arm met her body. He held her a beat longer than usual. He had done this last night also. He registered that he had done it twice now and let the registering pass.

She stepped back.

"It's good work," she said.

"Thank you."

"It's better than good."

"Maren..."

"I mean it. Don't be modest. Modesty is a tic. The work is what it is."

"All right."

"I'll think about how I can help. I'll send word."

"All right."

She turned. She climbed the stair. She didn't look back, the way Maren never looked back when she was leaving; the not-looking-back was a discipline, a way of saving them both from the ritual.

He watched her go up.

Halfway up the stair she paused. Just for a step. She put her hand on the rail and rested it there for the duration of one breath, and then she went on. From below it could have been anything. It could have been a shoelace-thought, a recalculated schedule, a moment of looking up at the sky. It could have been any of those.

He watched her reach the top. He watched her turn left along the upper terrace. He watched her gray coat go small along the wall and then disappear around the corner toward the lift.

He stood at the foot of the stair another moment.

The reservoir sat at thirty-nine. It had not moved all morning. He had channeled nothing. He had drunk tea. He had walked his sister around a project. The wall was where the wall was.

He turned and walked back to the workshop.

* * *

Haran was still at the bench.

The brass fitting in front of him had a different shape than it had had at breakfast. It had been made into something. Ash didn't ask what. Haran would tell him when Haran wanted to tell him, which was usually when the thing was about to be installed in something else.

Ash sat on the upturned crate. He put his hands flat on his knees.

"Your sister," Haran said.

"Yes."

Haran filed. He didn't look up.

"She tired faster than Vell," Haran said.

"Vell is fifteen and pumps every day."

"Mm."

The file went across the brass. Ash watched it go. He listened to the rhythm of it. Three strokes, a pause, three strokes, a pause. The pause was where Haran inspected; the strokes were where Haran shaped.

"You saw the tea," Ash said. Not quite a question.

The file paused. It resumed.

"Saw it."

"What did you see."

Haran filed another three strokes. He set the file down. He picked the brass fitting up and turned it in his fingers and put it back down without doing anything to it.

"Don't ask me," Haran said.

"You always say that."

"I always mean it." Haran looked up. The look was brief and full and unembellished. "Ask her, when you're ready. Not me."

"I'm not ready."

"I know."

"She doesn't want me to ask."

"I know that too."

Haran picked up the file. The strokes resumed. Three. Pause. Three.

Ash sat on the crate for a long time. He didn't speak. The light moved across the floor of the workshop and reached the leg of the bench and started up it. Outside the door the square went on with itself. Vell's afternoon shift wasn't until four. Tesha was somewhere lapping a flue. The cistern bell rang once, distant, the upper north pump finishing its midday fill.

He thought about his mother's face at the door last night. The angle of the head. The light on the cheek. The patient gaze that meant *you're doing it again.*

He thought about Ryn. He couldn't quite picture his face. He could picture the back of his neck, walking away across a courtyard, the way Ryn was usually leaving when Ash thought of him. He couldn't remember the last conversation. He could remember the conversation before that one, which had been about nothing, which had ended with Ryn's laugh, the laugh that fell off at the end the way Ryn's laughs did, like he had remembered halfway through that he wasn't sure the joke was funny.

Six weeks. Probably more.

He stood up from the crate.

"I'm going to walk," he said.

Haran nodded without looking up.

He went out into the square.

* * *

The afternoon had lengthened. The shadow of the upper terraces had pulled back from the eastern block and the square was lit full now, white-gold winter light, the kind that warmed the stone of the basin without raising its temperature. Vell wasn't at the pump. The pump stood with its handle locked. A child Ash didn't know was sitting on the basin wall with a piece of bread and feeding crumbs to a sparrow that did not seem to need them.

He walked the long way through Stonecurve.

He didn't go up to the Splits or back to the apartment or out toward Ryn's neighborhood, although the thought of Ryn's neighborhood flickered at him and he registered the flicker and let it pass. He walked the streets of the district he had been building. He walked past Block Three with its sealed walls and its thin straight chimney smoke. He walked past the south basin where a man he half-recognized was filling a bucket and walking back toward a doorway where a woman waited with a child on her hip. He walked past the workshop door from the other side of the square and saw Haran inside through the window, head down, file moving.

He walked.

He thought about his sister's hand on the cup. The half-second pause. He had filed it under *Maren is tired*. He recognized the filing as the kind of filing his mother would have noticed him doing if she had been there, the practiced way he had of putting a thing into a drawer because the drawer was easier than the looking. He felt, distantly, that the filing was wrong. He didn't open the drawer.

He thought about his father at the table, talking about a brewery flue from 1984. The cardigan with the elbow patches. The hand that turned the bread piece in the air like the bread was the joint.

He thought about the cracked bowl on the shelf above the stove. About his mother carrying it down through six flights of stairs in the days after the quake, packed in a towel because it was already cracked and a second crack would have ended it. About her setting it on the new shelf in the new apartment and stepping back and adjusting it a quarter inch left and another quarter inch right and standing back again.

He thought about *you're doing it again.*

He came back to the square at sunset.

The light had gone gold and then orange and then into the long blue of evening. The lamps were starting along the upper terraces, the channeled lamplight steady, holding. Below, across the lower city, the lamps came on where they came on and didn't where they didn't, the irregular constellation of expenditure, each window a household choosing.

The workshop window was lit.

He stood at the basin. He put his hand flat on the stone of the rim. The stone was cool. Below the stone, in the basin, the water that had come up out of the earth by a girl's strength and stayed up because gravity held it sat in its shallow pool and reflected the lamps a little, the way water did at this hour.

He thought about his sister's shoulder blade through the wool.

He thought about the half-second.

He didn't, yet, look at it.

He stood at the basin a long time.

Then he turned and walked across the square to the workshop. The light from the window made a yellow rectangle across the flagstones. He stepped into the rectangle and through the door and into the warm small room where Haran was filing the same brass and the lamp burned crooked and the kettle Maren had drunk from sat on the bench, empty, wiped, set back in its place.

He picked up his tools.

There was a flue to lap. There was a worker to teach. There was a project. There was a reservoir at thirty-nine and a math that didn't add up and a sister climbing a stair with her hand on the rail for the duration of one breath.

He picked up his tools and went back to work.

The reservoir sat. The wall was there. He didn't reach.

Outside, in the square, the basin held its water. The chimneys of Block Three drew clean. The lamp in the workshop burned crooked. Somewhere upstairs in the upper city his sister was sitting at a table he could not see and warming a cup of tea with a hand that paused for a half-second before the warmth came, and his mother was, in the apartment with the cracked bowl, washing the last of the spoons from last night and drying them on the towel that hung from the bent iron hook her husband had carried out of the rubble.

He filed it.

He did not, that night, open the file.

He worked until the lamp guttered, and then he banked the stove, and then he walked across the square in the dark to the room where his bedroll was, and he slept the way he had been sleeping lately, hard and dreamless, the rhythm of the day still beating in his arms.
