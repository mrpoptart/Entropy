# Chapter 13

The broadsheet came in at the workshop door before the runner did.

Tesha had set it on the bench under the brass weight, the long fold, the way Haran set things he wanted Ash to read. The fold was Tesha's fold now. The brass weight was the brass weight. The headline was in the heaviest face the press kept and the headline said one word and a name.

*COLLAPSE. SERA REINHARD.*

Ash read the word standing.

He read the name standing. He read the running text once and then again, the way a man read a thing he already knew before he'd read it, because the shape of the room when he came in had told him, and Tesha at her bench had not looked up, and the not-looking-up was the saying. He read about the public accountability session at the Halver's Square platform. He read about the elder channeler, sixty-three, thirty years on the public-buildings heating runs, voluntary participation per the framework, the daily contribution multiplied for visibility, *for the photograph*, the broadsheet did not say *for the photograph* but the broadsheet said *for visibility* and the broadsheet meant *for the photograph*. He read the line about the third pull and the fourth and the fifth. He read the line about the way she had gone down. *She set her hand on the platform rail. The rail was the thing that took her down slow.* He read the line about the water and the bench and the medic from the hospital warming circuit who had been in the crowd because the medic had been on her way home.

He set the broadsheet down.

He picked it up again.

He read the second column. The second column was the Levelers' statement. The statement was careful. The statement was the kind of statement the Levelers' lawyers would have written and the Levelers' lawyers had written. *Sera Reinhard contributed voluntarily. Sera Reinhard is recovering. Sera Reinhard's contribution will heat the south bath-house through the next fortnightly cycle.* The statement did not name the platform. The statement did not name the crowd. The statement did not name the photograph.

The third column was the Hoarders' Defense League. The Hoarders' Defense League had not existed three months ago. The Hoarders' Defense League existed today. The Hoarders' Defense League had a typeface. Ash read the typeface before he read the words, because the typeface was the thing that meant the column was paid and the page was split. *They will kill her next. They will kill the elder who refuses. They will kill the elder who agrees. There is no safe ground.*

He set the broadsheet down.

Tesha was at her bench. The pump-head was open in the clamp. She was not working. Her hands were on the wood, flat, the way she had set them flat at the bench three weeks ago when she had told him she was going to the meetings. She was looking at the seal seat and not at the seal seat.

"Tesha."

"Mm."

"When."

"Yesterday afternoon. They held the press until this morning. They wanted the second-day version. The second-day version is *recovering.*"

"Where."

"Halver's Square. The platform at the south end. The one the press uses."

"And she."

"She's at home. Her sister is with her. The medic from the warming circuit walked her there. She's going to be all right. She's going to be all right in a few days."

"In a few days."

"That's what they're saying. The medic is saying a week. The Levelers are saying a few days. The press is splitting on the *recovering.*"

He sat down at his bench. He did not pick up the gear. He did not pick up the file. He put his hands on the wood the way Tesha had her hands on the wood. The wood was cold. The wood was always cold at the third hour. The wood would warm under his hands by the fourth.

"Did you go."

"To the session."

"To the session."

"No."

A beat.

"I didn't go because Mira said the session wasn't going to be a session. Mira said it was going to be a photograph."

"Mira said."

"Mira said. Mira didn't go either. Mira and I were here. We were working on the seal."

"And the people who went."

"They went."

She picked up the wooden pick. She set it down. She picked it up.

"Korel was there."

"Korel."

"Korel was there. Korel left before the fifth pull. Korel told Mira he left. Korel said he couldn't, he couldn't watch it. Korel said he walked out and he walked the long way home and he didn't go back to the office for two hours."

"Korel walked out."

"Korel walked out."

He looked at the broadsheet.

He looked at the headline. He looked at the typeface. He looked at the third column with the Hoarders' Defense League in the new face, and he thought about who paid for type, and who paid for paper, and how three months ago there had been one face on this wall and now there were three, and the cry of the boys at the corners of Halver's Square had become a phrase, and the phrase had become a market, and the market had a column inch and a typeface, and the typeface had been bought by somebody, and the somebody had a name he did not yet know.

"Tesha."

"Mm."

"Show me where they had her stand."

She looked up.

"Why."

"Because I want to know where they had her stand."

She set the pick down. She wiped her hands. She came around her bench and stood at his shoulder, and she pulled the broadsheet across, and she pointed at the small woodcut at the head of the column. The woodcut was the platform at the south end of Halver's Square. The woodcut had a figure at the rail. The figure had a hand on the rail. The figure was not Sera. The figure was the figure the press always cut, the generic platform figure the press kept on a block because the press ran the same block on a hundred stories a year. But the rail was the rail. The platform was the platform. The platform was the platform Ash had stood on at the Council convening four months ago. The platform was the platform Dorenne had asked him to stand on the day he had walked out of her office. The platform was the platform.

"There," Tesha said. She put her finger on the rail.

"There."

"There."

He looked at her finger on the rail. He looked at his own hand on the bench. The cut at the base of the thumb had healed and the line of black at the cuticle had washed off two weeks ago and there was, in its place, a small new burn at the second knuckle from a soldering iron he had set down badly on Tuesday. The burn was a clean burn. The burn would close in three days. He looked at the burn and he looked at the rail in the woodcut, and he tried to put the rail and the burn in the same room and could not.

"Tesha."

"Mm."

"Did the framework ask her."

"The framework asked her."

"Did Davan ask her."

A pause.

"I don't know."

"Did Davan know."

"I don't know, Ash."

"Tesha."

"I don't know. I don't know what he knew. I know the session was on the calendar. I know the calendar was at the office. I know Korel was there. I don't know if Davan was there. I don't know what he saw. I don't, I don't know, Ash."

He looked at her.

She had a smear of pump-grease at the corner of her jaw the way she had had a smear of pump-grease at the corner of her jaw three weeks ago when she had asked him *why can't we do both.* He had not answered the question then. He had told her he would work on it. He had worked on it. He had not finished. He looked at the smear and at the broadsheet and at his own hands on the bench, and he thought about the question, and the question turned in him and came up against the rail in the woodcut and stopped.

He stood.

"Where are you going," she said.

"To see her."

"Sera."

"Sera."

"You don't know her."

"No."

"Ash."

"I know."

"She might not want a Wellspring at her door today."

"I know."

"All right."

He took his coat down. He went to the basin. He washed his hands. He dried them on his front.

"Tesha."

"Mm."

"If Haran comes in."

"I'll tell him."

"Tell him I went the long way."

"All right."

He went out.

* * *

Sera Reinhard's building was at the corner of Carver Lane and the canal turn.

He knew the building the way he knew most buildings in the lower city, which was the way he knew anything that had been on a list. The Sixth Ward list had been posted three weeks ago. The Sixth Ward list had had twelve names and twelve addresses, and the address at Carver Lane and the canal turn had been on it, and the name at the address had not been Sera Reinhard, because Sera Reinhard had given under the ordinance on the first day of the cycle, and Sera Reinhard had not been a hoarder, and Sera Reinhard had not been on any list the press had set in the heaviest face. Sera Reinhard had been on the other side of the page. Sera Reinhard had been the name in the small italic credit at the foot of the column where the bath-house warming notice ran. *Public-buildings heating: contribution by S. Reinhard and three others, fortnight of the 14th.*

He had read the credit and not read it. Now he had read it.

He went up.

The stair smelled of the canal in the way the lower-city stairs smelled of the canal in the third quarter, which was brine and char and the wet-stone smell under the cobbles. The runner on the second tread had been worn through and someone, recently, had set down a strip of oiled cloth, the new oiled cloth Ash had seen at the Open Hand and at three other doorways in the lower city in the last month. The cloth was a sign. He read it. He did not name what he read.

A woman opened the door at the third floor.

She was in her fifties. She had Sera's mouth. She had not Sera's mouth, she had a mouth that was Sera's mouth at sixty minus seven years. She had a cloth over her shoulder and a cup in one hand and the cup was cold tea. She looked at him and she did not know him and then she did, because everyone in the lower city now knew the Wellspring's face, and the knowing went across her face in the small way it went across the face of a person who had not expected the face at her door.

"You."

"My name is Ash."

"I know your name."

"I don't want to come in. I won't come in. I wanted to, I wanted to ask if she's all right."

"She's asleep."

"All right."

"She's going to be all right. The medic was here this morning. She's going to need a week. Maybe ten days. Then she'll be where she was."

"Where she was."

"Where she was a year ago. Not where she was last week."

He held that.

"I'm sorry," he said.

"For what."

"For coming to your door. For asking. For, I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry at my door, Wellspring. Be sorry where it costs you."

She did not say it cruel. She said it the way a woman said a sentence she had been holding in her mouth all morning waiting for a face to say it to, and his face had come to her door, and she had said it, and she watched it land, and she did not soften it or sharpen it after she said it. She drank from the cold tea. She held the cup in both hands.

"Will you tell her."

"Tell her what."

"That I came."

"Why."

He could not answer. He looked at the cloth on her shoulder. He looked at the cup. He looked at the runner on the second tread, the new oiled cloth, the sign he had read on the way up.

"You don't know," she said.

"No."

"All right. I won't tell her, then. I won't tell her something you don't know the reason for. She doesn't need a reason at her door this week. She has enough."

"All right."

"Wellspring."

"Yes."

"My sister gave for thirty years. Thirty years, the public buildings, the warming runs. She gave because she wanted to. She gave because the Hall asked nicely and she liked being asked nicely. She gave because the bath-house had her mother in it on Tuesdays and her mother liked the heat in February. She gave for thirty years and nobody put her on a platform for it. Nobody put her on a wall. Nobody asked her to pull until she went down. The Hall asked her like a neighbor and she gave like a neighbor and that was thirty years."

"I know."

"This year they asked her like a witness."

"I know."

"There's a difference."

"There is."

"Don't forget it."

"No."

She closed the door.

He stood at the door a moment. He put his hand flat on the wood, not pressing, just the flat of the palm on the wood, the way a man set a hand on a thing he was not going to open. He took the hand back. He went down.

At the second tread he stopped. He looked at the oiled cloth. The cloth was new. The cloth had been set down by someone who had paid for cloth and oil and the labor of cutting it to the tread, and the someone had a name, and the name was at the Open Hand or near it, and the cloth was here because Sera Reinhard's stair was a stair the lower city now climbed to, and the lower city climbing to a stair was a thing that needed cloth, because the press would come, and the press came on a tread, and a tread that complained was a tread the press wrote about.

He went on down.

He went out into the canal turn. The afternoon was grey. The lamps had not been lit. He walked.

* * *

The broadsheet wall at Halver's Square had three faces on it now.

The new face was the Hoarders' Defense League. The old face was the press. The middle face was the Levelers. The wall had been one face a year ago. It had been two for six months. It was three today. The three faces ran in three columns and the columns crowded each other at the seams, and the seams were where the people stood, because the people stood where the columns met, and read across, and the reading-across was the thing the wall now did that it had not done before.

He stood at the seam between the press and the Hoarders' Defense League.

The press version had the headline COLLAPSE in the heaviest face. The Hoarders' Defense League had a headline of its own. *MURDER BY VOLUNTEER.* The face was new. The face had been bought. He looked at the typeface and he tried to find a printer he knew and could not. Three months ago he had known every face on this wall. Today he did not.

A man at his elbow said, to no one:

"They'll say she volunteered."

"Mm."

"They'll say it tomorrow and the day after."

"Mm."

"And next month."

"Mm."

The man was talking to himself. The man was not talking to Ash. Ash listened anyway. The man was old. The man had a coat that had been mended at the cuff with a thread that was almost the right color and was not quite. The man stood at the wall and read the column and shook his head and did not move on. He was not reading. He was standing.

A woman across the seam, on the Levelers' column, said to her companion:

"She's recovering. She's recovering, Ren. Read it."

"I read it."

"She's recovering."

"I read it."

"Then say it."

"She's recovering."

"Don't say it like that."

"How do you want me to say it."

"Like you mean it."

He did not say it again. He looked at the column. He looked at the woodcut. He looked at the rail. The woman next to him put her hand on his elbow and held it there and did not move it and did not look at him and did not say *good*. She said nothing. They stood at the wall the way the old man at Ash's elbow stood at the wall, which was the way a person stood at a wall when the wall had taken a thing the person did not have a place to put.

Ash did not stay long.

He turned at the seam and walked west, away from the platform, past the press-yards, past the corner where the boys had called *hoarder rolls* the morning of the first list and where two boys today were calling something different. He listened. The cry was new. The cry was *Sera recovers. Sera recovers. Hoarders' rolls, fresh names, Sera recovers.* The boys were calling all three because all three sold, and the boys did not know the difference, and the coin came in for the cry, and the cry kept on.

He walked the long way to the workshop.

He did not go to the workshop.

At the corner of Stonecurve he turned the wrong way. He turned uphill. He had not turned uphill from this corner since the break. He turned uphill now because the route to the cooperage was uphill from this corner, and the cooperage was where he was going, and he had not decided to go to the cooperage and he was going to the cooperage, and Haran's voice in his head said *decide on the stair after* and he said back, in his own head, *I am.*

* * *

The Open Hand on the second floor above the cooperage had a third table now.

The third table had been a fourth bench at the workshop. The fourth bench had been the bench Davan had used for the soldering. The bench was here. The bench was a table now, set against the east wall under the window where the afternoon light came in at a useful angle. There were two men at the table, sealing letters with the new green wax.

Liss was at the south table.

Liss looked up and did not stand.

"He's in the back."

"Alone."

"With Korel."

"I'll wait."

"You don't need to."

"I'll wait."

She looked at him. She set the pencil down. She looked at him the way a person looked at a face she had decided about. She did not move.

"Ash."

"Mm."

"Sit down."

He sat. He sat at the chair against the wall near the door. The chair was new. The chair had been at a kitchen, once, and was at the wall now. He sat. He waited.

The back-room door opened.

Korel came out first. Korel did not look at Ash. Korel went past him to the south table and set a stack of paper down at Liss's elbow and went to the iron plate with the wick and poured a cup of tea. Korel's hand was not steady on the kettle. The wick was a wick. The wick was a draw. The wick had been wick three weeks ago. The wick was still wick. Ash noted it.

Davan came out behind him.

Davan was in the heavier coat. He had not taken it off. He had been out, or he was going out, and he had not yet decided. He saw Ash. He stopped at the door of the back room.

"Ash."

"Davan."

"Come back."

Ash stood.

He went back.

The back room had not changed. The desk. The chair behind the desk. The chair in front of the desk. The chair against the wall. Three crates of broadsheets at the wall, bound in twine. The pot of tea on the iron plate. The wick. The not-draw.

Davan sat behind the desk.

He sat behind it the way he had sat behind it three weeks ago, which was the conscious sitting of a man who knew the desk was new. The consciousness was no less. The desk was no newer. Ash registered the no-changing of the consciousness. He took the chair.

"You read it," Davan said.

"This morning."

"All right."

"Davan."

"Ash."

"She collapsed."

A beat.

"She did."

"Your people watched her collapse and they didn't stop it."

He said it the way Haran had taught him to say a thing, which was at the volume the thing required and not above it. He did not raise his voice. He did not lean forward. He set the sentence on the desk between them the way Dorenne set a survey on her desk between two people who had to look at it.

Davan put his hands flat on the wood.

"She was contributing to a community project. Voluntarily. People overextend themselves at work every day."

"She was performing for a crowd that would have called her a hoarder if she'd stopped. That's not voluntary. That's coercion with a civic face."

The two sentences sat on the desk.

Davan did not move. The room did not move. The wick on the iron plate did the small clean thing wicks did, which was burn, and the burn was a sound at the edge of the silence, and the silence was the silence.

Davan looked at him.

Davan's face did the thing it had done the morning at the bench when Davan had told him about the girl in the harbor quarter. The thing was not a softening. The thing was the face setting itself for a sentence the face had been holding in its mouth for a length of time longer than Ash had been in the room.

"What did you expect?"

"Davan."

"Ash, what did you actually expect?"

"I'm asking you about Sera."

"You have an ocean of power. She had a cup. She can refill it tomorrow."

"She can refill it in ten days."

"In ten days. Yes. Your reservoir never regenerates and you're spending it on plumbing. Who's being more reckless with their power?"

The line did its work.

Ash watched it do its work. He watched the line cross the desk and find the place in him it was going to find, which was the place under the rib on the left side where Dorenne's line had landed three weeks ago, and Dorenne's line had landed in the same place, and the place was a small place and the lines were finding it.

"This isn't about me."

"It's always about you."

"Davan."

"It's *always* about you, Ash. You're the most powerful person alive. Every choice you make is a choice nobody else can make. Every choice you don't make is the choice you made. I'm not asking you about Sera. Sera is fine. Sera will be fine. I'm asking you about every child who's cold tonight because the Wellspring is building toys."

"They aren't toys."

"They aren't toys to you."

"They aren't toys."

"To the child who's cold, Ash. To the child. The pump in Building Six runs at eighty percent. The child in the building next door doesn't have a pump. The child has a wall. The wall is cold. The wall has been cold for three winters. You have an answer to the wall and you are not using the answer because you have decided that the answer is the wrong shape, and the wall is the wall."

"The answer breaks the next winter."

"The wall doesn't last to the next winter, Ash."

A pause.

He said it quietly:

"You're asking the drowning to be patient while you decide which lifeboat to build."

The line landed.

Ash heard it land. He heard it land the way he had heard Dorenne's line land. He sat with it. He did not move. He looked at Davan's hands on the desk and Davan did not move them. The wick burned. The kettle on the iron plate did not whistle. Nothing in the room did anything.

He waited until he could trust the sound that was going to come out of his mouth.

"Davan."

"Mm."

"I came here to ask you about Sera."

"I know."

"You answered about me."

"I know."

"That isn't an answer."

"It's the answer I have."

"Then you don't have an answer."

"No."

"Davan."

"Mm."

"Did you know."

"Did I know what."

"That she would be on that platform. That the framework had her in for the photograph. That the ones who'd been giving for thirty years were the ones the lawyers were going to put in the column inch this week."

A long beat.

"I knew the calendar."

"That isn't the question."

"I knew what the calendar meant."

"Davan."

"I knew, Ash. I knew the photograph was the strategy. I didn't know she was going to go down. I didn't, I didn't think any of them would go down. I thought the photograph was the photograph. I thought the elders we asked were the elders who would give the photograph and walk off the platform and go home and have tea. That's what I thought."

"Korel walked out at the fifth pull."

"Korel did."

"You weren't there."

"I wasn't there."

"Why."

"Because I had a meeting at the Hall."

"At the Hall."

"On the upper threshold. On the conversation that becomes the conversation if this cycle holds. I had a meeting and I went to the meeting and I was at the meeting when the platform happened and I came back to the office at the seventh hour and Korel was not at his desk and Liss told me where Korel had gone and what had happened, and I sat where I am sitting now and I drafted the statement, and the statement was the statement you read this morning."

"You drafted the *recovering.*"

"I drafted the *recovering.*"

"Davan."

"Yes."

"Did the statement say you weren't there."

"No."

"Why."

"Because the statement was not the place to say that."

"What is the place to say that."

"This is the place. Right now. I'm telling you. I'm not telling the press."

The wick burned.

Ash sat with it.

He was not angry. He had walked uphill from Stonecurve and at the corner of the cooperage he had been angry, and he had carried the anger up the stair and it had been hot at the top, and he had brought it into the back room, and he had set it on the desk with the first sentence, and Davan had taken the sentence and not flinched, and Davan had said *what did you expect,* and the anger had gone, and what was under the anger was not anger. It was the thing he had no word for that was the shape of a man who had walked up a stair to a friend and had found a friend, and the friend was the same friend, and the friend was also a man who had drafted a *recovering* he had not seen, and both of those were Davan, and the room held both.

What he had under the rib was not anger. It was grief.

He did not name it. He did not move his hands. He did not look away.

"Davan."

"Ash."

"I don't know what to do with this."

"I know."

"You know."

"I know you don't, Ash. I haven't known what to do with a single day of this for four years. I've made the next move. That's all I do. I make the next move and I see if the next move was the right move on the day after, and most days it was, and some days it wasn't, and yesterday it wasn't. Yesterday the next move wasn't the right move. I'm telling you that. I'm not going to tell the press that. I'm telling you."

"Why."

"Because you came up the stair."

"That isn't a reason."

"It's the reason I have."

He looked at Davan.

He looked at Davan and he saw the man at the workbench in the second month, and the man at the cooperage in the third, and the man behind the desk three weeks ago, and the man behind the desk today, and the four men were the same man, and the same man was different from the man Ash had walked up the stair to find. He had walked up the stair to find a man who would hear the sentence and break, and the man had not broken, and the man had not pretended he had not heard, and the man had said *yesterday wasn't the right move,* and the man had drafted the *recovering,* and both were true, and the both was the thing.

"I'm not going to come back," Ash said.

"I know."

"I'm not, I'm not your enemy."

"I know."

"I think you're, I think you're going to do this without me. I think you're going to keep doing it. I think the next photograph is going to be a photograph you didn't draft and you'll draft the *recovering* on it too, and the *recovering* will hold for a fortnight, and then there'll be a third, and you'll draft that too. I think you know that."

"I know that."

"And you'll do it anyway."

"I will."

"Why."

"Because I haven't found the line, Ash. You asked me three weeks ago to show you the line. I haven't found it. I've found ground I can stand on for another month. I'll stand on it. I'll find ground for the month after. I'll stand on that. If I find the line, I'll stand at it. I haven't found it."

"That isn't enough."

"I know."

"Davan."

"Mm."

"It isn't enough."

"I know, Ash."

He stood.

Davan did not stand. Davan kept his hands flat on the desk, the way Davan had kept his hands flat on the desk three weeks ago, and the position was the position. The desk was the difference. The desk was now also the time. Three weeks of desk. The desk had three weeks of wear at the place where Davan put his hands. Ash had not noticed it on the way in. He noticed it on the way out.

At the door he turned.

"Davan."

"Ash."

"Sera's sister told me to be sorry where it costs me."

"What does it cost you."

He thought about it.

"This," he said.

"I know."

"All right."

"Ash."

"Mm."

"It costs me too."

"I know."

He went out.

Liss did not look up. The two men at the third table did not look up. Korel was at the iron plate. Korel had finished his tea. Korel set the cup down. Korel looked at him. Korel did not say *you all right.* Korel nodded the small nod a man nodded who had walked out at the fifth pull and had not gone back to the office for two hours and had no sentence in him for any face that was at this stair today.

Ash nodded back.

He went down the stair. He stepped over the second tread where the new oiled cloth was set. He went out into the cooperage yard, and the afternoon was grey, and the lamps along Stonecurve below him were beginning to come on, lit one by one by Sun who walked the curve at the fifth hour, and the lamps were the lamps, and he stood in the cooperage yard with one hand on the post of the gate and waited for the deciding to come.

It did not come.

What came was the cold of the post under his palm, and the small ache at the second knuckle where the burn from Tuesday had not yet closed, and the picture of Davan's hands on the desk and the wear where Davan had put his hands for three weeks. He stood until his hand on the post went numb. He took the hand off the post. He went down.

* * *

He took the long way home.

He took it because he could not walk the short way past Haran's door yet, the way he had not been able to three weeks ago, and the not-being-able was the same not-being-able, and that was a thing to notice and he noticed it. He took the route along the canal. The canal had a thin scum at the edge where the cold had come in and the water had not yet decided whether it was going to freeze. The water at the lower lock was running fast. He stopped at the lock. He watched.

The lock was old. The lock had been here when he was nine. The lock had run on a draw at the upper gate, a small clean coordinated draw two channelers had done at the dawn watch every day for sixty years, and the draw was gone now because the channelers were gone now, both of them, one to age and one to the second winter, and the draw was a pump now, an iron pump set in the housing, hand-cranked, run by a lock-keeper who was not at his post at this hour because the cycle had run at the fourth hour and the next cycle was at the dawn watch.

The pump was Haran's design.

The pump was running.

The pump was running at, he watched the discharge at the lower gate, the pump was running at eighty percent. Eighty was the new number. Eighty was the number the eastern row pump ran at. Eighty was the number Building Six pump ran at since the seal had failed and Tesha had put the new seal in and the seal had held but had not held at full. Eighty was not a hundred. Eighty was running.

He stood at the lock.

He thought:

*The heating grid works now.*

The Levelers' grid. The communal heating grid that had gone in two weeks ago at the corner of Carver Lane and the canal turn, which was the corner he had stood at this morning, which was the corner under Sera Reinhard's window. The grid worked. The grid was warm. The grid had heat at the wall at the third floor at the second winter for the first winter in six. The grid drew on the framework contribution. The grid drew on Sera Reinhard. The grid drew on the eleven others on the credit at the foot of the column. The grid was warm tonight. The grid would be warm tomorrow.

The grid would be warm for as long as Sera Reinhard could pull.

*Building Six pump at eighty percent works long-term.*

The pump did not need Sera. The pump did not need anyone to pull. The pump needed a seal once a year and a seat re-cut every three and a hand-crank at the dawn watch when the cistern came below the line. The pump worked at eighty. The pump would work at eighty next winter and the winter after. The pump would work in twenty years. The pump would work when Sera was not pulling. The pump would work when Ash was not pulling.

The pump did not warm a wall.

The pump moved water.

He stood at the lock and held the two truths in his hands the way a man held two stones of different weight and tried to feel which was heavier and could not, because both were heavy, and the heaviness was the thing.

The grid was warm tonight.

The pump would be warm in twenty years.

The grid would not last past Sera.

The pump did not warm anyone tonight.

Both true. Both insufficient.

He let the stones sit.

He did not put them down. He did not pick one up. He stood at the lock with the iron pump running at eighty percent in the housing, and he listened to the discharge, and he thought about the wall at the third floor at the second winter, and the woman at the cloth stall who had said *Branner,* and the boys at the corner calling *Sera recovers,* and the woodcut of the rail, and Davan's hands on the desk, and Dorenne's hand on the pencil that did not lift, and Tesha's question, *why can't we do both,* and his own answer that was not an answer. *Take your time. Not too much time.*

The reservoir sat in him at the level it had sat for a month. He had not channeled in three weeks. He had drawn once at the workshop on Friday to seal a joint Haran had asked him to seal, and the draw had been small, and the level had not noticeably moved, and the level was thirty-seven, the level was thirty-seven the way the pump was eighty, the way the grid was warm tonight, the way Sera was recovering, and the numbers were all numbers, and the numbers did not add to anything that was not also an absence.

Thirty-seven percent of an ocean.

A cup that did not refill.

He watched the discharge.

The lock-keeper's lamp was lit at the small shed at the upper gate. The lock-keeper was not in the shed. The lamp was lit because Sun had lit it on the way past, the way Sun lit every lamp along the curve at the fifth hour whether the keeper was in or not, because the lamps were the lamps and the keeper would come at the dawn watch and the lamp would be there.

The lamp was a small clean thing.

He looked at the lamp. He looked at the pump. He looked at the canal where the scum at the edge had not yet decided.

He went home along the canal.

Haran was at his bench. Tesha was at hers. The pump-head was closed in the clamp. The seal seat was clean. Haran was working a length of soft wire in his fingers, the way Haran worked when Haran was thinking with his hands and did not know what the hands were thinking.

Ash hung his coat.

He went to the basin. He washed his hands. He dried them on his front.

He came to the bench.

Haran did not look up. Tesha did not look up. The not-looking-up was the saying. The saying was: *we waited. We are still waiting. We are not asking.*

He sat.

He picked up the gear. He picked up the file. He set the gear in the small clamp at the corner of the bench. He looked at the next tooth and at the angle the eye did not want, and then at the angle the eye did not want from the other side. The burr was small. The burr would come off in two passes. "Mm."

"The lock pump is running at eighty."

"It is."

"It does."

He looked at Tesha, who had not looked up, who was working a small brass fitting at her bench, who had asked him three weeks ago why they couldn't do both, and who was waiting, the way she had said she would wait, with her hands on a fitting she was making fit.

Sun would have walked the curve and gone back up to the next quarter to light the next set. The lock pump at the canal would have run its fourth-hour cycle and would not run again until the dawn watch.

Sera Reinhard would be asleep at her sister's. The sister would be in the chair by the bed with the cup of cold tea and the cloth on her shoulder. The medic from the warming circuit would have come by in the evening and would come by in the morning.

Both true.

Both insufficient.

He filed.
