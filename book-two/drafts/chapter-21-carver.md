# Chapter 21

The workshop door was already open when Ash got there.

He saw the lamp before he saw Haran. Just the one lamp, low, set on the long bench. The forge was cold. The shutters were still latched. The light was the small kind a man uses when he does not want to wake a room.

Haran sat with his back to the door. A letter lay open on the bench in front of him, weighted at one corner by a flat-headed punch. He was reading it the way he read difficult work: slowly, and a second time.

Ash set his coat on the hook. He did not say good morning. Haran did not turn.

After a while Haran said, "Vesselton. And a place I haven't heard of. Reedmoss."

"Gone dark."

"Last week. The runner came through Hallow Cross yesterday and waited for a southbound cart." He pushed the letter an inch toward Ash without turning. "Read it."

Ash read it. The hand was careful, schooled, slow. A woman's hand. She had begun the letter twice. The first beginning was crossed out. *We have done what we could.* The second beginning was the one she had let stand. *I am writing to let you know.*

The mechanical pieces had been almost finished. A windlass on the well. A flue rebuild that needed only the cap. A grain mill whose stone was waiting on a shipment of dressing tools that had not arrived. The channeler in Vesselton had held the well for nine months past her safe range. She had stopped two weeks ago. Reedmoss had no channeler of its own and had been borrowing one from a neighbor village on a rotation that had failed twice and then a third time.

The decline had not collapsed them. The decline had simply outrun them.

Ash put the letter back on the bench. He set the punch on the corner where it had been.

"How many that makes," he said.

"Counting these. Eleven."

"Since the new year."

"Since the new year."

Haran put his hand flat on the bench beside the letter. He did not move it for some time.

"They almost made it," Ash said.

"They almost made it."

The lamp guttered and steadied. Outside, somewhere up the lane, a cart wheel struck a paving stone and the horse blew once and went on.

"It works," Haran said. "What we're sending. The plans. The drawings. The training round. It works. They were doing it right."

"Yes."

"They just ran out of time."

Ash did not answer. There was nothing to answer with. After a while he went to the shutters and unlatched them, one and then the other, and the gray morning came into the room in flat panels and laid itself across the floor.

He did not light the forge.

He stood at the bench and looked at the letter without picking it up again. The reservoir sat in him quiet and unstirred and lower than it had been a month ago. He noticed the lowness without reaching for it. It was simply there now, the way the bench was there.

"Tea," Haran said, eventually.

"I'll make it."

He made it.

They drank it standing.

\* \* \*

Tesha came in before the second bell.

She came in fast, the way she came in when something was wrong, and then she stopped just inside the door because Haran was still at the bench with the letter, and she read the room before she spoke. She had learned to do that in the last three months. Ash watched her do it and thought, briefly, of how she had been in the autumn, the girl who entered every room as if it were the same room.

"Bad news," she said.

"Yes."

She did not ask what. She waited.

"Two more places," Haran said. "Hill country."

"Adapting?"

"Adapting."

"Oh," she said.

She set her satchel down on the floor by the door, very carefully, as if the floor needed not to be jarred. Then she straightened and pushed her hair behind her ear with the back of her wrist, which was the gesture she used when she was trying to find her place again.

"I have a thing," she said. "It can wait."

"Tell us."

"It can wait, Ash."

"Tell us anyway."

She came to the bench. She did not sit. She put her hands flat on the wood the way Haran had, and she looked at the letter without reading it, and then she looked up.

"Brackmoor's copying us," she said.

Ash waited.

"The block north of Six. They sent two men over last week to look at the heating in Building Six. I thought they were inspectors. They were not inspectors." Her mouth did the small quick thing it did when she was annoyed at herself. "They were men from Brackmoor. They went home and built it."

"Built it how."

"Built it wrong."

She let that sit.

"They put the hybrid loop in," she said, "but they put it in flat. They didn't pitch it. The drain-back doesn't drain back. They've got standing water in the lower coil and the channeler on it has been holding it warm to keep it from freezing because it's too cold up there at night still, and she can't keep that up, and when she stops the coil's going to crack. I'd give it nine days."

"Did you tell them."

"I told them this morning. I told a man named Pettren who is, as far as I can tell, the person they decided was in charge after they decided to do it themselves. He listened. He understood maybe a third. He said could I come and show them. I said yes. Then on the walk back I started counting."

"Counting what."

"Blocks." She looked at him. "Brackmoor copied Six. There are people in Linden Row asking after Brackmoor. There's a foreman in the Stoneworks ward who came to one of my Tuesday sessions and stopped coming three weeks ago and I assumed he'd lost interest and what I think now is he went home and started building. I think there are six places doing it. I think by midsummer there will be twenty."

She stopped.

"That's good," Haran said, carefully.

"It would be good," Tesha said, "if any of them knew what they were doing."

She picked at a splinter on the edge of the bench. Did not pull it free.

"They've got the shape," she said. "They watched the men in Six work and they got the shape. They don't have the principle. They don't know why the pitch matters. They don't know that the coil has to be the alloy because if it's straight iron the channeler has to work twice as hard for the same throw and she'll burn down inside a season. They saw the visible parts. They built the visible parts. The invisible parts are what's going to kill it."

"Train them," Haran said.

"I know."

"Train them, Tesha."

"I know, Haran. I'm one person. I have eleven on Tuesdays. I'd need eleven on Tuesdays and eleven on Wednesdays and eleven on Thursdays and a person in Brackmoor who isn't me, and we don't have a person in Brackmoor who isn't me." She looked at Ash, finally. Not for a solution. To be seen. "It works, what we built. That's the bad part. It works well enough that people are copying it before they understand it, and the copies are going to fail, and when the copies fail people are going to say the thing doesn't work."

Ash watched her.

He thought about saying *we'll find a way*. He thought about saying *you've done well*. Both of those were sentences a man said to close a conversation. He kept his mouth shut.

After a moment she said, "Sorry. I didn't sleep."

"You should sit down."

"I shouldn't. I have to be in Brackmoor by ten."

"Sit down for five minutes."

She sat down for five minutes. She did not drink the tea he poured for her but she held it in both hands and looked at the lamp.

"Make me a list," he said.

"A list."

"The blocks. The names of the men in each. What they got right. What they got wrong. Bring it back tonight."

"For what."

"So we can see it."

"Seeing it isn't training them."

"I know that. Bring it anyway."

She nodded. She set the cup down half full. She stood. At the door she stopped, and turned, and looked at the letter on the bench.

"Vesselton was the one with the windlass," she said.

"Yes."

"Damn," she said, quietly, to no one in particular, and went out.

The door swung. Haran reached out without looking and steadied it before it banged.

\* \* \*

Ryn came in at the back of the morning.

She came in the way she always came in lately, which was tired and unhurried and as if she had been thinking on the walk and had not finished thinking. She had a cloth bag over one shoulder. She set it on the floor and did not take her coat off.

"Tell me you've eaten something," she said.

"Tea."

"Tea is not eating something."

"There's bread on the shelf."

"I saw the bread on the shelf. It's the same bread that was on the shelf yesterday."

She went to the shelf. She broke a piece off and brought it back and put it in his hand. He ate it. It was the same bread.

She did not sit.

"Davan spoke last night," she said.

He chewed. He did not speak. He waited for the rest.

"In the lower yard at the foundry. They'd cleared a space. Not many people. Maybe a hundred. He stood on a crate."

"Crowd."

"Smaller than the winter ones. By a lot."

"And."

She looked at the floor for a moment, deciding how to say it. He had watched her do this with his news a hundred times in the last year. He had not, until lately, recognized that it was a gift she gave him, the choosing.

"He's not saying redistribute anymore," she said. "He's saying we have to be honest about what's coming. That was the phrase. He said it three times. *Honest about what's coming.*"

"Honest meaning."

"Honest meaning the hill country. He talked about the hill country for ten minutes. He had numbers. I don't know if the numbers were right but he had them. He named places. He named Vesselton."

Ash put the bread down.

"He named Vesselton."

"He had the news before we did. Or at the same time. There's a runner who comes through the foundry yard. He had it."

"Go on."

"He said the building project, ours, yours, was a good idea badly scaled. He said the redistribution argument had been a good idea badly timed. He said both of those were last year's arguments. He said this year the argument is whether we're going to lie to people about whether they're going to be cold next winter or whether we're going to tell them and let them decide what to do with that."

"Decide what to do."

"He didn't say. That was the part he didn't say."

Ash put his palms on the bench. The wood was warm where the lamp had been over it for an hour. He moved the lamp.

"He named me," he said. He could feel her wanting to come to it.

"Yes."

"What did he say."

"He said," she said, and she stopped, and then she said it cleanly, the way she said hard things, "*The Wellspring chose to build water pumps while the world goes dark. History will judge the scale of that miscalculation.*"

The room held that for a moment.

Haran, at the bench, did not look up.

"All right," Ash said.

"All right?"

"What else is there to say to it."

"Nothing, probably." She watched him. "I wasn't bringing it because I thought it would hurt you. I brought it because I wanted you to hear it before someone told you wrong."

"I know."

"Are you all right."

"Yes."

She watched him a beat longer. He did not look away. She seemed to decide something and let it go.

"It's not the line that worries me," she said.

"No."

"It's his face."

"Tell me."

"He looked," she said, slowly, "the way my father looked the year he stopped going out to find work. He looked like a man who'd found the thing that explained everything to him and was relieved to have found it. My father had it too. It wasn't a good year."

She said it without heat. She was not making a speech. She was telling him a thing she had seen, the way she told him about a cracked flue or a child with a cough.

"Davan's frightened," she said. "He's frightened and he's tired and he's found a story that lets him be both of those without having to do anything anymore except say the story. That's what I saw."

"You think he's done."

"I think the Davan who was going to fix this is done. I don't know who's going to be standing where he was."

She picked up her bag.

"I have to go," she said. "I told a woman in the lower terrace I'd be there at one. Her boy keeps not getting better."

"Come back tonight."

"I was going to."

She paused at the door. Did not say what she paused to say. Went out.

\* \* \*

He went down to the district after midmorning.

He went on foot. He took the long way, not because he wanted the walk but because the long way ran past the old viaduct and he liked to look at it. The viaduct had been dry for two years. They had run a small line down the side of it last fall, mechanical, a hand-pumped feeder for the lower courts. It was still there. A boy was working the handle while a woman filled three jugs in a row, and the boy was working the handle the way boys work handles, which is to say badly, and the woman was telling him, without turning, to put his back into it.

He went on.

The pump in Building Six was running.

He stood across the alley from it for a while before he went any closer. The relocation had been done in the autumn, after the second freeze, by two of the men he had trained and one he had not. They had pulled it off the original wall and braced it on a frame they had built themselves out of salvage and put it in the courtyard where everyone could get at it. The frame was ugly. The bracing was ugly. There was a strap around the upper housing that should not have been needed and that had been put there because the men who built the frame had gotten the angle wrong on the bolts and rather than redo the frame they had strapped it. The strap was working. The pump was running.

A woman was at it now. She filled a kettle, set it down, filled a basin, picked up the kettle and the basin both and went away. Another woman came up behind her and started filling a bucket. She did not wait. She did not look around for permission. She filled the bucket and went.

He had not channeled in this courtyard in three weeks.

He stood a long time and watched.

After a while a man crossed the courtyard with a younger man at his elbow. The older man was one Ash knew. Hessel. He had been in the third training round, in the wet end of winter. He had been slow to learn and stubborn about the slow. The younger man Ash did not know.

Hessel was talking. He was pointing at the housing, at the strap, at the linkage where the handle joined the shaft. The younger man was nodding. Hessel made him put his hand on the strap and feel where the strap met the housing. He made him watch the linkage move when the next woman came up to pump. He said something Ash could not hear and the younger man laughed, a short reluctant laugh, and shook his head, and Hessel grinned and clapped him on the shoulder and walked away.

The younger man stayed and watched the linkage for another minute on his own.

Ash did not go over.

He went up to the heating room on the second floor of Building Six, which was unlocked because they had taken the lock off in the autumn so the maintenance men could come in at any hour. The hybrid coil was running. The pitch was right. The drain-back was draining. The channeler on duty was a woman named Ferrin who had not been a channeler before this winter and who had taken on the holding work two days a week because no one else in her stair would. She nodded to him and did not stop what she was doing.

"Warm enough," he said.

"Warm enough for now."

"Anything off."

"The upper return knocks at the start of every cycle. I think it's air. Gerten says he'll bleed it on Saturday."

"All right."

He stood beside her for another minute. She did not need him to. He left.

In the courtyard, on the way out, he saw Hessel again. Hessel saw him. Hessel did the small nod a man does when he sees another man he respects and does not want to interrupt, and went on with what he was doing, which was showing two more men a thing about the strap.

Ash went out under the arch.

The neighboring block was a five-minute walk. He almost did not go. He went.

Brackmoor had a courtyard too. The courtyard was empty. There was nobody at the pump. There was a pump. It had been built off a drawing and the drawing had been a good drawing but the men who built it had set the foot-block on bare earth instead of stone, and the foot-block had subsided through the winter, and the alignment had gone, and the pump moved when you worked it, and at some point the people in the courtyard had stopped working it and gone back to carrying water from the lower fountain four streets over.

He stood by the pump and put his hand on the housing. It was cold. He could feel the misalignment without working the handle. The shaft sat half a degree off true, and it would seize inside a month if it had not seized already.

He looked up at the windows.

Two of them had cloth nailed across the inside of the glass. The cloth was gray and old and wadded and it was there because the heating room on the third floor of this building was not warm enough to keep the upper rooms from losing through the windows, and the people who lived in those rooms had done the only thing they could do, which was put cloth in the gap.

The upper return on Brackmoor's coil knocked. He could hear it from the courtyard.

He did not go in.

He stood in the courtyard a little longer than he needed to and then he turned and went back the way he had come.

On the walk home he passed the boy at the viaduct pump again. The boy was still working the handle wrong. The woman had gone. There was a different woman now, with a smaller jug, and the boy was still working the handle wrong, and the woman was waiting, and the water was coming up because the pump was a good pump and because someone had built it right.

\* \* \*

The message from Dorenne came in the middle of the afternoon.

A boy brought it. Not one of the regular runners. A boy in a clean coat who had clearly been told to deliver it into Ash's hand and had clearly been told what to say if the hand was not available, which meant he had been told twice, which meant Dorenne had been particular.

Ash took it at the workshop door.

The seal was the small one, not the formal one. He noticed that before he noticed the paper. The small seal was the one she used for things that had not gone through the office.

He took it inside. He did not open it at the bench. He took it to the back, behind the bellows, and stood with his shoulder against the wall, and opened it there.

The hand was hers. The phrasing was the office's. She had written it in the voice of a department, which was the voice she used when she was saying something she did not want to have to repeat in person.

She wrote that the vote had taken her infrastructure brief. That the brief had been distributed across three subcommittees, none of which would carry it competently. That this was no longer a question to be argued. That there remained, however, certain administrative resources nominally still under her authority. The ledger of regional supply contracts. The logistics network for the southern haul. The internal records of three years of channeler placement, with notes. The standing relationship with the freight collective at the river yard. The list of which inspectors were trustworthy and which were not. She named them. She named the inspectors.

She wrote that she was prepared to redirect these resources to support the current building work. She used the word *current*. She did not use the word *your*. She did not use the word *partnership*. She wrote that she would expect no operational role and would accept oversight from whatever body the project chose to constitute. She wrote that the offer was not contingent on a response by any particular date, but that the resources were perishable, in the sense that they were perishable in her hands.

The last paragraph was one sentence.

*I would rather the work continue badly than not continue at all.*

It was not signed with her full name. It was signed with the initial.

He stood with the letter against the wall behind the bellows for some time.

He had not expected it. He had not, in fact, ever quite let himself believe it would come. He had built, in the last six months, a small private picture of Dorenne that allowed him to keep working without having to think about her, and the picture had her as a woman who would defend her authority to the last and would, when she lost it, withdraw entirely and become a private grief in a private room. The picture had been useful. The picture had been wrong.

The bitterness in the letter was real. It was in every sentence. It was in the choice not to use his name and in the choice to sign with the initial and in the careful, dry, official phrasing of a woman who would not pretend that this was not a defeat. It was a defeat. She had written it as a defeat.

The offer was real too.

He read it twice more. He folded it. He put it in the inside pocket of his coat, on the side where the note from Maren had been until last night.

He went back to the front of the workshop.

Haran was sharpening a chisel.

"From whom," he said, without looking up.

"Dorenne."

"Hm."

Ash sat on the stool by the door. He did not take the letter out again.

"She's giving me the office," he said.

"All of it."

"What's left of it."

Haran turned the chisel over. "What's left of it is more than you have."

"I know."

"And."

"I don't know."

Haran set the chisel down.

"You don't trust her."

"I don't know how to."

"That's not the same."

"No."

Haran looked at him for a moment. Then he picked the chisel up again and went on sharpening it.

"Think about it tonight," he said. "Don't think about it now."

Ash did not answer.

He sat on the stool a while longer. Outside, the light had started to change. It was the long part of an April afternoon, the part that did not yet feel like evening but had stopped feeling like day. He watched the square of it on the floor. He watched it move, very slowly, toward the leg of the workbench.

\* \* \*

The forge had been lit, eventually, in the middle of the afternoon. Haran had lit it for a small job and had banked it once the job was done and now it was throwing the kind of heat a forge throws at its lowest setting, which was almost nothing and which was also enough.

Ash and Haran sat at the bench.

Tesha had come and gone. She had brought the list. The list had eleven names on it. Six blocks. Two foremen who would, she thought, be useful if she could spend three days each with them. She had eaten standing up and gone home to sleep before she fell over.

Ryn had come back. She had not taken her coat off. She was sitting on the crate by the door, leaning against the frame, one knee up, watching the lamp.

The letter from Dorenne was on the bench. Ash had taken it out of his pocket and put it down. He had not read it again. Haran had read it. Ryn had read it. Nobody had said anything for some time.

Ash had told them about the rural letter, when Ryn first came in. He had told them about Brackmoor. He had told them, last, about Davan.

Then he had stopped talking, because there was nothing else to say. The day was on the bench in pieces and the pieces did not assemble.

Haran sat with his hands loose on his knees. He had the look he had when he was working out how a mechanism wanted to move, except that the mechanism today was not on the bench.

After a long silence he said, "You're not going to solve all of this."

"I know."

"You're not going to solve most of it."

"I know that too."

Haran reached out without looking and picked up the small gear mechanism Tesha had left there earlier, the spring assembly, the one she had brought to show him. He turned it once in his hand. He looked at it as if it were a thing he had not seen before, although he had handled a thousand like it. He set it back down on the bench.

"Good," he said. "That's a start."

Nothing else for a while.

The forge ticked as it cooled.

Ryn shifted on the crate. The leather of her coat made the small sound it made.

"I should go," she said.

She did not stand up immediately. She sat with the knee up another minute, looking at the lamp. Then she stood and put her bag on her shoulder.

Ash got up to walk her to the door.

At the door she stopped. She put her hand against the frame the way a person does when they are about to say something and want to know whether they are going to say it. Then she took her hand off the frame.

He waited.

"How," he said, finally, because she was not going to begin without him.

"How what."

"How am I supposed to," he said, "be. With all of it."

She thought about it. She did not answer quickly. She never answered quickly when the question mattered to her. The lamp was behind him and her face was half in the light from it and half in the dark from the open door.

"Like someone," she said, "who's going to lose something and knows it and isn't running."

He nodded once.

She looked at him a moment longer. She did not touch him. She did not say anything else. She went out, and the door closed behind her, and her steps went down the lane and faded into the larger sound of the lane.

He stood at the door a little while.

Then he went back to the bench.

\* \* \*

Haran had gone to the back to wash his hands.

Ash sat on the stool at the workbench. The same stool. The same bench. The light from the banked forge was the same light that had been in this room a year ago and two years ago and ten. He put his palms on the wood and felt the wood, which was warm in the place under the lamp and cool everywhere else, and old, and worn smooth in the two places where two men had worked at it for thirty years and a third had worked at it for half a dozen.

Haran's question, the one he had asked in this room a year ago, was still in the room. *What do you want to build.* It had not been answered. It had stopped being a question that needed an answer in a single sentence. It had become a question that was answered by the doing.

He picked up Tesha's spring assembly.

She had left it on the bench when she came back in the evening. She had wanted him to look at it. She had built it to do a thing his original mechanism had done, the small return-tension piece that sat under the heating-loop valve and reset the valve when the cycle ended. His original had used a single coil tempered hard. Hers used two coils stacked, softer steel, with a spacer between them. His had been a clean piece. Elegant. He had been proud of it. Hers was not elegant. It was clumsy. The spacer was a piece of strap brass that had been cut by hand and the cut was not square. The two coils were not perfectly matched.

Hers would last twice as long as his.

He had seen it the moment she put it down. The single tempered coil had been the right answer for the load he had imagined. It had been wrong for the load that turned out to exist, which had a small high-frequency chatter she had measured in three different installations and he had not measured anywhere because he had not thought to measure for it. Her two-coil stack damped the chatter. The clumsy spacer was clumsy because it had to be hand-cut to the exact thickness of a particular installation, and the thickness was different in each one, and there was no clean way to make a piece that had to be different every time.

Clumsy where his would have been elegant. Better where his would have been wrong.

He turned it in his hand. He set it down. He picked it up again.

He thought, briefly and without grief, that there were going to be a great many things in the next ten years that other people built better than he had built them. He thought about that without flinching from it. It was a true thing about the world. He set the spring back down on the bench.

The reservoir sat in him.

He noticed it because he had stopped noticing it for stretches in the last month, and noticing it again was a thing he did now the way a man notices the weight of a pack he has been carrying long enough to forget. It was lighter than it had been a year ago. It was finite. It had a bottom.

He could feel the bottom.

It was not a number. He could not have said how much was left. He could have said only that the place where the stored thing ended was closer to where he stood than it had been, and that he could feel the closeness the way one feels the floor below a thinning rug. The thing he had once thought infinite was thinning. It had been thinning for a year. It would go on thinning. There was a day, somewhere, that he could not see but could feel, on which he would draw and there would be nothing to draw.

The fear came up.

It was the old fear, the one from the stairwell, the one from the bowl in his mother's hand, the one he had carried since he was a child and had named the Wellspring and learned what the name meant. *Ordinary means worthless. Powerless means purposeless.* The fear had not gone away in the last year. It had not been argued with. It had not been beaten. It was simply still there, sitting where it had always sat, in the same place in his chest, breathing.

He let it be there.

There was something else there too. He could not name it. He tried, sitting at the bench, and the names he reached for did not fit. Not peace; the day had been too large for peace, and the rural letter was still on the bench, and Davan was still on a crate in a foundry yard somewhere in the city, and Brackmoor's pump was cold. Not resolve; he had been resolved before, in this same room, and the resolve had not held under the first real weight. Something quieter. Something that did not announce itself.

A willingness.

The willingness to keep building knowing it would not be enough. The willingness to become ordinary knowing the becoming would frighten him every day for the rest of his life. The willingness to set the work down at the end of the night and let other people pick it up in the morning, and to trust, without being able to verify, that the people in Six and the people in the hill country and Tesha in her makeshift back-room workshop with her hand-cut brass spacers would carry what he could not carry himself.

The fear was there. The willingness was there. They sat in him at the same time, and neither of them won, and he understood, sitting on the stool, that neither of them was going to win, and that this was the shape of the thing now.

He put his hand on the spring assembly one more time.

He left it on the bench.

\* \* \*

Haran came back from the wash. He looked at Ash and did not ask. He went to the forge and banked it the rest of the way, turning the damper down by hand and pulling the last of the embers in toward the center with the long iron, and then he set the iron in its bracket and wiped his hands on the rag at his belt.

"Lock up when you go," he said.

"I will."

"Don't sit here all night."

"I won't."

Haran put on his coat. At the door he stopped. He did not turn around.

"It was a long day," he said.

"Yes."

"Tomorrow's another one."

"I know."

He went.

Ash sat at the bench a few minutes more. Then he stood, and put on his coat, and put the letter from Dorenne back in the inside pocket. He looked at the bench once before he went. The lamp was out. The forge was banked. Tesha's spring assembly sat where he had left it, a small clumsy thing in the dim, waiting for the morning.

He locked the door behind him.

\* \* \*

He walked home the long way.

It was full dark. The lamps were lit along the upper terrace and not lit along the lower one, and he went down the lane between them, and the wind had come up a little, and there was the smell on it of woodsmoke from the blocks where the heating was running and the colder smell from the blocks where it was not.

He went past the courtyard of a building he did not know where two men were working a hand-pump in the dark, taking turns, one pumping while the other held a lantern. He went past a bakery whose ovens he could feel through the wall as he passed, the warmth of them pressed against the stone. He went past a stair-end where a child sat with her back against the wall and her arms around her knees and a cloth wrapped twice around her shoulders, waiting for someone, and a woman came out of the door above her as he passed and called her name and the child got up and went.

He went past a wall.

The wall was one he had passed a hundred times. It was the long blind wall of a warehouse on the slope where the lower terrace turned. People wrote on it. They had been writing on it for a year. The Levelers had painted their symbol there in the autumn, the circle with the line through it, large, in white. Someone had since scratched the symbol out. The white was scarred across with deep dry strokes from a piece of stone or a nail.

Beneath the scratched-out circle, in chalk, in a hand that was not careful and not careless, someone had written two words.

*WHAT NOW?*

The chalk was fresh. It had not yet been smudged by a passing shoulder or a hand.

He stood in front of it.

He did not have an answer. The city did not have an answer. The question was the right question and it was the question he had been carrying all day in the workshop and on the walk to Brackmoor and in the message from Dorenne and in Ryn's face at the door and in Haran's hand on the gear and in the rural letter weighted under the punch.

The wind moved along the wall.

The reservoir was almost gone.

He kept walking.
