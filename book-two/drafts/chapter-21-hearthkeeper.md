# Chapter 21

The workshop door was already open when he came up the lane, and Haran was at the bench by the back window with a letter in his hand and his reading spectacles pushed up onto the dome of his forehead.

The forge was cold. Haran had not lit it.

That was the first thing Ash noticed, before the letter, before the spectacles, before the way Haran had not turned around at the sound of the latch. The forge was cold. It was past seven. In ten years of mornings in this workshop Ash had not, that he could remember, walked in to a cold forge after seven.

"Morning," he said.

"Mm."

He set his coat on the peg. He set his bag on the bench by the door. He looked at the back of Haran's head and at the letter and at the cold forge, and he did not say anything else, because Haran was reading, and reading, with Haran, was a thing you did not interrupt.

He went and lit the forge himself.

The kindling caught on the second strike. He fed it slowly, the way Haran had taught him, the small careful additions that let a fire decide on itself before you asked anything of it. He did not channel. He had not channelled in this workshop for a thing as small as kindling in months now. The match took. The wood took. He stood for a moment with his hand on the iron lip of the forge and felt the first thread of heat come up off the kindling against the inside of his wrist, and he was glad of it the way a person was glad of an ordinary thing.

When he turned around Haran had set the letter down.

"Two more," Haran said.

"Where."

"Tilney's Reach. And the small one above it, what was the name."

"Cresston."

"Cresston. Yes."

Haran did not say anything else for a moment. He took the spectacles off his forehead and folded them and set them on the letter on the bench. He looked at the spectacles as if he had not seen them before.

"Not like Ardenmere," he said. "Quieter than that."

"How."

"They were doing the work. Tilney had a wind rig half up. They had a fellow down from the Reach who'd been over to see Goss in the spring. They were building the catchment at Cresston with the stone they had. They were doing the work, Ash. They simply, they ran out of time."

Ash did not move. He had his hand on the lip of the forge still and the heat was coming up against his wrist and he held it there.

"Tilney had the wind rig half up," he said.

"Half up."

"When."

"He doesn't say when. It went dark Eighth Day. The rig was half up Sevenday."

"One day."

"One day."

The forge had begun to make its soft inner noise, the small breathy sound a fire made when it was deciding to be a fire. Haran turned on the stool and looked at it. He did not look at Ash. He looked at the fire as if the fire were the letter.

"It's getting faster," he said.

"I know."

"The shape of it is changing. It isn't, it isn't a curve any more. It's a step."

"I know."

"They were doing the work."

"I know."

Haran picked up the letter again. He folded it. He folded it again. He set it on the bench under the spectacles and put the small iron weight on top of both, the way he had set every letter from the rural networks for the last six months, in the same spot, under the same weight, where a man could find a thing if he needed to find a thing.

"I'll write him back this evening," Haran said.

"All right."

"There isn't anything to write."

"No."

"I'll write it anyway."

* * *

Tesha came up the lane at nine.

He heard her before he saw her. She had a particular footstep on gravel, brisk and a little uneven, the gait of a woman who had been on her feet since before light and was not planning to sit down before dark. She had a roll of something under her arm and a satchel over the other shoulder and a face on her that he had begun, in the last month, to recognize as the face she brought when a thing had gone right and wrong at the same time and she did not know yet which of the two she was here to talk about.

"Ash."

"Tesha."

"Have you got ten minutes."

"I've got the morning."

"Ten will do."

She did not sit. She set the roll on the bench and unrolled it with the heel of her hand, and it was a sketch, and it was not her sketch. He saw that before he saw anything else. The hand was different. The line was heavier. The proportions were off in a way that someone who had been at the work for years would not get off, and he leaned over it and looked.

"This is from the next block over," she said. "Halder Row."

"They drew this."

"They drew it. They built it. They put it in last week. Three rooms on the top floor of the corner tenement. They came over to ours twice, asked Yula and Beni a few questions, looked at the sleeve on the southern riser, went home and built one."

"And."

"And it doesn't work."

He looked at the drawing. He looked at where his eye went on the drawing, which was the joint at the upper coupling, the place where the mechanical pre-heat handed over into the pooled channelling. The joint on the drawing was wrong. It was wrong in a way that was not careless. It was wrong in the way of a person who had seen the outside of a thing and reproduced the outside of the thing without ever having had his hands on the inside.

"The coupling," he said.

"The coupling. Yes. They put the sleeve flush. They didn't, they didn't bevel. They didn't know about the bevel."

"Why didn't."

"Because you can't see the bevel from outside the wall, Ash. You can see the sleeve. They saw the sleeve. They built the sleeve. They didn't know there was a bevel under the sleeve because nobody told them there was a bevel under the sleeve, because we never wrote down that there was a bevel under the sleeve, because we have not written down anything, because we have been too busy doing it to write any of it down."

She stopped. She put her hands on the bench on either side of the sketch and looked at it. Her hair had come out of its tie at some point in the walk over and she had not noticed.

"How bad," he said.

"Two of the rooms are cold. The third one is warm because the channel cycle they're running is, I think, fifty per cent over what they should be running, and they don't know it's fifty per cent over because they don't know what it should be. They are burning their pool to keep one room warm and thinking they have built the system. They have not built the system. They have built the picture of the system."

"Who's running their pool."

"Two channelers. One of them is a girl of seventeen."

He was quiet a moment.

"Tesha."

"What."

"How many people came to look at ours last month."

She did not answer immediately. She did the small quick count he had seen her do before, the count of a person who kept records in her head because there had not been time to keep them anywhere else.

"From other blocks."

"From other blocks."

"I don't know. Twenty. Thirty. I stopped counting after the fortnight."

"And how many are building."

"I don't know that either. Halder I know about because Beni's cousin lives there. The one on Old Cooper Street I heard about second-hand. There may be others. There almost certainly are others. Ash, I cannot, I cannot get to all of them. I cannot go and look and tell them about the bevel. I cannot teach a block of strangers in a morning what we taught Yula in two months. There aren't enough hours and there aren't enough of me."

"Right."

"And every block that builds it wrong is, is. It's worse than not building it. They're burning a pool to keep one room warm. They are using more, do you understand, they are using more, not less, and they think they have done the thing."

"I understand."

"What do we do."

She was looking at him. She was looking at him the way she had begun to look at him in the last month, which was not the way she had looked at him a year ago. A year ago she had looked at him like the answer. Now she looked at him like a partner she was going to argue with about the answer because there was not, between them, any answer yet, and the not-answer was something they were going to have to make together.

He looked at the sketch.

"We write it down," he said.

"We don't have time to write it down."

"We make time."

"Ash."

"I know. I know we don't have time. We make it anyway. You do the heating system. I do the pump. Haran does the rural pieces. We write it the way a person who has not seen it would need to read it. We start with the bevel. Then the next thing under the bevel. Then the next."

"And while we're writing it Halder Row is cold."

"Yes."

"And the next block."

"Yes."

She was quiet.

"I'll go to Halder this afternoon," she said. "I'll show them the bevel. I will, I'll do that one. But Ash."

"I know."

"I can't do all of them."

"I know."

"I'm one person."

"I know."

She rolled up the sketch. She did it slowly. She tied the cord on it the way she tied cord on a thing, with the small extra loop she always put in, and she put it back under her arm. She picked up the satchel.

"Halder this afternoon," she said. "I'll come back tomorrow."

"All right."

"Write down the bevel."

"I will."

She went out. He heard her footstep on the gravel going away, brisk and uneven, the gait of a woman who had not sat down today and was not going to.

Haran, at the back bench, had not moved during any of it. He spoke now without turning.

"Picture of the system," he said.

"Yes."

"That's the phrase."

"Yes."

"Write that down too."

* * *

Ryn came at ten.

She came in the way she had begun to come in, which was without knocking, with the small nod to Haran that Haran returned without looking up, and she went to the kettle on the back stove and put her hand on it to see if it was warm and then she set it on the burner without saying anything to anybody, and Ash watched her do it from the bench and felt the small ordinary gladness of her being in the room.

She did not start with the news. She started by getting down three cups.

"Haran."

"Mm."

"You'll have a cup."

"I'll have a cup."

She poured. She brought him his first because Haran was furthest from the kettle, then hers, then Ash's. She sat on the stool across from Ash's bench and wrapped her hands around her cup and looked at him over the rim of it, and he saw, in the way she looked at him, that she had something to say and was going to say it after she had drunk a swallow of tea, and he waited.

She drank her swallow.

"Davan spoke last night," she said.

"Where."

"The square at Foundry Cross. Smaller crowd than the last one. Maybe sixty. But they stayed, Ash. The ones who came stayed. He went on for an hour and they did not leave."

"What did he say."

She set the cup down. She looked at it.

"The line I'm going to give you, I'm going to give you the way he said it. I want you to have it the way he said it."

"All right."

"He said: *The Wellspring chose to build water pumps while the world goes dark. History will judge the scale of that miscalculation.*"

She did not look up when she said it. She had a way of saying a hard thing that was to set it down on the bench between them and let it lie there, and she did that with this, and he watched her do it.

He did not say anything for a moment.

"That's the line."

"That's the line. He said other things. He said, he said a lot of things. That was the line he meant."

"All right."

"There's something else."

"All right."

She picked up the cup again. She did not drink. She held it.

"He didn't sound like himself," she said. "He used to sound like a man who was angry at the world for being broken. He sounded last night like a man who has decided the world is broken and is going to be honest about it and that the honesty is the only thing left worth doing. The crowd, the crowd was different too. They weren't, they weren't there to be roused. They were there to be told the truth. He told them a truth."

"What truth."

"That it isn't going to be all right."

"Ah."

"He's not wrong."

"No."

"That's the part I." She stopped. She turned the cup a quarter on the saucer the way Maren had turned a cup yesterday, and he saw the gesture and did not say anything about it. "I'm not worried about the line, Ash. I'm not worried about what he said about you. I'm worried about him."

"Yes."

"I saw a face on him. I've seen that face before. I saw it on my father the year he stopped trying to find work. It's a particular face. It's the face of a person who has decided the trying was the thing that hurt them."

"Yes."

"He's going to do something."

"Yes."

"I don't know what. He doesn't know what either. But the face he had last night, that face does something. It doesn't sit and write a letter. It does a thing."

He did not answer for a long time.

He drank his tea. He set the cup down. He looked at Haran's back at the rear bench, and Haran was working now, the slow patient unfolding of a coil he had started yesterday, the small precise movement of a man who had been doing the small precise movement for thirty years and would do it today regardless.

"Thank you," he said.

"For what."

"For telling me."

"You'd rather know."

"I'd rather know."

"Right."

She drank.

"He named you," she said. "I don't think you should make anything of that. He named you because you're the shape that is closest to what he is angry at, and he had to name something. Don't, don't take that on as a thing to answer."

"I won't."

"You will. A little. Don't take it all on."

"All right."

"I'll come back tonight."

"All right."

She set the cup in the sink. She did not rinse it. Haran had a particular feeling about cups that were rinsed before the second pot of the day, and Ryn knew about it, and she did not rinse the cup, and she went out, and the door clicked behind her in the small definite way she closed doors.

Haran spoke without turning.

"She's a good one," he said.

"Mm."

"You'd be a fool to lose her."

"I know."

"Just so you know I know."

"I know you know."

"Good."

* * *

He went to the district at the noon hour.

He walked. He walked because he had walked it for a year now and the walk had become a way he thought, the long curve down off the workshop hill and through the lower commercial blocks and into the quarter where the proof-of-concept buildings stood in their familiar attitude along Ferrier Street. He passed three faces he knew on the way. Two nodded. One stopped him to ask about a cousin in the hill country, and he said he would ask Haran, and the woman thanked him and went on, and he went on.

He came up Ferrier Street to Building Six.

The pump was running.

He stood across the street and watched it for a minute the way he had stood and watched it for a minute every time he had come here for the last three weeks. The pump was relocated now from the cellar to the small alcove off the ground-floor passage, a thing the residents had decided on without him, because the cellar had flooded in the spring rains and they had not wanted to risk it again. The relocation was imperfect. The pipe ran up an inside wall in a way he would not have run a pipe up an inside wall. The bracket on the upper coupling was a bracket someone had cut from a sheet of tin and bent by hand. It was not the bracket he would have made. It was the bracket they had made. It worked.

He watched the small steady cycle of it. He watched a woman come out of the front door of Building Six with a bucket and not, he noted, look at the pump as she went past it. The pump had become the kind of thing a person did not look at. That was the thing. The pump had become wallpaper. The pump had become the way water came out of the wall. Three weeks ago the pump had been a thing. Now it was a fact.

He did not channel anything.

He had not channelled anything in this district in three weeks and one day. He stood across the street and listened, the way Haran had taught him to listen, for the small wrong note in a system that was running, and there was no wrong note, only the steady cycle of the relocated pump and the soft whistle of the heating riser two windows above it, the new riser, the one the residents had patched in themselves with a length of pipe Beni had cut from the old gantry on the east face.

The district breathed without him.

He stood with that for a moment. He stood with the small hard truth of it which was not a triumph. The district breathed without him and somewhere, two streets over, in a block called Halder Row, a girl of seventeen was burning a pool fifty per cent over to keep one room warm and thinking she had built the system. Both of those were true. The truth of one did not cancel the truth of the other.

He walked to the corner of Ferrier and looked east.

He could see, from the corner, the tall pale shoulder of the tenement at the top of Halder Row. He could not see the building itself. He could see the shoulder. He stood and looked at it for perhaps the count of ten and then he turned and went back the way he had come.

The neighbouring block was cold.

He felt the cold air come up the cross-street from the east as he turned for home. There was a woman at the doorway of the third building down from the corner with a child wrapped in a coat that was not quite enough coat for the child, and she looked up at him as he passed, and he nodded, and she nodded back, and there was nothing in the nod. There did not need to be. The cold was in the street and he walked back through it to the workshop and his coat was open and he had been warm when he left and he was not warm now.

* * *

The message from Dorenne came at three.

It came by runner. A young man Ash had not seen before, with a leather pouch and a face that did not know any of the people whose names were on the things in it, and he handed Ash a folded sheet sealed with the small grey wafer Dorenne used and waited a polite half-step back the way runners were trained to wait.

Ash broke the seal.

The letter was short. It was on her own paper. The letterhead was the old one, *Office of the Director of Infrastructure*, but a thin pencil line had been ruled through *Director* and nothing written above it, and he stood with the letter in his hand and looked at the ruled line for a moment before he read the body of the message.

The body was four sentences.

She had, the letter said, lost her authority over infrastructure allocation in the vote she had described to him in the spring. She had retained, she said, certain administrative resources, the logistics networks, the supply schedulers, the institutional knowledge, the people. These resources, the letter said, were available. She would direct any of them to the building work, at any scale it could absorb, on his word.

There was no greeting. There was no signature beyond her initial.

He read it twice. He read the ruled line through *Director* a third time.

The runner was waiting.

"Tell her," he said, and stopped.

The runner waited.

"Tell her thank you," he said. "Tell her I will write her tonight."

"Yes sir."

"Don't call me sir."

"Yes."

The runner went.

He stood with the letter in his hand and looked at it. He went back inside the workshop and put it on Haran's bench under the iron weight, beside the letter from the rural networks and under the spectacles, and he stood with his hand on the weight for a moment before he took the hand away.

Haran looked over.

"What."

"Dorenne."

"What does she say."

He did not answer for a moment.

"She's offering," he said. "The networks. The schedulers. What she has left."

"Ah."

"It isn't a partnership."

"No."

"It's a concession."

"Yes."

"She's choosing to be useful instead of in charge."

"Yes."

Haran turned the coil over in his hand. He turned it back. He set it down.

"That's hard," he said.

"Yes."

"For her, I mean."

"I know."

"You'll write her tonight."

"I'll write her tonight."

"Good."

* * *

The evening came down the slope of the workshop windows the way it had for ten years, the slow change in the colour of the iron rail outside the south sash, the small lengthening of the line of shadow off the bench leg onto the floor. Haran lit the second lamp at the rear bench. Ash lit the small one at the front. They had done this in this order for so long that neither of them had to look up when the other did it.

Tesha had not come back.

He had not expected her back tonight. She would be at Halder Row still or she would be home with her boy and a bowl of something. She would come tomorrow. The bevel would still be wrong tonight. The bevel would be wrong in the morning. He had a piece of paper in front of him on the bench with the word *bevel* written at the top of it and a small drawing of the joint under the word, and he had been at the small drawing for an hour now and he had not, he realized, written a second thing.

He set the pen down.

He looked across the workshop at Haran.

Haran was at his bench with the coil still in his hand. He had been working on the coil all afternoon and he was no further along on it than he had been at noon, and that was not, with Haran, a sign of anything, because Haran's pace was Haran's pace and a coil came when the coil came. He had the coil in one hand and a small file in the other and he was not, in this moment, doing either thing. He was looking at the cold patch on the wall above the bench where the old map had hung until last winter.

Ash got up. He brought his stool over and sat across from Haran the way he had sat across from Haran at the end of a hundred days.

Haran did not look up.

"You're not going to solve all of this," he said.

"I know."

Haran was quiet a moment.

"You're not going to solve most of it."

"I know that too."

Haran picked up the gear that lay between them on the bench. It was not a gear they were working on. It was a gear that had been on the bench for two weeks because somebody had brought it in for sharpening and the sharpening had not been done. He turned it in his fingers. He looked at the teeth of it. He set it back down on the bench in the same spot it had been in.

"Good," he said. "That's a start."

Ash did not say anything.

Haran picked up the file.

The file made the small dry sound on the coil that the file made, and the workshop made the small attendant sounds it made, the soft tick of the second lamp's wick, the wind on the south sash, the far complaint of a cart somewhere along the lower terrace. Ash sat on the stool a moment longer, and then he got up, and he went back to his bench, and he wrote, under *bevel*, the next thing.

He wrote: *The bevel is on the inside of the sleeve. You cannot see it from outside the wall.*

He looked at the sentence.

It was an ordinary sentence. It was not eloquent. It would not move anyone. He left it on the page and wrote the next.

* * *

Ryn came back at half past nine.

She did not come in. She stood at the door, with her coat on, and the coat was buttoned to the top, and she had the small grey scarf over the collar that meant the wind had picked up in the last hour. She knocked the small two-knock she used on the doorframe to say she was at the door without coming in, and Ash looked up from the page, and Haran, at the back, said *go on* without turning, and Ash got up.

He went to the door. He stepped out onto the small slate landing. He pulled the door half-to behind him.

The lane was dark. The lamp at the foot of the steps was lit, and the wind moved in the ash trees by the cooper's wall, and her face in the lamp light was the face she brought when she had been thinking a thing on the walk over and had decided to say it.

"Ryn."

"Hi."

"You've been walking."

"A bit."

"It's late."

"It's not that late."

She did not say anything for a moment. She looked at him and he looked at her and the wind moved in the ash trees and the lamp bracket on the wall above them creaked the small particular creak it made when the wind came up the lane from the river.

"You look different than you did six months ago," she said.

"How."

She thought about it. He could see her thinking. She did not answer quickly. She did not, in general, answer quickly. He had loved that in her, he realized, since the first month of her at the workshop, the way she let a question stand until she had something to say to it.

"Like someone who's going to lose something and knows it and isn't running."

He did not answer.

He stood with that. The wind came up the lane again and moved the small loose end of her scarf against her cheek and she pushed it back without looking at it.

"Ryn."

"Yes."

"Thank you."

"For what."

"For coming up here at half past nine to say that."

"Mm."

"You could have said it tomorrow."

"It wasn't a tomorrow thing."

"No."

She looked at him a moment longer. Then she put her hand on his arm above the elbow, the small pressure of her fingers through the wool of his shirt, three small distinct presses the way a person checked a wall for a beam, and then she took the hand away.

"Go on in," she said. "Wind's coming up."

"I'll walk you down."

"You won't. You've got writing to do. I saw the page."

"Ryn."

"Tomorrow."

"Tomorrow."

She went down the steps.

He stood on the landing and watched her go to the foot of the lane and turn at the cooper's wall, and then he could not see her any more, and he stood a moment longer in the wind, and then he went back inside.

Haran did not look up.

"Good," Haran said.

"Mm."

"Sit."

He sat.

* * *

He did not write any more that night.

He sat at the bench with the page in front of him and the small drawing of the joint and the two sentences under it, and after a while he put the page aside and picked up the spring mechanism Tesha had left on the corner of his bench three days ago. She had brought it for him to look at. She had not, in the end, asked him about it. She had set it down and said *when you get a minute* and he had not, until now, got the minute.

He turned it in his hands.

It was a small thing. It was a return spring for the coupling cycle on the heating system, the part of the mechanism that brought the iron arm back to its rest position after the pulse. The original spring, his spring, was a flat coil of the kind that had been in the workshop since he was a boy. Tesha's spring was not a flat coil. Tesha's spring was a helical wind on a small steel pin, set in a brass collar that she had cut herself from a piece of plate she had brought from somewhere, and he had not, when she had set it down three days ago, immediately understood why she had built it that way.

He understood now.

His spring fatigued. It fatigued at the inner curve, at the second bend, after about four months of the cycle the residents ran it at, and it had to be replaced, and the replacement was a workshop job, and the workshop job was an Ash job. Tesha's spring did not fatigue at the inner curve, because Tesha's spring had no inner curve. Tesha's spring distributed the load along the helix, and the helix could be replaced by anyone who could turn a brass collar, and a brass collar could be turned by Yula, and Yula could turn a brass collar in an afternoon, and the residents would never again need a workshop job for the coupling cycle because the coupling cycle would never again need a workshop.

The spring was clumsy.

The brass collar was thicker than it needed to be. The pin was slightly off centre. The wind of the helix was not even. He could see, in the unevenness, the small hand corrections she had made as she went, the places where she had tightened a turn she had let go too loose two turns ago. It was the work of a person who had not done the work before. He could have made the spring more elegant in an afternoon.

He could not have made the spring more right.

He had not, when he had designed the original, thought about who would replace it. He had thought about how it would work. He had thought about it the way a man with a Wellspring thought about a thing, which was as a problem to be solved by the person who could solve it. Tesha had thought about who would replace it because Tesha did not have a Wellspring and had never had one and had grown up in a flat where the person who fixed a thing was the person whose hand was free, and the spring she had built was the spring of a person who had built a thing for the hands that would have to live with it.

He set the spring down.

He set it down carefully, on the corner of the bench, in the spot where she had set it three days ago. He looked at it.

He did not say anything.

* * *

Haran banked the forge at ten and went up the back stair to his rooms without saying goodnight, which was Haran's way of saying goodnight, and Ash sat at the bench another quarter of an hour after the back stair had stopped making the small sounds it made when Haran climbed it. The workshop went quiet the way it went quiet at the end of a day, the small ticks of cooling iron, the soft settling of the second lamp's wick, the wind on the south sash that was not a wind any more but a pressure.

He sat with his hands on the bench.

The reservoir was inside him.

He noticed it the way a person noticed a thing that had been there all along. He had stopped, in the last three weeks, doing the small daily count of it that he had done all year. He had stopped because the count had become a thing he carried and he had been trying not to carry the count, the way Haran had taught him not to count a fire in the forge, the way you let a fire be the fire it was without weighing it.

He felt for it now.

It was there. It was thinner than it had been and it was lower than it had been, and the bottom of it, the floor of it, was closer to his hand than it had ever been in his life. He could feel the bottom. That was the thing. He had not been able to feel the bottom a year ago. A year ago the reservoir had been the thing the river had given him and the bottom had been somewhere far down, somewhere the worry could not reach without a torch, and he had known, as a fact, that there was a bottom to it because everything had a bottom, but he had not, in the body, felt it.

He felt it now.

It was not numerical. It was not the *eighteen per cent* he had once heard a Wellspring assessor use as a phrase, in some other Wellspring's house, in some long-ago year. It was a thing in his chest. It was the way a cup felt, in the hand, when the tea had cooled, and there was less in the cup than there had been when it was hot, and the holding of the cup was a different holding because the weight in the hand was different. It was that. It was a sensation. It was a fact.

The fear had not gone.

He sat with that too. The old fear was where it had always been. Twenty-three years he had carried it, and a year of the river, and four months since he had let it have a name, and it had not gone away because the river had not given him a thing that took the fear away. The river had given him a reservoir. It had not given him the absence of fear. The fear was there. *Ordinary means worthless. Powerless means purposeless.* It was old. It was old as the kettle in his mother's kitchen. It was old as the back of his hand on the night he had been seven and stood on a stool.

It had not gone.

But there was a thing beside it.

He could not name it. It was not peace. He had read books with the word *peace* in them and the books had not been about this. It was not resolve. He had been resolved in the spring, after the river, and the resolve had broken on the rock of the rural collapses and he had had to find a thing under the resolve. This was the thing under the resolve. It was a smaller thing than the resolve. It was the willingness to keep building knowing it was not enough. It was the willingness to let the spring on the bench be Tesha's spring and not his spring. It was the willingness to be ordinary in a way the seven-year-old on the stool would not have understood, would have wept at, would have refused. It was the willingness to trust the people, the residents in Building Six, the girl of seventeen on Halder Row who would learn the bevel in a week, Haran's letters, Tesha's writing-down, Ryn at the door in the wind. To trust them with the thing he could not carry. To let what he could not carry be carried, or not be carried, by hands that were not his.

The fear was there. The willingness was there.

He sat with both.

* * *

He banked the forge by hand.

He did it the way Haran had banked it for thirty years, the slow careful raking of the embers to the back of the firebed, the layering of the ash over the embers in the flat patient drift that would keep the fire's heart alive until the morning. He did not channel. He had never channelled to bank a forge, because Haran did not channel to bank a forge, and the way Haran banked a forge was the way the forge was banked.

The ash settled.

He stood with the rake in his hand for a moment and watched the small last red glow under the ash, the eye of the fire that would be the fire's morning, and then he hung the rake on its peg and he put on his coat and he went out and he closed the workshop door behind him.

The lane was dark.

The wind had dropped. The lamp at the foot of the steps was still lit and the lamp at the cooper's wall was still lit and the lane between them was the lane it always was at this hour, and he went down the steps and turned at the cooper's wall and started home.

He went the long way.

He had taken to going the long way, in the last weeks, because the long way went through the proof-of-concept district and across the bridge and along the lower terrace past the cold blocks east of the river, and the going through and the going past had become a thing he did at the end of a day, the way a man checked the lines he had set in a river before he went to bed.

He went past Building Six.

The pump was running. The sound of it came up through the slot grate at the alcove window, the small steady cycle, and he did not stop, and he went on.

He went past the corner of Ferrier.

He turned east.

He went into the cold blocks.

The street was darker here. The lamps were further apart and two of the three on the first stretch had not been lit. He passed the doorway where the woman with the wrapped child had stood at noon, and the doorway was empty now, and the building was dark, and he could see, in two windows on the second floor, the faint warm flicker of a contributed pulse, the small careful pooling that the residents on the upper floors did for each other in the evenings, and he could see, in the rest of the windows, no light at all.

He went on.

He passed a door where someone had nailed a paper to the lintel with a hand-written list of names and times for the next morning's pool, the small unromantic logistics of people keeping each other warm with what they had between them. He passed a door that was open onto a hall and the hall smelled of cold stone. He passed a door that was closed.

He came to the end of the second cold block and turned for home.

* * *

The wall was at the corner.

He had passed the wall a thousand times. It was the wall of the storage shed at the back of the bakery on Tinker Lane, a wall of old brick and old plaster and the sort of cracked grey paint that the lower terrace put on a wall when the wall was not a wall anyone was going to look at. The wall had had, for two months now, the Levelers' symbol on it, the circle and line, painted in the white the Levelers used, four feet high, in the spot where the lamp from the corner threw its light when the corner lamp was lit.

Tonight someone had scratched the symbol out.

It was scratched, not painted over. Scratched with something hard, a piece of slate or the corner of a brick, the white paint gouged through to the plaster underneath in a scribble of crossings that took the circle and the line and made of them a small frantic mess. Whoever had done it had not done it neatly. They had done it the way a person scratched a thing they had decided to be done with, and his eye took the scratching first.

Then his eye took the writing.

It was under the scratching. It was in chalk. The chalk was the white chalk a workman used for marking a beam, and the letters were not workman's letters. They were the letters of a hand that did not write often. They were large and they were not even and the bottom of one of them had run down the brick where the chalk had caught a wet spot. They said:

*WHAT NOW?*

He stood across the lane from the wall and looked at it.

The lamp from the corner threw its light on the paint and on the chalk and on the scratching, and he stood with his coat open in the cold and looked at the question and did not have an answer for it, and the city did not have an answer for it, and the wall did not have an answer for it, and the chalk had run a little down the brick where it had caught the wet, and that was all.

He did not have an answer.

He stood a moment longer.

He kept walking.
