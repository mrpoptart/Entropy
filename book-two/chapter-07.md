# Chapter 7

Building Four had gone cold in the night.

Ash heard it before he saw it. He came down the stairs from the small room above the workshop and crossed the square in the gray hour and Tesha was already at the door of Block Three's neighbor, her coat over her nightshirt, a child of perhaps six on her hip with the child's face pressed into her shoulder. The child wasn't crying. The child was simply where a child went when a room had stopped being warm.

"Fourth floor," Tesha said. "All of it. The risers ran cold past the second."

"When."

"Sometime after the third hour. Reska felt it first."

He went up.

The stair was cold the way a stair was cold when the heat had been gone long enough for the masonry to give up its store. On the fourth-floor landing a man stood in the doorway of his flat with his arms folded across his chest and his breath visible. He nodded at Ash. He didn't say anything. He had been a skeptic in the second week and a participant in the fourth and tonight he was a man whose flat was cold and he was holding his face still about it.

"Morning," Ash said.

"Morning."

"How long."

"Three hours. The wall by the window went first."

Ash put his palm on the inner wall by the bedroom door. The plaster was the temperature of plaster on a winter morning in a building with no heat. He moved his hand to the riser channel. The channel was the same.

"I'll get Haran," he said.

"You'll get him."

"I will."

The man nodded once. He did not move from the doorway. Behind him, in the dim of the room, his wife sat on the edge of the bed with a blanket around her shoulders and a kettle beside her on the floor that she had filled, perhaps, from the basin tap in the dark, and had not yet decided what to do with.

Ash went down.

* * *

Haran came up with the lamp and the long iron and the small leather roll of brass tools he carried for the work he expected to find and had not yet seen.

He didn't speak on the stairs. He didn't speak on the landing. He nodded at the man in the doorway and the man stepped back, and Haran went into the flat and put his palm on the wall the way Ash had put his palm on the wall, and then he put his ear to the riser channel and held it there for a long moment.

"Thirty," Haran said.

"Thirty?"

"Thirty seams in this riser. One of them's gone. We'll find it."

"Where."

"Between here and the cellar."

He went down. Ash followed.

In the cellar Haran knelt by the manifold. He set the lamp on the floor beside his knee. He put his palm on the brass at the manifold's joint and held it. Moved it. Held it on the next.

"Cold," he said. "Cold. Cold. Warm. Cold."

He looked up.

"Second riser," Haran said. "Two seams above the manifold. The flux didn't take."

"Last week."

"Last week. The day Vell was sick and we ran two short. I did that joint. I did it tired."

He said it without weight. He said it the way he said most things he meant. The lamp on the floor was at the level of his knee and the light came up under his glasses and made the lenses two small circles, and his face above the lenses was the face of a man reading a measurement off a rule.

"I'll redo it," Haran said.

"I'll help."

"You'll bring brass. Bring the small flux. Bring two rags."

Ash went up.

* * *

By the seventh hour the riser was warm again.

Haran had cut the bad joint, reamed both pipe ends, brazed the new sleeve, and tested the line at three pressures before he let the manifold take heat. Ash had held the lamp. Ash had passed the flux. Ash had brought, on Haran's instruction, the third length of pipe from the shelf above the bench, the spare that the spare's spare had become, and they had used the spare and not the spare's spare, and Haran, when the work was done, had said, *Tell your father two is fine for a riser. Three is for a pump.*

The man on the fourth floor came down the stair with his wife at his elbow as Haran was coiling the leather roll. He stopped at the cellar door. He didn't come in.

"It's warm," he said.

"It's warm."

"For how long."

Haran lifted his head. He looked at the man over the rim of his glasses.

"Until the next one," Haran said.

The man considered this. The man's wife, behind him, did not look at Haran. She looked at the manifold, at the lamp, at the brass sleeve still bright where the file had taken the oxide off. She put her hand on her husband's arm.

"Thank you," she said.

"Mm," Haran said.

She nodded. She turned. They went back up.

Ash watched them go.

"It's the second time this month," he said.

"It is."

"Building Four."

"Building Four. The risers are old. The pipe we put in is good. The risers we tied into are what they are."

"We replace them."

"We replace them when we have brass and a week."

"And until then."

"Until then we get up at the third hour and we redo the joint that didn't take. That's the work."

He said it without complaint. He said it the way a man said *cut here* when the cut needed to be there. He stood. He set the leather roll in the cabinet. He took his glasses off and wiped them on his shirt and put them back on.

"Eat," he said.

"I'm not hungry."

"Eat."

* * *

The eastern-block pump had been running on a cracked stroke-arm for nine days.

The stroke-arm had been wound at The Splits and brought down by the cart that came once a fortnight with the brass and the small steel. The replacement Haran had sent for would not arrive for another six days. In the meantime the pump ran. The crack ran the length of the arm's thinnest section, hairline, no widening yet, and Haran had wrapped it in copper wire and rosin and told the morning crew to listen for the change in the sound the crank made under load.

"You'll know it," Haran had said. "It'll go from a hum to a buzz. When it buzzes you stop."

The morning crew chief was a woman named Reska, who had come around in the third week and had taken to the work the way a person took to a thing they had been waiting their life for without knowing it. She had trained the eastern-block crew on the listening. She had drawn the change in the sound as a line on a slate. *Hum,* she had written. *Buzz. Stop.*

The arm had not buzzed yet.

Ash stopped at the eastern pump on his way back from Building Four. The morning fill was done. The cistern indicator stood at its mark. The cover had been tied at the corners over the platform the way Vell tied her own pump's cover, which was the way Reska had taught her crew to tie it because Vell had taught Reska. The square smelled of the basin water and of the bread from the oven two doors down, and a child was crouched at the basin's edge with a cup, drinking slow, the way a child drank when the water had become a thing the child trusted.

Reska came out of the cellar door behind the pump.

"Quiet morning," she said.

"You heard about Four."

"I heard. Haran's down?"

"Done. Riser's warm."

"Mm."

She did not ask which seam. She did not ask whether it was Haran's or the original masons'. She had decided, some weeks ago, that the seam was the seam and the work was the work and the asking did not change either.

"Two more quit," she said.

He looked at her.

"This morning?"

"Last night. Aren and the boy from the second crew. They came to me at the end of shift."

"What did they say."

She considered. She had a slate under her arm. She pulled it out and looked at it as if the answer were on it, which it was not, and put it back.

"They said," Reska said, "that they were tired of being taught like children."

"I see."

"Aren's words. The boy didn't say anything. He stood behind Aren."

"What did you say."

"I said the work was the work. I said if they came back tomorrow we'd put them back on. I said if they didn't, we'd find someone."

"Good."

"They won't come back."

"No."

She looked at the pump. She didn't look at him.

"Aren's a good worker," she said. "He'll find a crew. Channeling crew, probably. Someone who'll let him do it the way he was taught to do it."

"Probably."

"It's the slow that gets to him. The pause-for-looking. Tesha's pause-for-looking. He thinks it's a thing you do when you don't know what you're doing."

"It's not."

"I know it's not."

She did not need him to confirm it. She had not asked him to. She put her hand on the pump's platform brace, the way a person put a hand on the back of a chair belonging to a friend.

"They've started naming them," she said.

"What."

"The systems. The fourth-floor riser is Greta. The pump in the second square's Old Pat. This one's Pat the Younger. I don't know how it started."

"Who's Greta."

"Someone's grandmother. Aren's, maybe. He named it before he quit."

She said it without a smile. She said it the way she said most things, which was the way Tesha said most things, which was the way Haran said most things, which was, Ash registered without naming, beginning to be the way the district said most things.

"Pat the Younger," he said.

"Pat the Younger."

"Mm."

He left her at the pump. He climbed back up to the workshop and the broadsheet was on the bench when he came in.

* * *

It had been folded once.

Haran had not folded it. Haran did not fold broadsheets. Haran left them flat on the bench until the bench needed the space and then he put them under the bench until the under-bench needed the space and then he took them out to the stove. The fold meant someone had handed it to him, and he had taken it and set it down, and the fold was the fold the giver had given it.

The headline ran across the top of the second column.

**THE WELLSPRING'S WORKSHOP: Can Hand Cranks Replace Channeling?**

The byline was *L. Korrin, Civic Affairs.* Ash had not heard the name. The piece ran half a column on the front and continued on the third page, and he sat on the crate by the door and read it through.

The journalist had visited. Ash didn't remember meeting her. He read the piece and located, in the second paragraph, the moment she must have been there, *on a brisk morning at the Stonecurve basin, where a girl of perhaps fifteen turns a crank with the unselfconscious competence of long practice.* That was Vell. The week before last, perhaps. He had been in Building Two that morning. Korrin had come, watched, talked to someone, and gone, and he had not known.

The piece was favorable. It was curious. It was, in the third paragraph, the fifth, and the line that ran over to page three, mannered in a way that made him put the broadsheet down and pick it up again to be sure he had read it.

*One is tempted, in the presence of such ingenuity, to ask whether the Wellspring's workshop is the future of civic infrastructure or a charming exercise in restraint, undertaken by the most powerful channeler of the age in the spirit of a wealthy man who has chosen, for a season, to walk to work.*

He read it twice.

The framing held all the way through. The piece described the proof-of-concept as an experiment. It described the experiment as authorized. It described the authorization as Dorenne's. It quoted, twice, an unnamed senior administrator on the *strategic value of pluralism in civic provision,* which was a phrase Ash had not heard Dorenne speak but which sat in the air around her like the smell of ink in her office. The piece closed with the image of Vell at the crank and the line *the question is not whether hand cranks can replace channeling, but whether a city can be persuaded to want them to.*

He set the broadsheet down.

Haran was at the bench. The file moved.

"You read it," Haran said.

"I read it."

"Mm."

"It's not bad."

"It's not."

"It puts us inside Dorenne's tent."

"It does."

Haran filed. The brass shaving curled off.

"Does it matter," Haran said.

Ash thought.

"Not yet," he said.

"Mm."

"The work matters more than the credit."

"Mm."

He said it twice, the same syllable, the same length, and the second one was not the first one. The second one was the sound a man made when the apprentice had said the right thing for the wrong reason and the man had decided not to correct him today.

Ash heard it. He did not, in the moment, name what he had heard.

He folded the broadsheet along the fold the giver had given it, and he set it on the under-bench shelf, and Haran kept filing.

* * *

The maintenance chief came at the fourth hour.

He came alone. He came on foot. He came with a leather satchel under one arm and a slate under the other and a square gray cap pulled low over his forehead in the way men of his trade wore their caps in the middle districts, where the sun came off the white plaster of the upper terraces and into the eyes of anyone working below.

He stopped at the basin. He turned the tap. The water ran.

He held his hand under it for the time it took to feel the pressure. He closed it. He looked at the cistern. He looked at the pump. He walked the length of the square once with his eyes on the paving and the sloped channels along the edges where the overflow ran. He came to the workshop door.

"Wellspring," he said.

"Yes."

"Velden. Felor Velden. Middle Quarter Maintenance."

He did not extend a hand. He shifted the satchel under his arm and stood with his weight on his back foot. He had the look of a man who had come down from his own neighborhood for the first time in a long time and was deciding, in the first minute, whether the trip had been worth the climb back.

"Come in," Ash said.

"I'll stand."

"Come in and stand."

Velden considered. He came in. He stood.

"My pipes are failing," Velden said. "Pressure's down a third on the upper line. My channelers are putting in two morning shifts to hold it and the second shift is the same five people every day and three of them have asked me if they can work less."

"How many people on the line."

"Twelve hundred households."

"What's the gravity available."

"Fifteen meters from the upper cistern. The cistern's good. The pipes are the pipes."

"You want a supplemental."

"I want a supplemental and I want to know what the supplemental costs."

He said it flat. He said it the way a man said the price of a thing he had decided to buy if the price made sense and to leave if it didn't, and his eyes did not move from Ash's face while he said it.

Ash sat on the crate.

"Costs in what."

"Brass. Time. My people's hands. Whatever it is. I have a budget. I don't have a lot of budget. I have crew."

"How big a crew."

"Eight. Six trained pipefitters. Two apprentices who are useful three days in five."

"What's their channeling."

"Two of the six. Modest pools. They use them for other things."

"Good."

Velden waited.

"Supplemental's possible," Ash said. "Hand-pump at the low cistern, a gravity-fed manifold to your worst run, separation valve so your channeled line stays as it is. We don't replace. We add."

"How long."

"Six weeks if Haran sets the joints. Eight if your crew does and we supervise."

"Eight."

"Eight."

"Eight's longer. Eight's also mine. I'll take eight."

He had not, in the conversation, smiled. He had not nodded. He had not, at any point, indicated that he agreed. He had, at the moment of *I'll take eight,* simply moved on, the way a man moved on from a price he had decided to pay.

"What's it cost the Wellspring," Velden said.

"What."

"You. Your reservoir. To do this on my line. I read the piece this morning. I want to know the number."

Ash looked at him.

He had not, in two months of district work, been asked the question by anyone outside the workshop. Dorenne hadn't asked. Felden hadn't asked. The maintenance chief from the middle quarter who had come down on his own feet with a slate under his arm was asking, and his eyes were the eyes of a man who already had the answer he wanted, which was a number, and the number's size was not the point of the asking.

"For the manifold seal-work," Ash said. "On a line of your size. About a tenth of a percent."

"Of."

"Of what I have."

"How much do you have."

"Enough."

Velden considered this. The corner of his mouth did not move. His eyes did not move. He shifted his weight to his front foot and stood at the same height he had stood and did not make the small adjustment a man made when an answer surprised him.

"All right," Velden said.

"All right."

"Eight weeks. I'll send a man down on Sixth-day for the layout. His name's Korrin. Different Korrin."

"All right."

Velden touched the brim of his cap. Half a touch. He turned. He went out into the square. He did not, on the way out, look at the pump. He had already looked at the pump. He had, Ash registered, done all of his looking before he had come in the door.

Haran filed.

"Pragmatic," Haran said.

"Yes."

"Will pay. Will not thank."

"Yes."

"Best kind."

* * *

The rural liaison came at the sixth hour.

He had been waiting in the square for two hours by the time Haran finished what he was doing and looked up. Ash had brought him tea, twice. The man had drunk both cups slowly and had thanked Ash both times and had not, between the two, asked a question. He sat on the low wall beside the basin with his back against the stone and his legs stretched out in front of him in heavy boots that had been resoled, and he watched the square the way a farmer watched a field, without urgency, registering the shape of what was happening without entering it.

He was perhaps sixty. He was perhaps older. His face was the face of a man who had been outside for most of fifty years and his hands were the hands of a man who had handled rope and hot iron in roughly equal measure for as long. He had given his name as *Othen, from Wash Hollow,* and had not added a surname. Ash did not know whether Wash Hollow was the name of his community or the name of a place near the name of his community, and Othen had not, on either of the two cups of tea, clarified.

Haran came out of the workshop.

"Othen," Haran said.

"Master Haran."

"Not master."

"Old habit."

"Mm."

Haran sat on the low wall beside Othen. He did not, before sitting, brush off the wall. He sat with his hands on his knees and looked at the same field Othen had been looking at, and for a little while neither of them said anything.

"Heating," Othen said, eventually.

"Heating."

"We've been at it twelve years. Since the second bad winter."

"Twelve."

"Started with stoves. Stoves work. Stoves have always worked. It was the moving the heat that we couldn't get."

"Risers."

"Risers, ducts, the thing where you push the warm air with a fan you turn by hand. We tried all of it. Some of it works."

"Show me the some that works."

Othen reached down beside the wall and pulled a leather tube from where it had been leaning against the stone. He undid the cord. He drew out a roll of paper. He unrolled it across his knees, flattened it with the side of his hand, and Haran leaned over and looked.

Ash watched from the workshop step.

The drawing was crude. The lines were not ruled. The dimensions were noted in a tight square hand in the margin, the kind of hand a man developed when he had taught himself to write small because paper was dear. The drawing showed a stove, a flue, a duct, a set of vanes, and a hand-cranked fan mounted in a frame that was, even in the rough drawing, visibly homemade.

Haran looked at the drawing for a long time.

"The vanes," Haran said.

"The vanes."

"You're shedding heat through them."

"We are. We didn't know it for two winters. The man who'd built the first one had moved on. By the time we figured the loss we'd built nine of them."

"Did you fix the nine."

"We fixed seven. Two we left for the school."

"Why the school."

"The school doesn't need it as warm. The children move."

Haran looked at him.

"You sealed the vanes," Haran said.

"We sealed them. With clay. Then we figured the clay cracked the second winter so we sealed them with a clay-and-tow paste. That holds two winters. Then we redo it."

"You redo it every two winters."

"Every two."

"On nine houses."

"On seven."

"Mm."

Haran was looking at the drawing. He took his glasses off. He put them back on. He looked at the drawing again. He pointed at the vanes.

"I have a sleeve," Haran said. "Brass sleeve. Holds twenty winters. I can show you tomorrow."

"Brass."

"Brass. Cast at The Splits. I have a man there. He'll cast you a set if I write him."

Othen did not, in the next moment, do anything. He looked at the drawing. He looked at Haran. He looked at the square. He, slowly, laid his palm flat on the drawing over the vanes, the way a man laid his palm on a thing he had been carrying for twelve years and was, perhaps, about to put down.

"Twenty winters," Othen said.

"Twenty."

"That's a different conversation."

"It is."

"I'll need to write it down. The exact of it."

"I'll write it for you. With the man's name. With the price. With what you ship him to take in trade if you don't have coin."

"We don't have coin."

"I know."

Haran said it the way he said most things. He did not, on saying *I know,* explain what he knew or how he had come to know it. Othen looked at him for a beat that was not uncomfortable. Then he nodded, once, and rolled the drawing carefully back into its tube and tied the cord.

"I'll stay tonight," Othen said.

"You'll stay at the bakery. The room above the workshop has the boy in it."

"The boy."

"The Wellspring."

Othen looked across the square at Ash. He looked at Ash the way he had looked at the square. Without urgency. Registering.

"Mm," he said.

He did not call him *Wellspring.* He did not call him anything. He turned back to Haran and they began, between them, on the question of where, in the bakery, the bedroll would best go.

Ash stood on the workshop step.

He had been called *boy* by his father for twenty-six years and by Haran twice and he had not been called *boy* by a stranger in his adult life. He registered the word the way a man registered a small unfamiliar weight, and set it down.

Othen had not been impressed. Othen had been polite. Othen had, on the way past Ash to the bakery's door, touched the brim of a hat he wasn't wearing, the gesture of a man who had been raised to it and would be doing it on his deathbed. He had not, at any point in the conversation with Haran, looked back at Ash to see how a sentence had landed.

Haran walked Othen to the bakery and came back.

"He didn't ask about you," Ash said.

"No."

"He didn't ask about the reservoir. He didn't ask about the catalytic. He didn't ask anything I expected."

"No."

"What did he come for."

"The vanes."

"The vanes."

"And the man at The Splits whose name he didn't have."

Haran went into the workshop. The file resumed. Ash stayed on the step a minute, and the square in front of him was the square it had been that morning, and an old woman crossed it with a covered pot, slowly, the pot held against her hip the way she had held it the day before and the day before that.

He went in.

* * *

The letters came together.

Felden had sent them down with the second cart of the day, which arrived at the seventh hour with brass and oilcloth and a small box of files Haran had ordered three weeks before. The two letters were on top of the box. They had been opened. Felden had not added a note. Felden, Ash had registered over two months, was a man who delivered objects, not opinions.

He took the letters to the crate by the door and read them in order of the seal.

The first was sealed with the dark blue wax of the young channelers' collective that had begun, in the past year, signing pieces in the third-tier broadsheets under the name *the Open Hand.* He had read four of their pieces. They had been better written than most. The seal was the same one. He broke it.

*To the Wellspring,*

*The broadsheet piece on your workshop reached us yesterday. We have read it. We are writing because we do not believe you have read it the way it will be read.*

*You are a powerful channeler building hand pumps for a poor district. The piece celebrates this. We do not. Or rather, we celebrate what you have built, and we ask you what you have not built.*

*The Wellspring builds toys for the poor while the powerful keep their channeled comfort. When does redistribution start? When does the ocean you carry become a pool the rest of us draw from? When does the man who can warm a city decide to warm it, instead of teaching its poorest to warm themselves with their hands?*

*We are not your enemies. We may be your colleagues. But the piece in this morning's broadsheet has placed you, with your consent or without it, on the side of the people who are content to let the powerful keep what they have. We are writing to ask whether that is where you intended to stand.*

*With respect, and with urgency,*

*The Open Hand.*

He read it twice.

He set it on his knee. He picked up the second.

The second was sealed in red. The wax was the wax of the elder council, the seal a small simple circle without a sigil, the way a councillor sealed a letter to a private person rather than a chamber. He had, in two months, received two such letters. He broke it.

*Wellspring,*

*I have read the broadsheet piece this morning with interest and with concern. Your work is admirable. Your intentions, as I understand them, are sound. I write you privately because I would not write you publicly on the subject I am about to raise.*

*Mechanical alternatives are, with respect, a retreat. They are the recourse of communities that have lost faith in our common inheritance. We have not lost ours. We should not encourage others to lose theirs.*

*Strengthen the channeling infrastructure. Train more channelers. Direct your considerable resources to the maintenance and expansion of the pools we already have. The hand pump in your district will outlive its enthusiasm. Channeling has outlived twelve generations of enthusiasms and will outlive this one.*

*I write you as one civic builder to another. Build forward, not backward.*

*With cordial regard,*

*Councillor Tomas Hellen.*

Ash read it twice.

He set the second letter on top of the first on his knee.

He looked at Haran's back. Haran was at the bench. The file moved.

"Two letters," Ash said.

"Mm."

"One says I'm building toys. The other says I'm retreating."

"Mm."

"They can't both be right."

"They can."

He said it without looking up. He said it the way he had said *the lead-tin won't last six years,* like a measurement read off a rule.

"Both," Ash said.

"You're building a thing the powerful people don't need and the powerless people can't afford to wait for. Both letters are correct. Both letters are about themselves."

"What do I write back."

"To which."

"Either."

Haran filed.

"Don't write back today," Haran said. "Don't write back tomorrow. Read them again on Sixth-day. Then write."

"Why Sixth-day."

"Because by Sixth-day you'll know what you think. Today you only know what they think."

Ash held the letters.

The first, he had seen the sentences of in his own handwriting, in the broadsheet pages he had read at twenty-one and twenty-two and twenty-three, when he had been magicless and angry and had been writing letters in his head to a Wellspring he had not yet known would be him. *The man with an ocean, asking the thirsty to wait.* He had written that line. He had written it in his head walking past the upper terraces in winter. He had written it walking past Hellen's house, perhaps. Hellen's house was on the upper terraces. He did not know if it was the same house.

He set the letters on the under-bench shelf, beside the broadsheet.

The shelf was getting full.

* * *

He left for home at the eighth hour.

He took the long way out of Stonecurve, past the basin, past the eastern-block pump where Reska's evening crew was settling the cover for the night. The crank was quiet. The arm had not buzzed. The boy who had quit had not come back.

He climbed.

The streets between the second and third terraces were the streets that ran along the line where the earthquake's worst had stopped and the elder masonry had held. The buildings on the right side of the street were two centuries older than the buildings on the left. The right side had not cracked. The left side had been demolished and, in places, rebuilt. He walked the line between.

The square at the top of the second terrace was Halver's Square. It was, in the daytime, a market. The market closed at the seventh hour. By the time he reached it on the climb the stalls were down and the flagstones were swept and a crowd had assembled around the broken plinth at the eastern edge where the old statue of Halver had stood until the quake had taken Halver's head and the lower city had not yet replaced it.

Thirty people. Forty. Mostly young. He stopped at the edge.

A man was speaking from the plinth.

He was perhaps thirty. He was tall. He had the build of a person who had channeled all his life and walked a great deal besides, and he had a voice that carried without strain, the voice of a teacher or a lawyer or someone who had decided early what his voice was for. He was not shouting. He was speaking, clearly, in the cadence of an argument that had been built before he had stepped onto the plinth.

"The first claim," the speaker said, "is that channeling is a private inheritance. That what runs in your pool is yours, by accident of birth, and yours to spend. The second claim is that channeling is a public good, that we draw from the same waters and we owe each other something for the drawing. Both claims are old. Both have been argued in this square and on this plinth, when there was a head on it, for longer than my mother's mother was alive."

He paused. The pause was for looking. The crowd was nodding.

"The earthquake," the speaker said, "ended the argument."

Ash stood at the edge.

"The earthquake ended the argument because the earthquake showed us, in three nights, what private channeling and public channeling actually purchase. Private channeling purchased the upper terraces. Public channeling purchased the inspectors who certified the lower buildings as sound. The lower buildings fell. The upper buildings held. The pool of every person in this square was drawn upon, in the days after, to keep bodies alive whose lives had been put at risk by buildings whose foundations had been certified by people the upper terraces had paid to certify them. We poured ourselves into a hole the rich had dug."

The crowd was quiet. The crowd was the kind of quiet that meant the crowd was not, this evening, going to interrupt.

"The first argument now," the speaker said, "is the argument about what to do. There are three answers. The first answer is to do nothing. The second answer is to build alternatives that do not require the pools at all. The third answer is to redistribute the pools we have."

He paused again. The pause was longer.

"The second answer is fashionable this morning."

Some of the crowd laughed. Some did not.

"It is fashionable," the speaker said, "because it is comfortable. It does not ask the powerful to give up anything. It asks the powerful to permit the powerless to do without. It is a generous answer offered by people who have a great deal to be generous with. We have read the broadsheet. We have read the piece. We are familiar with the workshop on Stonecurve."

Ash did not move.

"And there he is," the speaker said.

The crowd turned.

"The man with an ocean, asking the thirsty to wait."

The crowd turned, and Ash registered the turn the way a man registered a wave coming up against the side of a boat, the small change in the level of the water under the hull. Some faces were hostile. The hostility was not new. He had been hated, in his year as the Wellspring, by people in upper-terrace drawing rooms and by people in lower-quarter taverns, and the upper-terrace hatred had been a cold polite thing and the lower-quarter hatred had been a warm direct thing, and the faces in Halver's Square were neither. They were the faces of people who had been listening to an argument and had been persuaded by it and who, on his appearance, were testing the argument against the body of the man it had named.

Some of the faces were not hostile. They were the harder ones.

A woman near the front. Perhaps twenty. Her coat was thin for the hour. Her hands were red at the knuckles in the way hands got when they had been outdoors a great deal. She was looking at him with the look his mother had given him at twelve, the day she had finally asked him whether the school had been hard. The look was hope. The look was *please.* The look was *if you choose us, we win.*

He did not have an answer for the look.

"Do you have something to say, Wellspring?" the speaker said.

The speaker was good. The speaker had pivoted cleanly. The speaker had built the argument and had built it to lead, in the case of Ash's appearance, to this question, and the speaker was standing on the plinth with his hands open at his sides, the open hands of a man who had nothing in them and wanted to be seen to have nothing in them.

Ash looked at him.

He looked at the woman in the thin coat.

He looked at the crowd.

"Not today," Ash said.

He said it without volume. He said it the way Haran said *until the next one.* He said it because he had nothing to say that he had finished thinking, and because he knew, the way Haran had told him he would know by Sixth-day, that he did not yet know what he thought, and because saying anything tonight would be saying it for them and not for himself.

Some of the crowd booed.

Some of the crowd did not.

The woman in the thin coat closed her eyes for a half-second and opened them, and the hope was still there, and the hope was angrier now, the way hope went when it had been asked to wait one more time.

"Not today," the speaker repeated. He said it loud enough for the crowd to hear. He said it with a small smile that was not, Ash registered, cruel. The speaker was a man building an argument, and the argument required the Wellspring's silence, and the Wellspring had given him the silence, and the speaker had taken the silence and used it.

"Not today," the speaker said again. "Tomorrow, perhaps. The day after, perhaps. The Wellspring will tell us, when he is ready, whether the ocean is for him or for us."

Ash turned.

He walked.

The crowd parted for him because crowds parted for him, and because the speaker had not asked them to stop him, and because the people who had wanted him to choose their side had been, in the half-second the speaker's words had taken to land, given a different thing to want, which was to watch him leave.

He climbed out of the square.

He did not look back. He did not, for the next two streets, think clearly. He thought *I should have spoken.* He thought *I should not have spoken.* He thought *the woman in the coat.* He thought *the broadsheet piece. The two letters. Hellen's red wax. The Open Hand's blue.*

He thought, for the first time since Building Four had gone cold that morning, *the math doesn't work at three percent.*

He set the thought down.

He climbed.

* * *

The workshop window was lit when he came back down into Stonecurve.

He had walked, the long way, around the upper edge of the second terrace and back down through the western stair, and the walk had been an hour, and the dusk had become night while he walked. He had not stopped at his mother's. He had decided, at the second street, that he would not. He would, on Twelfth-day, sit at her table and eat her stew. Tonight he would sit on the crate by the door and watch the lamp.

He came across the square. The basin tap ran. Vell's pump was under its cover. The eastern pump was under its cover.

The shift was changing at Block Three.

He stopped at the door.

Reska had gone home. The crew chief on the evening shift was a woman named Jora, who had been on the morning crew for six weeks and had moved to evenings at her own request, because her mother was at home in the day and she was at home at night, and the shift fit the household better. She was at the bench. A new recruit stood beside her, a girl perhaps eighteen, the daughter of someone Ash did not yet know by name.

Jora was teaching her the manifold check.

Ash stood in the doorway.

"You start at the cellar," Jora said. "Always start at the cellar. The manifold's the place where, if a thing's gone wrong, the going-wrong shows first."

The girl nodded.

"Put your palm on the manifold's first joint. Hold it for a count of three. Move to the second. Count of three. Move to the third. The temperature's the temperature. Cold, cold, warm, cold means the second riser. Cold, warm, cold, cold means the third. You'll learn the pattern."

"What if they're all warm."

"Then the manifold's fine and you go up. You walk every riser. Hand on the wall. Count of three. The wall's the wall."

The girl nodded again. She had a slate. She was making marks.

"Don't skip the count," Jora said. "The count is for looking. The count is when you find the buckle before it's a buckle."

Ash watched.

He watched Jora's hands. The hands were good. The hands moved the way Tesha's hands moved, which was the way Haran's hands moved, which was the way, he registered, the hands of any worker in the district had begun to move when the work asked the hands to move that way.

The phrasing was the thing.

*The count is for looking. The count is when you find the buckle before it's a buckle.* That was Tesha's phrase. Tesha had said it on the bench, two weeks before, the morning Maren had been in the workshop. Jora had been there. Jora had, the line said, taken the phrase and was using it now, and the new recruit was making a mark on her slate that was, perhaps, the same mark Jora had made on her slate when Tesha had said it the first time.

He watched.

"What's the pattern," Jora said. "When the riser's gone."

"Cold, cold, warm, cold."

"Good. What's the pattern when the manifold's the problem."

"All cold."

"All cold and the cellar's cold. If the cellar's warm and the manifold's cold, you've got a different problem. You come and get me."

"I come and get you."

"Mm."

The girl smiled. The smile was the small private smile of a person who had said the right thing to the teacher and knew it. Jora did not smile back. Jora was already at the next thing, the priming valve, the little brass cap on the manifold's secondary line that Haran had installed in the third week and had taught Reska and Reska had taught Jora.

"This," Jora said, "is the priming valve. It does what the Wellspring says priming valves do."

The girl looked up.

"What does the Wellspring say."

"He says a priming valve gives the system somewhere to fail safely. He says you build a system not to fail and then you build, into the system, the place where, when it fails anyway, the failure is small."

The girl wrote it on her slate.

She wrote it the way she had written the count.

Ash stood in the doorway.

He had said the line about the priming valve, perhaps, in the second week, in the workshop, with three workers around the bench. He had said it once. He had not, in the weeks since, said it again. He had not, until this evening, heard it said back.

Jora was good. Jora was patient. Jora was proud, in the small way she had been proud since the first week, when she had brought her teenage son to the workshop and shown him the manifold and said, in front of the boy, *this is what your mother does now.* The pride was the thing he had been hoping to see in any worker on any crew, and he had seen it tonight, in Jora, and the seeing had a clean clear quality, the quality of a thing that worked.

He watched her teach.

The girl wrote on her slate. The slate was the same kind of slate Reska carried. The marks on the slate were the marks Reska made. The phrasing Jora used was Tesha's phrasing, and the phrasing about the priming valve was Ash's phrasing, and the count of three was Haran's count, and the pattern of cold-cold-warm-cold for the diagnostic was the pattern Haran had drawn on the cellar wall in chalk in the second week and that Reska had copied to her slate and that Jora had memorized off Reska's slate.

He watched.

He thought, *this is what I'm building.*

He thought, *this is people teaching people.*

He thought it because the scene was beautiful and the scene was working and the scene was, in every visible respect, the thing he had set out to make. He thought it the way a builder thought *the wall holds* on the first morning a wall held.

Jora was at the priming valve. The girl had her hand on the cap. Jora was guiding her hand with two fingers on the back of the girl's wrist, the way Tesha had guided a worker's hand on the lap two weeks before.

"Three-quarters of a turn," Jora said. "Don't go past three-quarters. The Wellspring says the valve seats at three-quarters. Past that you're stressing the spring."

"Three-quarters."

"The Wellspring says."

The girl nodded. She turned the cap. Three-quarters. She stopped.

"Good," Jora said.

The girl smiled the small private smile again.

Ash stood in the doorway.

He watched a long minute.

He did not, in the moment, name what he was watching. He registered the warmth of it. He registered the working of it. He registered Jora's pride and the girl's smile and the manifold's quiet hum and the lamp's small steady light, and he set the registering down where he had set the things he set down at the end of a day, on the shelf, beside the broadsheet, beside the two letters, beside the half-beat at his sister's cup that he had not, since Twelfth-day, lifted the cloth off of.

The shelf was getting full.

Jora finished the lesson. She put the slate in the rack by the door. She nodded at the girl. The girl went out into the square with the small straight back of a person who had been, that evening, taught a thing she had wanted to learn.

Jora turned. She saw Ash in the doorway.

"Wellspring," she said.

"Jora."

"Long day."

"Long day."

"Building Four."

"Building Four."

She nodded. She did not ask which seam. She did not ask whether Haran had said anything about the joint he had done tired. She had decided, like Reska, that the seam was the seam. She had taken the deciding from Reska, perhaps, the way she had taken the count from Tesha, the way she had taken the priming valve from him.

"She's a good one," Jora said. She tilted her head at the doorway the girl had gone out of. "Quiet. Listens."

"Mm."

"She'll be running a crew by the spring."

"Mm."

"If we're still here."

She said it lightly. She said it the way Reska had said *they won't come back,* which was the way Tesha said most things, which was the way Haran said most things. She had become, in eight weeks, the kind of person who said the hard thing the way the people around her said the hard thing.

"We'll be here," Ash said.

"Mm."

She went past him into the square. The shift was changing. The morning crew would come at the third hour. The night was settling on the basin tap and the covered pumps and the lamps in the windows that were beginning, one by one, to come on.

Ash stood in the doorway a minute longer.

The cellar was quiet. The manifold hummed. The lamp on the workbench burned. The slate was in the rack by the door, the chalk marks still on it, the Wellspring's lines and Haran's lines and Tesha's lines and Reska's lines and Jora's lines, all of them, in the same hand now, because Jora had copied them, because Reska had copied them, because the new recruit had copied them tonight onto her own slate and would carry them out into the square and write them down, tomorrow, on a slate of her own.

He looked at the slate.

He looked at the lamp.

He thought, *this is what I'm building.*

He turned. He went out into the square. He crossed it slowly, past the basin tap, past Vell's pump, past the eastern pump and the bakery where Othen would be settling on a bedroll tonight, and he climbed the small stair to the room above the workshop and lay down on the bedroll Haran had laid out for him in the second week.

He did not sleep for a while.

He thought of the speaker in Halver's Square. He thought of the woman in the thin coat. He thought of the two letters on the under-bench shelf and the broadsheet folded along the giver's fold, and he thought of Velden's flat eyes, and he thought of Othen saying *boy,* and he thought of Jora saying *the Wellspring says* in a voice that was patient and proud and entirely her own and entirely, at the same time, his.

He did not, in the moment, name what he was thinking.

He set it down.

He slept.

The reservoir sat. The wall held. The lamp in the workshop below burned, and the file moved across the brass, and somewhere at the far edge of the square an old woman crossed slowly with a covered pot, and the night, in Stonecurve, was the night the district had been having for two months now, the small ordinary night of a place that worked.

The shelf in the back of the room he set things on was, by morning, fuller than it had ever been.

He did not, by morning, lift the cloth.
