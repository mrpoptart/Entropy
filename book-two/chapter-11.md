# Chapter 11

The stairs to his parents' apartment were the stairs to his parents' apartment. Six flights. The third landing where the rail had loosened in the second month and his father had pinned it back with a wedge of pine cut from a furniture scrap. The wedge was still there. It had darkened a quarter shade.

Ash climbed with his hands in his coat pockets and his hands in his coat pockets to keep them off the rail, because the rail was clean and his hands were not. He had come from the Cooperage by way of the workshop and the workshop by way of the canal stair, and the grime on his knuckles was the grime of the redesign: lamp black at the cuticles, a smear of gasket compound along the side of the right thumb, a thin cut on the left index where the shears had caught him at the second hour and that he had forgotten until the cold of the stairwell found it.

He stopped at the fifth landing. He pulled a handkerchief from the inside pocket and worked at the worst of it. The compound came off in a black thread on the cloth. The lamp black did not. He folded the cloth and put it back.

He was late. He had told his mother the seventh hour. The seventh hour had passed at the canal stair.

He climbed the last flight.

The door opened before he reached for it. His father was holding it. His father had heard the step on the fifth landing the way his father always heard the step on the fifth landing, which was to say without seeming to hear it, without looking up from whatever he had been doing, and then standing up at the moment the foot found the sixth tread and crossing to the door in the time it took the foot to find the seventh.

"Ashlyn."

"I'm late."

"You are."

His father stepped aside. Ash went in.

The apartment held the smell of bread. Not the morning bread, which Ash had stopped catching, but the evening bread, the long-rise loaf his mother set the night before and pulled at the sixth hour, and the smell sat across the front room the way it had sat across the front room of the old apartment, the way it had sat across the front room of every apartment his mother had ever made bread in. Ash registered the smell and registered, behind it, the lamb. Onions. The thin sweetness of carrots cooked too long because his mother had been waiting for him.

The cracked bowl was on the sideboard. The dented kettle was on the stove. The singed curtain at the south window had been washed since the last time he had seen it and had come out of the wash slightly less singed and slightly more grey, which was not an improvement so much as a decision.

"Mum."

"Take your coat off."

She did not turn from the stove. She was at the pot with a wooden spoon and the spoon was working a slow figure across the bottom of the pot, and her left hand was on the handle, and her hip was set the way it set when the leg had been on its feet for too long. She was talking before the spoon stopped.

"I'm pulling the bread in a minute. I told your father a minute ago and I'll tell you now because nobody hears me the first time. The carrots have gone past where I wanted them. I left the lamb in the warming oven because I thought you'd be here at the seventh and you weren't, and the warming oven is fine for lamb if you don't mind it tighter than it should be. Hang your coat."

He hung his coat.

"Wash your hands."

He went to the basin. The basin was the basin from the new apartment, the shallow one with the lattice-fed tap, which his mother did not use. The pitcher his mother used was on the shelf above. He took the pitcher. He poured cold water into the basin and washed in the cold water and did not channel it. The lamp black came off in two passes. The cut at his finger opened and closed and opened again and he pressed it against the cloth until it gave up.

His father was at the table. His father had the small knife and the heel of yesterday's loaf and was cutting it into the soup bowls in the careful even slices of a man who had done it five thousand times.

"Sit down, Ashlyn," his mother said.

He sat down.

His father slid a bowl across.

"How was the canal."

"Cold."

"Mm."

"The pump on Six went."

His father set the knife down. His father did not pick it up again until the next bowl needed cutting, which was the next sentence away.

"Seal?"

"Seal."

"Brass or fused?"

"Fused."

"Mm."

His mother brought the pot to the table. She brought the pot herself and set it on the trivet herself and lifted the lid herself and the steam came up and crossed her face and she did not move her face out of it. She sat. She served his father first, because the order was the order, and then him, and then herself last, and then she sat.

"Eat."

He ate.

The lamb was tighter than it should have been. It was also good. His mother's lamb was always good in the way his mother's lamb had been good in the old apartment: the rosemary cut fine, the salt set early so the meat had drawn it, the carrots gone past the point a careful cook would have stopped them because his mother was not a careful cook, she was a kitchen-fluent one, and the carrots in his mother's stew tasted, always, like the long wait of the household for the third one to come home.

His father told a story.

His father told it the way his father told stories, which was the way a man placed bricks, one and then the next and then the next, and a small pause between because a brick had to set before the next brick went on it. The story was about Mern. Mern, two doors down, who had been on the masonry crew with his father in the third year and who had left masonry for the river barges in the fifth and who had come back from the barges last winter because the river had taken his shoulder and the shoulder had not come back the way the shoulder needed to. Mern had taken up cobbling. Cobbling, his father said, was not masonry, but it had this in common with masonry, that a man who had laid bricks could see how a sole was meant to sit on a last and could see, at the second look, what the cobbler down the lane was doing wrong.

"Wrong how."

"Nailing through. Should be tacking and sewing."

"Mm."

"Mern told him. The cobbler took it badly. Then the cobbler tried it Mern's way on a pair he'd already given up on, and the pair sold."

His mother snorted. Quiet. Into her bowl.

"Now Mern has a stool in the back of the cobbler's shop," his father said. "Two days a week. The cobbler tells people Mern is a friend who comes by. Mern tells me the cobbler is paying him under the bench."

"Under the bench."

"Mern's words."

His father lifted his spoon. He looked at Ash over the spoon.

"Like that," he said.

The *that* sat across the table for a moment. Ash did not chase it. His father did not unpack it. His mother said, without looking up:

"Like what, Rendell."

"Like a man finding the thing his hands already knew."

His mother went back to her lamb.

Ash ate.

* * *

Maren came in at the eighth.

She did not knock. She had a key, and the key was a key the household had given her at fourteen and that she had carried through three apartments without remarking on it, and the lock turned and the door opened and she was in the room before the door had finished its arc.

"Mum."

"Maren."

"I'm late."

"Sit down."

She did not sit. She stood in the doorway of the front room with her satchel still on the shoulder and her coat still buttoned, and Ash watched her register the table and the people at the table and the bowl set for her and she did, in the registering, the small thing she did, which was the resetting of her face from corps-face to family-face. The reset took half a beat longer tonight than it usually took. He marked the half-beat and did not mark it.

She set the satchel down.

She got the coat off in two motions instead of one. The second motion caught at the cuff and she worked it through, and the working-through was not effort exactly, it was the small slow of a hand that was running on a budget, and he looked at his bowl while she did it.

She sat.

She sat heavily. The chair gave its small particular complaint, the one that meant the back leg had been loose since the move and his father had not fixed it because his mother had told him not to, because his mother had a theory about the chair, which was that the chair was fine and the floor was uneven, and his father, who knew the chair was not fine, deferred.

"Lamb," his mother said. She was already serving.

"A little."

"A little."

A little was a quarter of what his mother had served Ash. His mother served the quarter without comment. His mother set the bowl down in front of Maren and went back to her own bowl and did not look at the bowl in front of Maren again.

"Tea?" his mother said.

"No."

"Mm."

A beat.

"Yes," Maren said. "Please."

His mother did not answer. His mother stood. The kettle was already warm because the kettle had been on the back of the stove since the bread had come out, and his mother lifted it and poured and the tea went into the cup and the cup went across to Maren, and Maren took it.

She did not drink. She set both hands around it. She set them around it the way a person set hands around a heat source, which was the wrong way for a channeler at a kitchen table to set hands around a teacup, because a channeler at a kitchen table did not need the cup to warm the hands, the hands warmed the cup. Ash watched her watch her hands. She did not look up. She wrapped the fingers around the ceramic and held them there and the ceramic was hot enough that a person who was not borrowing from it should have flinched.

His father was telling another piece of the Mern story. The cobbler's wife. The cobbler's wife's opinion. The cobbler's wife's opinion of Mern. Ash heard it and did not hear it. His mother heard it and did not hear it. His mother was at her bowl with her spoon in her right hand, and her left hand had moved, in the time the kettle had been poured, from the table to her lap to the edge of the table again, and the fingers were resting at the edge of the table the way fingers rested at the edge of a table when they were waiting to be told what to do.

Nobody said anything.

"How's the work," his mother said.

"Work."

"Maren."

"It's work, Mum."

"Mm."

"The lower-district inspection cycle finished at the fifteenth. We're behind on the shoring of, of the eastern cisterns. They want me to take a second crew next month. I told them I'd think about it."

"You told them you'd think about it."

"I told them I'd think about it."

His mother did not press. His father did not press. Ash watched Maren over his bowl and Maren did not look at him.

"And the redesign," his mother said. To Ash now. The pivot was clean. "What did Haran say when you brought it home."

"He said it would hold."

"He said it would hold."

"He said other things."

"Mm."

Ash put a piece of lamb in his mouth. His father had set his spoon down. His father was looking at the table the way his father looked at the table when his father was not done with the previous subject and was making, by the looking, the small placeholder a careful man made.

"Davan," his father said.

"He was at the bench three nights ago."

"And."

"He told me about a girl in the harbor quarter who isn't going to be a healer. He told it the way a man tells a thing to give it to someone. I took it."

His father nodded once.

His father did not nod twice. His father had a one-nod limit on subjects he was not done with, and the second nod was held back, because the second nod would have been an ending, and his father was not ending it.

"The pump went today," Ash said. To break the held nod, because he could not sit under it. "Building Six. The seal I'd fused in the second week. I went over and patched it. I told the crew the pattern. There are nine more like it in the western line."

"Nine."

"Nine that I can think of from the bench."

"Mm."

His mother served Maren another small portion of carrots without asking. Maren ate one of them.

"It's good, Mum," she said.

"Mm."

"The carrots are good."

"They went past."

"They're good."

"They went past, Maren. Eat them anyway."

Maren ate two more.

She set the spoon down. She set it down too carefully. She set it down at the precise angle a person set a tool down when the person was conscious of the placing, and Ash had seen Maren put a spoon down without thinking about a spoon her entire life, and the conscious placing was the new thing in the room, and it sat there.

"I should," she started, and stopped.

She picked up the cup.

"I'll wash up," she said.

"Sit," his mother said.

"Mum."

"You sit."

"I'll wash up. Ash will help."

His mother looked at his father. His father said nothing. His father's stillness now had the quality of a man who was holding a door open for a daughter and not naming the door.

"Both of you," his mother said. "The trivet too. The trivet has burnt at the underside again and I cannot see under it from this side of the table."

"The trivet."

"The trivet, Maren."

Maren stood.

She stood and the standing was a procedure. She put her hand on the back of the chair and pushed up off the hand and the chair did its small complaint, and she crossed to the sideboard and set the cup down on the sideboard and turned for the kitchen, and Ash, who had been about to follow, watched the turn and saw, at the turn, the small adjustment of her shoulder, the registering of distance, the calculation of how many steps were left between her and the sink. He had never seen Maren calculate a distance to a sink.

He picked up his bowl and his father's bowl and followed her in.

* * *

The kitchen was the kitchen. The narrow one off the front room with the window that looked at the back of the next building's chimney and the shelf above the basin with the pitcher and the soft-iron rack his father had bent in the third week of the move because the cabinet over the basin had been hung at the wrong height for his mother and his father had not raised the cabinet, he had lowered the rack, because lowering the rack had been the thing he could do that day.

Maren was at the basin.

She had her hands flat on the lip of it. She was looking at the basin the way a person looked at a basin before doing the thing that was about to happen at the basin. He set the bowls on the counter behind her. He did not stand behind her. He stood at the counter at her left, and he picked up the cloth from the rack, and he held the cloth and waited.

She drew.

He felt it the way he felt a draw in a room, not as a thing in him but as a thing in the room, the small particular shift in the air a person who had been around channelers his whole life had learned to recognize before he had any idea what he was recognizing. The water was meant to come up out of the cistern feed and into the basin and warm in the rising. That was the thing she had done at this basin and at the basin in the old apartment and at every basin in every apartment for fifteen years.

The water did not come.

She held the draw. He saw her jaw set. He did not look at her jaw, he saw it at the edge of his vision, which was the only place a brother could see a sister's jaw set in a kitchen at the eighth hour, and he kept his eyes on the cloth.

The water came.

It came late. It came in the wrong pulse, a stutter, the way a tap came when air was in the line, and Maren caught it on the second pulse and pulled it through and the basin filled, and the steam came up off it later than the steam should have come, and the heat in the steam was not the heat of a basin she had warmed, it was the heat of a basin she had warmed *on the second try.*

She picked up a plate.

She washed it.

Her hands were shaking.

The shake was the small shake of a hand that had spent the budget it had been given, that was not, in the spending, asking the body for any more, but the body was being asked anyway. The plate went under the water and came up and she set it on the rack and she reached for the next.

He stepped forward.

He did not take the plate. He took the plate she had already set on the rack and he picked up the cloth and he dried it. The cloth went around the plate twice. He set the plate on the shelf. He took the next plate from the rack.

She washed.

He dried.

The kitchen was the kitchen. The window was the window. His father's voice was a low murmur from the front room, the second piece of the Mern story now, the cobbler's wife's brother, who had a stake in the cobbler shop, his mother's quiet hum of acknowledgment between the placed sentences. The sounds came in through the doorway and stopped at the threshold of the kitchen as if the kitchen had a different weather.

A plate. A bowl. Another bowl. A spoon.

He dried.

Maren reached for the trivet. The trivet was at the back of the sink and had the burnt mark his mother had named, and Maren reached past the burn and picked it up by the handle and the handle slipped half an inch in her grip before the fingers closed, and she set it down again and picked it up the second time with both hands, and she washed it the second time without looking at him.

He took it from the rack.

He dried it.

He set it on the shelf.

He took the next thing.

She did not look up.

"Don't," she said.

"Don't what."

"Whatever you're about to say. I know what you're noticing. *Don't.*"

He folded the cloth. He folded it once and then again and set it on the rack at the angle his mother set it, which was not the angle Maren set it, and he picked up the next plate.

He dried it.

He did not say anything.

He could feel the not-saying in his chest the way he had felt the not-saying in his chest at fourteen and at sixteen and at nineteen, every time the kitchen had been the kitchen and the choreography had been the choreography and the thing they did not talk about had been the thing they did not talk about. The choreography was older than him. The choreography had been built across his lifetime by two children who had each, separately, decided that the wall between them would be load-bearing, and had agreed without agreeing to keep their hands off it. He was holding a cloth. She was washing a cup. The cup went on the rack. He took the cup. He dried the cup.

She handed him the next plate without putting it on the rack.

The handing was a thing.

It was a small thing. It was the smallest thing that had happened in the kitchen tonight, and it was a thing, because Maren had washed dishes at his mother's basin for fifteen years and had never once handed him a plate. The plates went on the rack. He took them off the rack. The system was the system. The handing-across was a half-second saved and a half-second was, tonight, a half-second she did not have.

He took it.

He dried it.

He set it on the shelf.

She handed him the next.

They worked the rest of the stack that way. Plate, plate, bowl, bowl, two cups, the spoons, the trivet again because she had set it down on the wrong rack, the small paring knife she had used for the carrots that morning, the wooden spoon that was older than him. The water cooled. She did not warm it again. He did not offer to warm it. He had warmed it for her at the basin in the old apartment when he was eleven, the one and only time, by leaning across her and putting his hand on the rim of the basin and pretending it was not what it was, and she had not spoken to him for the rest of the evening, and he had never tried again.

The last plate went on the shelf.

She drained the basin.

She drained it by hand. She lifted the small plug at the corner and let the water down the way a person without a pool let water down, which was the way she had washed her hands at fourteen and the way his mother washed her hands every day, and she did not, in the draining, channel anything. She wiped the basin with the cloth she pulled from his hand and she set the cloth on the rack at his mother's angle.

She dried her hands on her front.

"Tea's gone cold," she said.

"Mum will warm it."

"Mum will warm it."

She did not move.

He did not move.

The kitchen held them. The two of them at the basin, a foot apart, the window over her shoulder, the chimney of the next building a darker dark in the dark. The choreography that had been the load-bearing wall of every interaction since he was old enough to know what a wall was had been, in the last quarter of an hour, pulled out from under them, and they were standing on what was left, and what was left was a kitchen floor with a wet rag on a rack.

She turned.

She went past him.

She did not touch him going past, which was the way she went past him, which was what Maren did, which was what Maren had always done, and the not-touching was, tonight, a kindness, because if she had touched him he would not have known what to do with the touching.

He stayed at the basin a minute longer.

He picked up the cloth. He folded it again, the same fold, the angle she had set, the angle his mother set. He put it on the rack.

He went back to the front room.

* * *

His father was still telling the Mern story.

His father had moved on to a piece that was either later in the chronology or earlier. Ash could not tell. The cobbler's apprentice had said something to Mern in the second week and Mern had not said anything back, and the not-saying-back had been, his father said, the moment the apprentice had decided he was going to learn from Mern, and the cobbler had not yet noticed.

"Like the file," his father said, to the room.

"The file."

"The one you wore through."

"I remember the file."

"You filed that blade for a week."

"It was a chisel."

"Was it."

"It was your chisel. You'd given up on it."

"I'd given up on it because the steel was bad."

"The steel was fine."

"The steel was fine," his father agreed. "I'd given up on it because I was tired."

His father had never said that before. Not in the twenty years of the chisel story. The chisel had been bad steel and Ash had brought it back and the bringing-back had been the named act, and the bad steel had been the frame that held the named act, and his father had, tonight, in front of his sister at the table holding a cooling cup, taken the frame down.

His father did not look at Maren.

His mother did.

His mother's look was not a look. It was a glance that came up off the bowl and went across the table and went back down again and the going-down was the part of the glance that mattered, because it was the part where his mother had seen what she had seen and was, in the going-down, deciding what to do with it, which was nothing, which was tonight's nothing, which was not last week's nothing.

"I should go," Maren said.

"You haven't finished your tea."

"It's cold."

"I'll warm it."

"Mum."

"Sit, Maren. Two more minutes. Your father isn't done."

His father took the cue.

His father told the last piece of the Mern story, which was not about Mern, which was about the cobbler, who had told his father at the end of the second month that the shop was doing better, and had not, in the telling, said why. His father unpacked the not-saying. His father unpacked it gently. The unpacking took ninety seconds. At the end of the ninety seconds Maren had drunk half the tea. His mother had not warmed it. The tea had warmed itself in the holding of it, which it could not have done, except that there were three other channelers at the table and the room was warm, and a cup that sat in a warm room came up half a degree on its own.

Maren stood.

She stood better the second time. She had budgeted for the standing. She crossed to the door and got her coat on in two motions and the second motion did not catch at the cuff, because the second motion was the motion of a Maren who had recovered the half-degree of margin, and Ash watched the recovery and did not name it.

"Mum."

"Maren."

His mother did not stand. His mother's leg had the stiffness it had at the eighth hour, and his mother did not stand at the eighth hour to walk a daughter to a door, because his mother had walked her own self to her own doors her whole life. Maren bent. Maren put a hand on his mother's shoulder. His mother put her hand over Maren's. The two hands sat there for the count of three.

"Eat tomorrow," his mother said.

"Mum."

"Eat tomorrow, Maren. I'll bring bread to your office."

"Don't bring bread to my office."

"Mm."

His father stood. His father walked Maren to the door. At the door his father did the thing his father did, which was the hand on the shoulder, held a beat longer than the conversation expected, and Maren stood under the hand and did not lean into it and did not pull away from it, and his father took the hand back at the count of four.

"Ash."

"I'll walk you down."

"You don't have to."

"I'm going."

"Ash."

"I'm going, Maren."

She did not argue.

* * *

The stair was the stair.

He went down ahead of her. He went down a step ahead and to the left, because a step ahead and to the left was the place a person stood for a person who might not trust a stair, and he did not announce the standing-there and she did not name it, and they went down that way to the fifth landing and the fourth and the third where the wedge of pine was. She put her hand on the rail at the third landing. She had not put her hand on the rail at any other landing.

At the bottom he opened the door for her.

The street was cold. The cold came in at the door and met them on the threshold and Maren pulled her coat at the collar with one hand the way a person pulled a coat when the person was not going to channel against the cold, and she stepped out.

He stepped out after her.

"I can walk myself."

"I know."

"Then go up."

"In a minute."

She did not argue the minute.

They stood at the top of the steps. The lamp at the corner was on. The lamp was the lamp the lighter had set at the sixth hour, oil now, lattice last winter, oil since the spring because the lattice had stopped holding, and the wick had been trimmed badly and the flame was a half-flame above the half-cup. Maren looked at the lamp.

"The wick."

"Mm."

"Rakel does that lamp. She used to do it right."

"Pool's not what it was."

"It's not the pool. It's the trim."

"Mm."

She did not look at him. He did not look at her. They were doing the thing. They were both doing it. The doing of the thing had been, tonight, the line they had walked up to and stepped back from in a kitchen, and they were doing it again now, here, in the cold, at a lamp, because the doing of the thing was what they had, and tonight was not the night to give it up, even though tonight was the night that had told them, both of them, that they were going to have to.

"Goodnight, Ash."

"Goodnight."

She went left.

He watched her to the corner. She did not look back. He had not expected her to look back. At the corner she stopped, and the stopping was not for him, the stopping was for the curb, which was a curb, and she stepped down it carefully and went on, and the carefully was a thing he had not seen before, and he marked it.

He did not go up.

He stood on the step a minute longer.

He thought about the twenty-three years.

He thought about them not in the order they had been lived in but in the order a person thought of years on a step at the eighth hour when his sister had just gone left, which was as a stack of small competences. The way to climb a stair when the stair would not lift you. The way to carry water from a tap when the tap was a tap. The way to find the warmth in a room without making it. The way to test a chair before sitting on it. The way to feel the give in a flagstone with the ball of the foot. The way to dry your hands on your front. The way to set a cloth on a rack at the angle a mother set a cloth. The way to hold a cup and let the cup do what a cup did. The way to lose, every day, the small private arguments with a world that had not been built for you, and to lose them quietly, and to walk on.

He had built those, none of them on purpose, all of them by living a life in the only way his life was available to be lived. He had built them, and Maren had not, because Maren had not had to.

He turned the offer over in his head.

It would land badly.

It would land badly tonight and worse tomorrow and worse in any moment in which Maren was the person who could still hold the cup without asking it to do anything for her. The day he could give her what he had was the day she could not pretend to herself she did not need it, and that day was not tonight. That day was being walked toward, with care, on a curb, by a Maren who had calculated the stepping-down.

He did not know when the day would be.

He knew he would have it ready.

The conversation in the kitchen had not been an unspoken conversation anymore. That was the thing. He had said *don't what* and she had said *whatever you're about to say,* and the saying-of-it had moved the conversation from the wall they had built into a thing in the room with them, and a thing in the room could not be put back into a wall. It could be set down. It could be left for later. It could be picked up by either of them at a different basin in a different kitchen at a different hour. But it was not a wall anymore.

He turned to go up.

He stopped at the door.

He looked back, the way a person looked back without meaning to, at the front room window. The window was lit. The lamp on the table was lit and the lamp by the window was lit and the curtain was the singed curtain washed grey, and through the curtain, in the warm narrow of the room, his mother was at the sideboard.

She had the cracked bowl in her hands.

She was not doing anything with it. She was holding it. She had it cupped in the way a person held a thing that had been carried through more than one apartment, and her thumb was at the crack, and she was looking down at the bowl, and her face, in the lamplight, through the curtain, was the face of a woman who was alone in a kitchen at the eighth hour because her son had walked her daughter down the stairs and her husband had gone to wash, and the room had emptied, and she had picked up the bowl.

She lifted her tea with the other hand. She drank. The drinking warmed her face by a degree, the way tea did, and she set the cup down and went on holding the bowl.

He did not go up.

He stood on the step in the cold and watched the window for the count of ten. His mother did not move. She held the bowl. She held it the way she had held it after the earthquake, the way she had held it the morning of the move, the way she held everything that had been in her hands long enough to belong to the hands. Her pool was steady. Her hands were steady. The bowl was cracked.

He turned.

He went down the street.

The image went with him. The lamp, the curtain, the bowl in his mother's hands. He carried it the way he carried all the small things, which was unsorted, on the surface, available for the sorting later, and tonight he did not sort. Tonight he walked. The cold was the cold. The cut on his finger had closed. His hands were in his coat pockets and his hands were in his coat pockets to keep them warm, because nobody was warming them for him, and he had, somewhere along the years, learned how to walk with his hands in his pockets without thinking about it.

He walked home.
