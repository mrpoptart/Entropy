# Chapter 14

He went down the curve in the morning the way he had gone up it the night before, slow, with his hands in his pockets, and the city was the city.

He had not slept. He had lain on the cot in the back of the workshop and listened to the wind work the loose tile on the roof, the one Haran had been meaning to set, and the working of the tile had been the working of the tile, and at some point the wind had gone and the tile had been quiet, and the quiet had been worse. He had got up before the lamp wanted lighting and washed his face at the basin and stood with the cloth in his hands a long time before he had used it.

He had not told Haran where he was going.

Haran had been at his bench when Ash came through, the way Haran was at his bench when Ash came through, and Haran had looked at him over the wire and not asked, and Ash had not said, and the not-asking and the not-saying had been the thing.

The streets were wet. It had rained at some hour he had not heard, a thin spring rain that had laid the dust and not done anything else, and the cobbles held the wet at the seams. He passed the bakery at the fourth-hour corner. He did not stop. The girl at the counter was the girl at the counter and she saw him and she did not call out, because the not-calling-out was what people did now when they saw him on a street, and the not-calling-out was a kind of room they were giving him.

He went past the cloth stall. He went past the corner with the chalked list that the boys had washed off twice and chalked again. He came down to the second bridge and crossed it.

He did not go to the south block.

He went west, past the canal, into the lower terraces where the brick was older and the streets ran narrower and the buildings leaned toward each other across the lanes the way buildings leaned when nobody had straightened them in a hundred years. He had not been down here in months. He had not been down here, properly, in more than a year. The smell of the lower terraces was the smell he remembered, which was bread and lamp oil and the wet stone of the old walls, and the smell did not arrange itself into anything that was not what it was.

He came to the lane where Ryn's workshop was.

The workshop was not a workshop the way Haran's was a workshop. It was a yard, mostly, with a low shed at the back and a long bench under an awning along one wall and a rack of pipe lengths under the eaves. The gate was open. He stopped at the gate.

He stood at the gate a count he did not count.

There were three people in the yard. A woman his mother's age was sitting on a crate with a kettle in her lap, watching. A man in a leather apron was at the far end of the bench, sorting fittings into a wooden tray. And Ryn was on her knees at the bench, with her sleeves pushed up, working at a coupling clamped in a vise.

She had not seen him.

She had her head bent and her hair pulled back the way she pulled it back when she was working, in a knot at the base of her neck with a length of cord. The cord had been blue once. It was the color of nothing now. She was talking to the woman on the crate without looking at her, fast, low, and the woman was laughing the small dry laugh of a person who was tired and being entertained and was glad of both.

He stepped into the yard.

The man in the apron looked up first. He looked at Ash and he did not perform anything with his face. He went back to his fittings. The woman on the crate looked up, and her hand on the kettle went still, and then she went back to looking at the kettle the way a person went back to a thing she had been looking at after a thing had passed in front of her she had decided not to be in the way of.

Ryn did not look up.

"Hand me the small wrench," she said. Not at the man in the apron. Not at the woman on the crate.

He waited a half count. The wrenches were in a roll on the bench, three of them, in three sizes. He took the small one out of the roll. He stepped to the bench and set it in her hand.

Her hand closed on it without her looking.

"Thanks."

She worked the coupling. She worked it the way a person worked a coupling she had worked a thousand times, with the wrench short and her wrist turned in close, three quarter-turns and a check and three more, and the check was the thing, and the check was what told her when to stop, and she stopped. She set the wrench on the bench. She put her palm flat on the coupling and held it a count and took the palm away.

"Margit," she said. The woman on the crate looked up. "Pour."

Margit poured from the kettle into a small tin cup beside her on the crate. Ryn watched the pour. Ryn nodded once. Margit set the kettle down.

"That'll hold to the end of the week."

"You said end of the week last time," Margit said.

"I did."

"It held three days."

"Three days is part of a week."

The man in the apron made a sound that was a laugh without being one. Margit shook her head at the kettle and put her hand over the lid and lifted it and looked inside and put it back.

"Ryn."

"End of next week," Ryn said. "Honest."

"Mm."

"If it goes before then I'll come look at it for nothing."

"I'd hope so."

"You'd hope wrong, but I'm being generous."

Margit got off the crate. She held the kettle in both hands, the way a woman held a thing she was carrying back up a stair, and she went out of the yard with it without saying goodbye, because the goodbye had been the lifting of the kettle, and Ryn had said *go* with the angle of her chin without saying *go,* and the going was the going.

Ryn wiped her hands on a rag from her belt.

She turned, finally, and looked at him.

She looked at him a count, and her face did the small adjustment her face did when she was looking at something she had already taken in and was now choosing what to do with, and she did not, in the looking, perform anything.

"You look terrible," Ryn said.

He laughed.

"Yeah," he said.

"When did you last sleep."

"Mm."

"That's not a number."

"No."

"Eat?"

"This morning," Ash said.

"That's a no."

"It's a mm."

"It's a no, Ash."

She crossed the yard to the shed and came back with a heel of bread from a cloth on a shelf, and she handed it to him, and she did not, in the handing, make it a thing.

"Eat," Ryn said. "I'll finish this. Then we'll walk."

"Ryn."

"Eat the bread."

He ate the bread.

He ate it standing at the corner of the bench while she went back to the coupling, because the coupling was not done, the holding of the palm had been a check and not a finish, and there was a fitting on the far end she was wanting to seat. He watched her work. The man in the apron, whose name he did not know, finished his sorting and took the tray into the shed and came back without it and went out of the yard the way a man went out of a yard at the end of his work, with a small tilt of the chin at Ryn, and Ryn returned the tilt without looking up.

The bread was good. It was a day old and it was good.

She finished the coupling. She washed her hands at a basin in the corner of the yard, with a thin line of water from a tap that ran when she opened it because someone, somewhere, was channeling for the lower terraces today, and Ash watched the water and did not, this time, calculate the channel. She dried her hands on the front of her shirt. She came back to the bench. She rolled the wrench roll closed and tied it. She took her coat down from a peg by the shed.

"Walk," Ryn said.

"Where."

"Up the lane. Down to the old gate. Around. The way we used to."

"All right."

She went out of the yard. She did not lock the gate. She pulled it to and looped a length of twine over a nail, and the twine was the lock, and the lock was the lock the lower terraces had, and she went down the lane and he went with her.

* * *

They walked.

The lane gave on the longer street that ran along the back of the bakeries, and the bakeries were running, and the smell of them was the smell that had been the smell of his childhood without his noticing it, the way the smell of a place was a smell a person did not notice until he had been gone a while and come back. They passed the second bakery. The boy at the door, sweeping, raised his broom an inch at Ryn, and Ryn raised her hand the same inch, and they went on.

She did not ask.

She walked the way she walked, which was at a pace a person could keep up with without thinking about keeping up, and her hands were in her coat pockets, and she did not, with her face or her shoulder or the angle of her chin, ask. She walked. He walked.

He did not start at the start.

He started in the middle, because the middle was where he was, and the middle was Davan's *you're asking the drowning to be patient while you decide which lifeboat to build,* and the middle was Sera in her sister's bed, and the middle was the laundry running on the warmth of a girl easing on a stool, and the middle was Branner alone in a yard at the handle of a pump that worked at eighty percent, and he started there.

He talked.

He talked the way he had not talked in months, in the kind of talking that did not arrange itself and did not hold itself to a point, where one thing led to another not because it followed but because it was the next thing in the chest. He talked about Davan. He talked about the brass cap polished in the empty square. He talked about the broadsheets under the weight on the bench. He talked about Edda's hands going in and out of her pockets. He talked about Dorenne, the part he had been not-talking-about for weeks, the long meeting in her office where she had sat on her side of the desk and he had sat on his and the desk had been the room. He talked about the cracked bowl that his mother had said *don't* about. He talked about Tesha at the next bench who had asked him a question he had not answered, and who had not asked it again, and whose not-asking-again had been a kind of asking.

He talked about himself.

He talked about how he did not know where he was meant to stand. He talked about the people he respected, two of them, who had broken against each other across a street and across a lane, and how he had stood between them and had not been able to be what either of them needed him to be, and how the not-being had not been a refusal, and how the not-being also had not been an absence, and he did not have a word for what the not-being had been.

He talked about doing the right thing in the wrong way.

He talked about the wrong thing in the right way.

He talked about not being able, anymore, to tell which was which. About how the people he was trying to help had not asked him to help them, and how the help, when he had brought it, had been a thing they had taken because the taking had been the thing left to them, and the taking was not the same as a wanting, and he did not know how to tell whether a thing was a help when the people it was for had not been the ones to name it.

He talked.

Ryn walked.

They went down the long lane and crossed at the corner where the wall had been patched in winter, the patch a different brick from the rest, and they went under the low arch that had been the old gate before the city had outgrown the old gate and not bothered to take it down, and the arch was the arch, and Ryn touched the keystone with two fingers as she went under it the way she had touched it as a child, and the touching was a thing she did not look at.

She let him talk a long time.

She let him talk past the old gate and along the wall and down to the corner where the canal came up out of the culvert, and the canal was running thin, and the thinness of the canal was a thing they both saw and did not name, and she let him talk past that. She let him talk until his talking had run itself down, and he stopped.

They walked another half block.

She put her hand on his wrist for a count.

"Sit," Ryn said.

There was a low wall along the canal where the wall had been built up against flooding two generations ago and had not been needed in a generation. They sat. She sat first. He sat next to her. The wall was cold through his coat. He did not move.

She did not say anything for a count.

Then she said it.

"You became someone else. You left. I didn't know if you were coming back."

She said it the way a person said a thing she had rehearsed and had then decided not to rehearse, in the plain order of what had been the case, with no edge on any of the words. He did not, at first, hear the words. He heard the cadence of them. The cadence was the cadence of a report.

"I watched you save the city from the earthquake. Everyone watched. It was the most impressive thing I've ever seen."

He looked at the canal.

"And afterward I went home and warmed my tea and thought: he doesn't need me to do that anymore. He doesn't need anyone."

He did not move his hands. He did not take them out of his pockets. The wall was cold and he did not feel the cold of the wall now, and the not-feeling was not a numbness. The not-feeling was the thing his body did when his attention had gone all the way out of it, into another place, into the small quiet kitchen on the lower terraces where a woman his age had stood at a stove and warmed a kettle and held a cup in her two hands.

"And I missed you."

She said it.

She said it without looking at him, with her face turned to the canal the way his face was turned to the canal, and the saying was not a confession and was not a complaint and was not anything but the saying of the thing.

"Not the Wellspring. You."

The canal ran.

It ran thin and slow and it caught the grey light at its edge, and a leaf went past on the surface and went under and did not come up, and a bird he did not see called from the wall on the other side, and the bird called twice and stopped.

He did not, for a long count, speak.

When he spoke, his voice was the voice that came out of him.

"I'm sorry," he said.

"I know."

She did not turn her face.

"You were sorry the last time too," Ryn said. "When we ran into each other and you were already halfway out the door. Being sorry isn't the same as being here."

"I'm here now."

"Are you?"

He looked at her then. She did not look at him.

"Or are you here because the other places didn't work out?"

He did not answer.

He did not answer because the answer was the answer, and the answer was not a clean one, and he was not going to give her a clean one across the canal where the leaf had gone under and not come up. He sat. He put his hands on his knees and looked at the back of his hands. The cut at the base of his thumb had closed clean and the skin was new and pink at the line of it. He turned his hands over and looked at his palms. The palms were the palms.

"I came because I didn't know where else," he said.

"Mm."

"That's not better."

"No."

"I know it isn't."

"It's also not nothing."

"Okay," he said.

"Okay."

They sat.

A man went past behind them on the lane with a barrow. The barrow's wheel had a flat at one place on the rim and the flat made a small hard sound on the cobbles every quarter turn, and the sound went past, and the sound went small, and the sound went away.

"Walk more," she said. "I'm cold."

"Yes."

They got up. She put her hand at the small of his back for a half count as he stood, the way she had done when they were fourteen and had been sitting too long on a wall and had decided to go, and the half count was the half count, and her hand was gone, and she was walking, and he walked.

* * *

They went the long way back.

She took him up the lane behind her workshop and along the upper terraces, the streets that ran level with the canal at the higher water mark, and the upper terraces were where the heating lattice came up from below and ran under the brick in the long zigzag pattern that the old engineers had laid out before any of them had been born. The brick was patched in three places he could see. The patches were new, the mortar pale, the edges not yet weathered.

"Three winters," Ryn said, without his asking. "Three winters they've patched it three places. The patches don't hold. They patch the patches."

"Who's on it," Ash said.

"On what."

"The lattice. Who's maintaining it."

She did not answer at first. She walked. She nodded at a woman in a doorway who was beating a rug, and the woman nodded back without stopping the beating, and they went past.

"Was Esten," Ryn said. "Esten and his apprentice. Esten retired in autumn. The apprentice was reassigned. Now it's a man who comes from the second district when he comes."

"When does he come."

"When he comes, Ash."

"Right."

"He was supposed to come in the third week of the month. He didn't. Nobody knows why. He'll come when he comes. The lattice is cold by mid-afternoon and Margit, who you saw, who I just patched a kettle for, sits with her coat on after the third hour because the lattice runs out and the room runs cold and she's seventy-one and her hands hurt."

"Margit's the one you said."

"Margit's the one I said."

They walked.

"There's a woman in my building," Ryn said. "Two floors down. Hira. Younger than us. She has a baby and a job at the press. She channels for the press, eight hours, comes home, and the building is cold from mid-afternoon and she can't, she's tired, she can't push her own pool to warm the room and the baby. So she puts the baby in her coat and sits at the window and waits for the sun on the brick across the lane, because the sun on the brick across the lane warms the brick a degree by the fourth hour, and the brick keeps it past sunset, and Hira times the feeding to the brick across the lane."

"Ryn."

"That's how it is. That's what's happening. That's the lattice failing."

"I know."

"You know."

"I know."

"You know it the way you know a number. Hira knows it the way a woman with a baby in a cold flat knows it."

"Yes."

"You asked who's on it. Nobody is on it. That's the answer. The channelers who used to be on it have been reassigned to higher-priority districts, which means the districts where the people the Hall thinks matter live, which doesn't include the upper terraces, which has not included the upper terraces in any list anybody who lives here has seen."

"All right."

"I'm not telling you to fix it."

"I know."

"I'm not."

"I know, Ryn."

"I don't want you to fix it. I don't want a redistribution. I don't want a mechanical pump. I want, what I want is, what I want is for the world to stop getting harder, and I know it isn't going to."

She said it walking. She said it without breaking the pace, and the pace held, and a boy came past with a goat on a length of twine and the goat looked at them and went on, and the lane bent and they took the bend.

"I'm not going to give you that," he said.

"I know you're not."

"I don't have it to give."

"You have a lot of it to give. You don't have all of it. I know the difference. So do you."

"Yes."

"I'm not asking for it anyway. I told you what I want. I told you because you asked. I didn't tell you because I expected anything from telling."

"Mm."

"You can carry it," Ryn said. "Or you can put it down. I don't care. I'm carrying it because it's mine. I told you because it's also yours. That's all."

He did not answer.

He walked. The lattice ran under their feet here in the thin form, the form that came up out of the lower brick into the upper terraces, and he could feel through his boots the place where it was running and the place where it was not, and the not-running was longer than the running, and he had not been counting and he was now counting, and the counting was a thing his attention did when his attention did not want to be doing the thing it was doing.

"Ryn," he said.

"Mm."

"What do I do."

He said it because he said it.

He did not say it the way he had said it to himself a hundred times over the past month, the way a man said a question to himself in the small hours when he was not going to answer it. He said it to her. He said it the way a man asked a person across a kitchen table to pass him a thing, in the plain voice of a man who was asking and was waiting for what the other person was going to say.

She did not answer for a count.

She did not stop walking. She turned her face toward him for the first time since they had left the canal wall, and she looked at him sideways with her chin tucked down a half inch the way she looked when she was about to tell him a thing she had been waiting to tell him for a long time and had not been sure he was going to ask.

"I don't know."

"All right."

"But I know what you don't do."

"All right."

"You don't disappear again."

"All right."

"You don't decide you know the answer and build it alone and forget that the people you're building for have opinions."

He looked at the cobbles.

"You did that with Dorenne," Ryn said.

"Ryn."

"You did it in the district. You're doing the thing you always do, you find the smart answer, you build it, and you wonder why nobody's grateful."

"That's not, "

"It is."

"That's not what, "

"It is, Ash."

She stopped walking.

She stopped at a corner where the lane gave on a small square with a well in the middle that the upper terraces had drawn from for two hundred years and which was now drawn from less because the channeled mains came up to the edge of the square and the people had moved their drawing to the mains, because a channeled main was warm and a well was cold. The well was in the middle of the square. The cover was on. The cover had moss along the south rim.

"You built a water system for people who didn't ask for a water system," Ryn said.

"They needed, "

"You built it brilliantly. You trained them to operate it. You never asked what they actually needed. Maybe they needed a water system. Maybe they needed someone to listen first. Maybe they needed both. Maybe they needed the listening before the building so that the building was a building they had a part in and not a building you handed them."

"I asked, "

"You asked Branner. Once. After. You asked Branner what the rhythm should be on the handle and Branner told you and you adjusted. That's not asking, Ash. That's checking your work."

"I, "

"I'm not saying the pump is bad."

"All right."

"I'm saying the pump is the answer to a question that you wrote and then asked yourself and then answered. I'm saying Dorenne wrote a different question and answered it the same way. I'm saying Davan is writing a third question and is answering it the same way too. None of you are asking the question. The question is what the people you're for need, and the answer is in their mouths, and you, none of you, have spent enough time with your mouths shut."

He looked at the well.

He looked at the moss along the south rim. The moss was the deep moss of a thing that had been in shade a long time and not been moved. There was a small fissure in the cover where the wood had split along the grain and a finger of light went down into the well.

"That's hard to hear," he said.

"Yes."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me."

"I'm thanking you."

"Don't. Thanking me makes it a gift," Ryn said. "It wasn't a gift. It was a thing I had been thinking for a year and you finally stood still long enough for me to say it."

"All right."

"All right."

A woman came across the square with a bucket. She did not go to the well. She went to the corner where the channeled main came up against the wall, and she put the bucket under the spout, and she turned the tap, and water came, and she stood with her hand on the tap and watched the bucket, and when the bucket was three-quarters full she turned the tap off, because the carrying was easier at three-quarters, and she lifted the bucket and went back the way she had come.

Ryn watched the woman.

"I'd have done it the same," she said.

"What."

"Three-quarters. The carrying is easier."

"Yes."

"I'm not better than any of them, Ash. I'm not the conscience of the district. I'm just the person who knows you and is willing to tell you because you came down the curve and asked and I had it ready."

"I know."

"Walk me home."

"Yes."

* * *

They walked the last stretch in a long quiet.

The quiet was not the quiet of a thing being avoided. The quiet was the quiet of a thing having been said. They went down out of the upper terraces and along the wall and past the bakery and down her lane, and the lane was the lane, and a girl was sitting on a step with a book on her knees, and the girl looked up at Ryn and waved with two fingers, and Ryn waved with two fingers back.

They came to her door.

Her door was the door he remembered. The paint had gone since he had last stood in front of it, gone in the way paint went in a damp lane, in patches at the bottom panel where the rain came up off the cobbles and worked at the wood. The number had been repainted, recently, in white, and the painting of the number was the kind of small care a person took when she was not going to be moving and was going to be in the door a long time.

She turned at the door.

She did not put her hand on the handle. She turned with her back to the door and looked at him, and she looked at him with her face fully toward him for the second time in the day, and the looking was the looking.

"Come back tomorrow," Ryn said.

He waited.

"Not because you're failing. Because you used to come by on Tuesdays."

"Tomorrow's Tuesday."

"Yes."

"All right."

"Come at the third hour. I'll be done with the first round of repairs. We can go up the wall and watch the boats."

"All right."

"Ash."

"Yes."

"Eat tonight," Ryn said. "A real meal. With your sister. Not bread at a shelf."

"How did you, "

"I know what you eat when you eat. It's not eating. Eat tonight."

"All right."

"Go."

She put her palm flat between his shoulder blades and pushed once, light, and it was the push she had pushed when they were nine and she had been telling him to go home before his mother got worried, and the push was the push, and he went.

He went two steps and stopped.

He turned. She had not gone in. She was at the door with her hand on the handle and she was looking at him and her face had the small set it got when she was not going to say what she was about to think, and he saw the not-saying and he did not push for the saying, and he raised his hand the small way she had raised hers at the boy with the broom, and she raised hers back the same way, and he turned and went.

* * *

He did not go back to the workshop.

He went to his sister's. He went the long way, up the curve and past the workshop without stopping at it, and he did not look at the lamp by the door though the lamp was lit, and he came down the other side of the curve to Maren's, where the door was the door it had been since he was small, and he knocked.

Maren opened it.

She looked at him. She looked at him the way Maren looked at things, which was the way of a woman who took the measure of a thing in the time another person took to draw a breath, and she stepped back from the door without saying anything, and he went in, and she closed the door, and the room was warm.

"Eat," she said.

"Yes."

"I made stew," Maren said. "I always make stew. Sit."

He sat at her table.

She put the bowl in front of him. She put the bread next to the bowl. She put the spoon in his hand, because his hand had not gone for the spoon, and when his hand had the spoon she sat across from him and put her elbows on the table and watched him eat.

He ate.

He ate the way a person ate when a person had not eaten properly in a week and had not known he had not eaten properly in a week until the smell of stew had been in front of him. He ate slow. He ate steady. He did not talk. Maren did not talk. She watched him eat, and when the bowl was half empty she got up and refilled it without asking, and he ate the second bowl too, and at the end of the second bowl he set the spoon down and put his hands flat on the table.

"Ryn," he said.

"Mm."

"I went down to see her."

"Good."

"She told me some things," Ash said.

"Good."

"You knew she would."

"I hoped she would."

"You could have."

"I'm not Ryn."

She said it without an edge. She said it the way Maren said a thing that was the case, in the plain voice of a woman who had decided some time ago who she was and was not going to apologize for it and was also not going to perform it.

"No."

"Eat the bread."

He ate the bread.

He ate the bread and she got up and put the kettle on the stove, and the kettle was warmed because she channeled for it the way she had channeled for it since she was twelve, and her pool was her pool, and she did not, in the channeling, perform anything, and the kettle warmed, and she poured two cups, and she set one in front of him and took one for herself and sat.

"She said come back Tuesday," Ash said.

"Tomorrow."

"Yes."

"Are you going."

"Yes."

"Good."

They drank the tea.

He drank the tea and his hands warmed around the cup and his shoulders, which had been at his ears since the canal, came down the rest of the way, and Maren saw the coming-down and did not name it. She drank her tea. She watched him over the rim of her cup. The cup was the green cup, the one with the chip on the rim that she always took and never gave him, and she had given him the blue cup, which was the cup she gave him when he was a child and had been crying and had been told to stop and had stopped, and the blue cup was the blue cup, and he drank from it.

"I'm going to sleep here tonight."

"Yes."

"On the couch."

"Yes."

"I have to be at Ryn's at the third hour tomorrow."

"I'll wake you."

"All right."

He slept on the couch.

He slept the way a person slept who had been not sleeping for three weeks and had decided to sleep, which was hard at first, and then easy, and then deep. Maren put the blanket over him after he was under and tucked the corner under his foot the way she had tucked the corner under his foot when he was eight, and the tucking was a thing he did not feel and a thing he would, on waking, know had happened.

* * *

He woke before the lamp.

The room was the room. The window was grey. Maren was at the stove in her nightshirt with her shawl over her shoulders, and the kettle was on, and the smell of bread was the smell of the bread Maren cut in the morning, which was yesterday's bread sliced and warmed on the stovetop, which was the breakfast Maren had served since their mother had stopped serving it.

"Third hour," he said.

"You have time."

"What hour."

"Second."

"All right."

He sat up. He pulled the blanket off and folded it the way Maren folded it, in thirds, and put it on the arm of the couch. He went to the basin and washed his face. He came to the table.

She put the bread in front of him and a cup of tea and a small dish of jam, the red jam she had put up in autumn and had not, this winter, opened, and the opening of the jam was the thing he saw, and he did not, on seeing it, say.

He ate.

He ate the bread and the jam and drank the tea, and Maren sat across from him and ate her own bread, and neither of them said much, and the not-saying-much was the not-saying-much it always had been at her table, which was a not-saying that was a kind of company, and the company was the company.

At the second-and-three-quarters hour he stood.

"Coat."

"On the peg."

He took his coat off the peg. He put it on. He stopped at the door.

"Maren."

"Mm."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Eat tonight too."

"All right."

He went out.

He went down her lane and across the square and up the other lane to the lower terraces, the way he had gone the day before, but slower this time, because he was not going down the curve at dawn with his hands carrying a thing he could not put down. He was going to a yard with a gate, and the gate would be open, and Ryn would be at the bench, and she would not, when he came in, drop what she was doing.

She would say *hand me the small wrench.*

He would hand her the small wrench.

She would finish her work.

Then they would walk.

He came to the lane. The lane was the lane. The boy at the bakery raised his broom an inch and Ash raised his hand the same inch, and the boy went back to his sweeping. He came to the gate.

The gate was open.

She was at the bench.

She was on her knees again, with her sleeves pushed up, with the wrench roll open beside her, and she did not look up when he came into the yard, and the not-looking-up was, this time, not a not-looking-up. It was the rhythm of a Tuesday. It was the rhythm of how it had been when they were sixteen and he had come down the lane on a Tuesday afternoon to wait for her to finish so they could walk.

"Hand me the small wrench," she said.

He took the small wrench out of the roll.

He set it in her hand.

Her hand closed on it without her looking, and she said "thanks," and she went back to the coupling, and he stood at the corner of the bench and watched her work, and the watching was the watching, and the Tuesday was a Tuesday, and the yard was the yard, and the wind came down the lane from the upper terraces and lifted the corner of the cloth on the shelf in the shed and let it go, and the cloth settled, and Ryn worked the coupling, three quarter-turns and a check and three more.

He did not, while she worked, talk.

He did not need to.

She finished the coupling. She put her palm flat on it and held it a count and took her palm away.

"That's done," Ryn said.

"All right."

She wiped her hands on the rag from her belt. She looked at him. Her face did the small adjustment her face did. She did not, this time, say *you look terrible.*

"Better."

"Mm."

"Walk?" Ryn said.

"Yes."

"Up the wall. The boats."

"Yes."

She took her coat off the peg. She tied the gate with the loop of twine. They went down the lane, the two of them, in the same pace, with their hands in their pockets, and the smell of the bakeries was the smell, and the lattice ran cold under their feet in the place it ran cold and warm in the place it ran warm, and the city was the city, and they walked.
