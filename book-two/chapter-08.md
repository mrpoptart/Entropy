# Chapter 8

The transcript was on the bench when Ash came down at the third hour.

Haran had not folded it. The transcript was the kind of document the Council printed in narrow type on cheap paper and bound at the spine with a single staple, and it lay flat on the bench beside the file and the small leather roll and a brass blank that Haran had begun cutting before deciding the blank wanted another half hour at temperature first. The transcript was open to the second page. There was a small chalk mark in the margin beside a line on the second page, and another beside a line on the fourth, and another on the seventh.

Ash sat on the crate.

The session was titled, on the cover page, *Council Hearing on Reconstruction Funding, Eighth Session.* Underneath, in smaller type, *Subhearing on Equitable Magical Resource Allocation.* He read the smaller type twice. He had not, in the four months since the earthquake, seen the phrase under a Council seal. The phrase had been on broadsheets. The phrase had been on the plinth at Halver's Square. The phrase had been, in the Open Hand's blue-wax letter, the spine of a paragraph he had read three times.

It was on a Council document now.

He turned to the second page.

The chalk-marked line was a question from a councillor whose name he did not know, asked of a witness whose name he did not know, and the question was *whether the term redistribution, as used by the petitioners, corresponds to any existing legal mechanism in the city's charter, or whether the petitioners propose new mechanisms.* The answer ran three paragraphs. The answer was patient. The answer named four mechanisms that did not exist and proposed the architecture of a fifth, and the architecture was clean, the way a thing was clean when a person had drawn it more than once before drawing it for a transcript.

Haran came in from the square.

He set the kettle on the stove. He did not look at Ash. He took the file from the bench and held the brass blank up to the lamp and turned it once and put it back down.

"You read it," Haran said.

"I'm reading it."

"Mm."

"They have a platform."

"They have."

"Named organizers."

"Mm."

Haran did not, on the second *mm,* add anything. He took the kettle off when it began to make the small sound it made before the boil, and he poured into the brown pot, and he set the pot on the bench and went back out into the square. The door closed. The transcript lay flat. Ash read.

He read to the seventh page. He read the chalk-marked line on the seventh page. The line was a sentence that began *the decline is not a natural disaster,* and continued, *it is a political one,* and then a comma and three clauses and a period, and the three clauses were a list, and the list named the elder channelers and the institutional directors and, last, the Wellspring.

The Wellspring was a category.

He had been the Wellspring for a year. He had been, in the broadsheets, a man and a story and an emblem and a punchline, and he had been, in the upper-terrace drawing rooms, a problem and an asset and a curiosity. He had not, until this morning, been a category in a Council transcript, listed alongside elder channelers and institutional directors as a class of person whose comfort the city was being asked to stop preserving.

He read the sentence again.

*Above all the Wellspring.*

He set the transcript on the under-bench shelf, beside the two letters and the broadsheet.

* * *

The broadsheet came at the fifth hour.

Haran brought it in from the square with the morning's bread under his arm and laid it on the bench the way a man laid a thing he had been given for a purpose and had decided, in the walking, to honor the purpose. He did not say where it had come from. The fold ran across the top fold of the page. The fold was not Haran's.

The headline was small. The piece was on the second page.

**THE LEVELERS PUBLISH PLATFORM.**

The byline was not L. Korrin. The byline was a name Ash did not know. The piece ran two columns and quoted the platform at length, and the platform's first sentence was the chalk-marked sentence from the transcript, and the platform's second sentence was the line that had been, in the Halver's Square speech, *the man with an ocean asking the thirsty to wait.* The line was in the platform now. It was attributed to no one. It was set in the same type as the rest, the way a thing was set when it had stopped belonging to its first speaker and had become a sentence the movement said.

He read the platform through.

The platform was twelve paragraphs. The first paragraph was the diagnosis. The second paragraph was the history. The third paragraph was the failure of the existing institutions. The fourth through the seventh were the prescription, which was redistribution, which was named in the platform with four named mechanisms that were the four mechanisms the witness had described in the Council transcript and had architected the fifth of, and Ash registered, on the second reading, that the witness in the transcript and the writer of the platform were the same person, or were at least people who had drawn the same drawings on the same paper.

The eighth and ninth paragraphs were on the Wellspring.

The paragraphs did not name him. The paragraphs said *the most powerful channeler of the age,* and *the man whose pool exceeds, by some accounts, the combined pools of every elder in the city.* The paragraphs said *we have read with interest the accounts of his civic experiment,* and *we observe that the experiment has not yet, in any meaningful way, addressed the question of his own holding.* The paragraphs said *charity is what the powerful offer instead of justice.*

The word *hoarding* was in the tenth paragraph.

It was in the eleventh.

It was in the twelfth, twice.

He read the paragraphs the way a man read a thing about himself in a register he could not, while reading, refute. The wound the paragraphs opened was not the wound of a thing said falsely about him. The wound was the wound of a thing said truly about a person he had been, twenty-two years, twenty-three years, watching the upper terraces from the lower city in winter and writing in his head, exactly, these paragraphs, about other people.

He had written *charity is what the powerful offer instead of justice.*

He had written it in his head walking past Hellen's house, perhaps. He had written it at twenty-one. He had written it, the way a person wrote a sentence they would carry into a room one day and put down on a table, and the table the sentence had been put down on was the table he was sitting at.

He set the broadsheet on the shelf.

He drank the tea Haran had poured an hour before. The tea was cold. He drank it.

"There's a rally," Haran said.

Ash looked up.

Haran was at the bench. He had a small rectangle of paper in his hand, the size of a handbill, and he set it on the bench without looking at it and went back to the file.

"Tonight," Haran said. "Seventh hour. The Cooperage."

"Where's the Cooperage."

"Lower city. Old barrel yard. Big enough."

"How big."

"Hundreds, they think."

"Mm."

Haran filed.

"You'll go," Haran said.

It was not a question. Haran did not, in the next moment, look at him to see whether the not-question had landed where Haran had aimed it. The file moved. The brass shaving curled.

"I'll go," Ash said.

"In a coat."

"In a coat."

"At the back."

"At the back."

"Mm," Haran said.

He filed.

* * *

The Cooperage was below Stonecurve and west.

He took the long way. The long way ran along the disused canal that ran from the lower cisterns down to the harbor flats, and the canal was empty at this hour the way the canal was always empty since the second cistern had been put out of service in the bad winter, and his boots on the towpath made the sound boots made on a path that had not been a working path in twelve years. He had borrowed a coat from the peg by the workshop door. The coat was Haran's spare. The coat was heavy at the shoulders and short at the wrists and was, in every way that mattered, a coat that did not announce its wearer.

He came into the lower city by the western stair.

The crowd was already in the streets. He registered this two blocks before he reached the yard. The lower city was not a quiet quarter at the seventh hour. The lower city at the seventh hour was, on most evenings, the sound of taverns opening and pots being scoured and dogs and the small percussion of a quarter putting itself to bed. Tonight the streets were directional. People were walking the same way. They were walking the way a crowd walked when the crowd had not yet become a crowd but had decided where the crowd was going to be, and the walking was steady, and most of the walkers were not talking, and the few who were talking were not loud.

He fell in.

Nobody looked at him.

The coat held. He had a knit cap pulled low. He had not, in the year, walked through a public crowd in clothes that did not announce him, and he registered, the way a man registered a thing he had forgotten the texture of, the small clean fact of being passed by people who had no reason to look twice.

The Cooperage gate was open.

The yard had been a barrel works. The yard was paved in the old way, fitted stones, with the iron rails for the cart still set into the paving where a cart had run from the kiln to the sorting shed before the works had closed. The kiln was gone. The sorting shed was gone. The walls of the yard were the walls of the yard, and the yard was perhaps eighty paces across, and the yard was filling.

He stopped at the back, against the eastern wall.

He counted.

He counted by tens, the way Haran had taught him to count a crowd by tens, picking a row of ten heads and laying it in his head against the next row of ten heads and the next, and at five rows he had fifty and at ten rows he had a hundred and at twenty rows he had two hundred, and there were more than twenty rows. There was a band along the back where the rows broke up into knots and the knots went on, against the western wall, for another forty paces.

Three hundred. Perhaps four.

The crowd was young. He had registered this on the towpath and he registered it again in the yard. The crowd was, mostly, his age and younger. The crowd was, in its dress, the crowd of the lower districts and the second terrace and the canal-quarters, with a thin scatter, near the front, of people whose coats were of the cut the upper terraces wore, and the scatter near the front was, Ash registered, deliberate. They had been allowed to the front. They were being allowed to be seen at the front. The movement had a sense of the room.

Pamphlets were being handed out at the gate.

A woman near him had one. She was standing two paces to his left along the wall, and she had the pamphlet open across her two hands, and she was reading. She was perhaps twenty-five. Her coat was good. Her hair was pulled back tight under a cap and she was reading the way a person read a thing she had read before and was checking, against her memory, the edges of what she remembered.

She looked up at the platform.

The platform was a flatbed cart pushed against the western wall. Two lamps had been hung at the corners. There was no banner. There was no painted sigil. The absence of a banner was, he registered, also deliberate. The platform was a flatbed cart with two lamps on it and the absence of a banner said *we are not yet a thing that needs a sigil.*

A man climbed onto the cart.

Not the man from Halver's Square. A different man, older, in the working clothes of the canal quarter, who raised a hand and waited for the talking in the yard to drop off, and the talking dropped off, and the man said:

"You know why we're here."

A few in the front said yes. Most did not. The man nodded as if both responses had been correct.

"You know who you're here to listen to."

A wider yes.

"Davan will speak. Then there will be three speakers from the districts. Then we will pass the platform around in print, and you will read it and you will sign or not sign, and there is no shame in not signing tonight. There is shame in signing without reading. Read."

He stepped down.

The crowd shifted. The shift was not a surge. The shift was a settling, the way a body settled in a chair when the body had decided it was going to be in the chair for an hour. Ash registered the settling. He had been in crowds. He had been in crowds the night of the earthquake and in crowds at the upper-terrace receptions and in the crowd at Halver's Square, and the settling tonight was a kind of settling he had not, in any of those crowds, felt before. It was the settling of a body of people who had come to listen.

A second figure climbed onto the cart.

He heard him before he saw him.

* * *

The voice carried.

It carried without channeling. He registered this in the first two words. The yard was eighty paces across and the voice reached the eastern wall the way a voice reached a wall it had been pitched to reach, by a man who knew, by long practice, what his voice was for and how far it would go without being asked to do more than it would do.

"Good evening."

Two words. The yard quieted further. The man on the cart waited the half-beat the quieting took.

"My name is Davan."

He said it the way a man said a name he had said in this yard before. He did not say *thank you for coming.* He did not say *it is good to see so many of you.* He said his name and he stopped, and the stop was the stop of a man building.

Ash leaned against the wall. He could see, now. The man on the cart was perhaps thirty. He was tall. He was the man from Halver's Square. He stood on the cart with his hands at his sides and his coat unbuttoned, and the coat was a working coat, the kind a canal-quarter man wore in the second year of the coat's life, and the man's voice came out of him the way water came out of a tap that had been opened the right amount.

"I want to start," Davan said, "with a sentence I have said before."

He paused.

"The decline is not a natural disaster. It is a political one."

The yard made the sound the yard made. It was not a cheer. It was the sound a crowd made when the crowd had heard the sentence three times before and was hearing it, this evening, in the room where the sentence was about to be spent. A low collective register. Recognition.

"I have said it in this yard. I have said it in Halver's Square. I have said it in the Council gallery, on a day they were calling the session a hearing on funding. I have said it in print. I will say it tonight one more time, and then I will not say it again for the rest of the evening, because the saying is not the work."

He paused again. He let the not-saying be a thing.

"Here is the work."

He raised one hand. Not high. He raised it to the level of his chest and held it open, palm up, the way a man held a hand that was going to lay something on a table.

"Every day," Davan said, "the strong keep what they have. Every day, the weak lose more. The decline is not closing the gap. The decline is widening it. Every winter that comes, the people in this yard burn a little colder. Every winter, the people on the upper terraces burn the same."

He moved his hand. He set it down on the rail of the cart, the open palm flat.

"They are not malicious. I want to say that clearly. The people who are warm are not warm because they hate us. They are warm because they were warm yesterday and the day before and the year before, and the system that warmed them yesterday warms them today, and the system was not built to ask whether warming them is fair. The system was built to keep doing what it did. And so it does."

A woman in the third row said yes. She said it quietly. The yes was not for the crowd. The yes was for herself.

"The argument we are asked to accept," Davan said, "is that the decline is a problem to be solved by patience. Wait. Wait while the institutions adjust. Wait while the engineers build alternatives. Wait while the powerful, in their own time, in their own way, rediscover their conscience."

He looked across the yard. He did not, Ash registered, look at the eastern wall. He looked across the yard the way a teacher looked across a room, in even sweeps, his eyes finding rows and not faces.

"Waiting," Davan said, "is what got us here."

The woman two paces to Ash's left closed her eyes. She did not nod. She closed her eyes for a moment and opened them and read the next line off the platform in her hands a half-second before Davan said it, and her lips moved the shape of it as he said it.

"Waiting," Davan said, "was what they asked of our parents. Waiting was what they asked of our grandparents. Waiting is the polite name for the answer the powerful give when they have decided, in the quiet of their own minds, that they are going to keep what they have."

He paused.

"I am not going to tell you tonight to stop waiting. You have already stopped. You are in this yard. You are reading the platform in your hands. You have stopped, and you are looking for the next thing, and the next thing is the question I want to put to you."

He lifted his hand off the rail.

"Who," Davan said, "do we ask."

The yard was quiet.

"Not the Council. The Council has been asked. The Council has been hearing us politely for two months and the politeness is the answer. Not the institutions. The institutions are the system that built the gap and they are not the system that closes it. Not the upper terraces. The upper terraces have been comfortable for six generations. They will be comfortable until comfort costs them more than they can pay, and we are not, this evening, the cost they cannot pay."

He stopped. He looked, this time, at the rows. He looked at the rows the way a man looked at a list.

"Who has the pool," Davan said, "to close the gap."

He did not say the name.

He did not need to.

The yard did not turn. The yard did not look around for the man with an ocean. The yard did not, in the half-second after the question, do the thing the Halver's Square crowd had done, which was wave-up against the wall of a boat. The yard, instead, held still. Ash registered this. The movement had learned, in the weeks since Halver's Square, that the question worked better unanswered. The question was the architecture. The question was the room the platform built and asked the listener to stand in. The naming would have been small. The not-naming was large.

The woman two paces away was reading the platform. She had not, in the not-naming, looked around.

"There is one pool in this city," Davan said, "that could, by itself, hold the lower districts in heat through the next three winters. There is one. We know it. The people who have it know it. The institutions that have allowed it to exist as private holding know it. The argument we have been asked to accept, in print, in the broadsheets, in the polite language of the Council subhearings, is that this pool is being used, in the spirit of its holder's choosing, for civic experiment."

The word *experiment* sat in the yard a moment.

"I want to say something about civic experiment," Davan said.

Ash felt the wall against his back.

"Civic experiment," Davan said, "is the form generosity takes when generosity has decided not to be redistribution. It is a real thing. I am not going to tell you tonight that it is nothing. The hand pumps in Stonecurve are real. The water is real. The people who turn the cranks are real, and they are warmed, and they drink, and what they drink is what the experiment has put in their cups."

He paused.

"And the question I want to put to you is not whether the experiment is good. The question is whether the experiment is the answer to the question we are asking. The question we are asking is not *can the powerful design a way for us to do without their power.* The question we are asking is *will the powerful share what they have.* These are different questions. They have different answers. And one of them, the one we are being offered, is not the one we asked."

The woman two paces away said, without volume, *yes.*

Ash did not move.

He did not move because the wall was at his back and the coat was buttoned and the cap was low, and because moving would have been a thing visible, and because the voice on the cart was, in its precision and in its restraint and in the architecture of its sentences, doing a thing that asked of him no movement, only listening.

He listened.

He listened to the next four sentences. He listened to the way the four sentences built on the four before them. He listened to Davan say *the strong are choosing,* and to the qualification that followed, which was that choosing did not require malice, which was, in the sentence after, the thing that made choosing harder to argue with, not easier. He listened to Davan say *we are done asking,* and to the half-beat afterward in which Davan did not, by tone, by gesture, by any of the small theatrical claims a speaker made on a crowd, make the claim a threat. He had said *we are done asking* the way a man said *the bread is gone,* the way a man named a fact.

He recognized the architecture.

He recognized it the way the engineer recognized a building that had been drawn by a person who had drawn buildings before. The platform on the cart was not a stack of grievances. The platform was a structure. Each sentence carried a load. Each pause was a joint. The argument moved from premise to premise the way a load moved from beam to column, and the column rested on a foundation Ash had, twenty-three years, helped to dig.

He had dug it himself.

He had written its sentences. He had written them in his head walking past Hellen's house in the winter at twenty-one. He had written them at the long cold table in his parents' kitchen at twenty-two, in the dark, when he had known the kettle would not warm and his sister had been asleep on the bench by the stove and his mother had been bent over the ledger, and he had known, the way a person knew a thing without being able to change it, that the people in the upper terraces were warm tonight because his mother was bent over the ledger.

The sentences on the cart were his.

They were better-built than his. They had been honed in rooms he had not been in. They had been rehearsed in front of audiences he had not addressed. They were his sentences come back to him in the mouth of a man who had earned them through the work of saying them and saying them.

Davan was speaking, now, of the platform's specific mechanisms.

Ash did not, for the next two minutes, hear the mechanisms.

He looked at the crowd.

* * *

He saw Vell first.

She was eight rows in. She had a cap on, the small wool cap she wore at the pump in the cold mornings, and she was standing with two girls who had the look of girls who came up from the Stonecurve school. Vell was not, at fifteen, the tallest in her row. He had to count in by tens twice before he caught her, and then he caught her, and the catch was a small clean shock, the shock of seeing a person in a place he had not put them in.

She was listening.

She was listening the way she listened at the pump when Reska was teaching, which was the way a person listened who was already half-knowing what she was being told and was checking the half against the rest. She was not nodding. She was not making the small affirmative sounds the woman two paces away was making. She was registering. He watched her register. He watched her, between two of Davan's sentences, look down at the pamphlet she had been handed at the gate, and then back up, and then down again, and he watched her finger move along a line on the pamphlet the way her finger moved along a sleeve seam when she was checking a brass joint for the line where the file had taken the oxide off.

Ash did not move.

He did not move because she had not yet, in the eight rows in, looked back. He did not move because if she looked back and found him she would have to decide what to do with the finding, and he did not want her to have to decide. He did not move because the Vell in the crowd was not the Vell at the pump and was not, this evening, the Vell who needed him to be anything.

She did not look back.

She did, after a time, move her head a little to the left, the way she moved her head when she was hearing a thing about a system she was running. She did this twice. The second time, she half-turned to the older of the two girls beside her and said something, and the older girl nodded, and Vell turned back to the cart.

Ash kept watching.

He saw, three rows behind Vell and to the right, Reska's cousin, who came to the morning crew on Third-day to bring Reska her lunch, and who had stayed twice to watch the crew work, and who had asked, on the second time, whether the morning crew was hiring. The cousin was a woman of perhaps forty. She was in a coat heavier than Vell's. She was, like Vell, listening, and like Vell, not nodding.

He saw, against the western wall, the boy from the second crew. The boy who had quit. The boy who, with Aren, had told Reska *we are tired of being taught like children.* He was alone. Aren was not with him. The boy was standing with his arms folded across his chest in the way the man at Building Four had stood with his arms folded across his chest at the third hour, and the boy was, Ash registered, not listening the way the others were listening. The boy was listening the way a person listened who had already signed the thing that was, this evening, being signed in the yard.

He saw, near the gate, two faces from the earthquake nights.

He did not know their names. He had carried a beam off a man's leg in the second night, in the third district, with the help of three other people, and one of the three had had a face he had not, in the dark, seen well, but the shape of the face had stayed with him the way a face stayed when the face had been near his own face under load, and that face was at the gate. The other face was a woman who had brought water to the responders on the fourth night in the canal quarter. She had filled a clay jug from a stranger's basin and walked it down the line, and Ash had drunk from the jug, and she had, on his thanking her, said *drink, son,* in a voice that did not come back in his head until he saw her tonight at the gate of the Cooperage, and then her voice came back the way the face had come back, whole.

He registered this, against the wall, in Haran's coat, with the cap pulled low, the way an engineer registered a measurement. He did not, in the registering, name what he felt. He felt it in his hands. His hands had gone tight at his sides. He noticed this and let them open, and the opening was a small effort, the kind of effort it took to open a hand a man had not, until that moment, known he had closed.

Davan was still speaking.

Davan said: "The platform proposes four mechanisms. I will name them. I will not, tonight, defend them. The pamphlet defends them. You will read."

He named the four mechanisms. They were the four mechanisms from the transcript. They were the four mechanisms from the eighth-page paragraph of the platform that lay, this morning, on the under-bench shelf in the workshop in Stonecurve. The four mechanisms were not new to Ash. The four mechanisms were, however, Vell's and Reska's cousin's and the woman with the jug's and the boy who had quit's, in a way they had not, until this evening, been theirs. They had been read aloud to the people whose lives the mechanisms were the architecture for, and the people were standing in the yard, and the people were holding the pamphlet.

He thought, *I should leave.*

He did not leave.

He stayed three more sentences, four. He stayed for the line he had been waiting for without knowing he was waiting for it, the line Davan said quietly, near the end of his time on the cart, with one hand on the rail and his weight on his back foot, the way a man said a line he had decided to put on the table and leave there.

"We are not asking the Wellspring," Davan said, "to be a hero. We are asking him to be a citizen."

The yard made the sound. It made it lower than it had made it before. It made it the way a yard made a sound when the yard had heard, in a sentence, the precise shape of a thought it had been carrying without words.

A man near the front said, loud, and clear, and not addressed to the cart, "*Citizen.*"

Just the word. He said it the way a man tested a coin against a stone. Then he said it again, lower, to himself, and the woman beside him said it back to him, and the saying-back was not a chant. The saying-back was two people who had heard a word land and were, between them, picking it up.

Behind Ash, against the wall, a young woman said, almost in his ear, "He's right."

She wasn't speaking to him. She was speaking to herself, or to the friend on her other side, and her voice was the voice of a person who had, in the half-hour of the speech, decided a thing. She had not been certain when she came in. She had come to listen. She was leaving certain. He had heard the moment of the deciding go through her, in the half-second before *he's right,* and the moment had had the small quiet click of a latch.

Ash turned his head a quarter-inch.

He could see the woman in the corner of his eye. She was perhaps twenty-two. She had the broad clean hands of a worker in one of the trades that did not require channeling, weaving, perhaps, or paper, and her cap was the cap a paper-quarter girl wore. She was holding the pamphlet at her chest. She had not opened it again since the first reading.

She said it once more. *He's right.*

The friend beside her said *yes.*

He turned, slowly, against the wall, and went out of the yard the way he had come in.

* * *

The street outside the Cooperage was empty.

He did not, on the way out of the gate, look back at the cart. He did not look at the woman two paces from where he had been standing. He did not look at Vell. He did not look at the boy from the second crew. He went out the way a man went out of a room when going out was, that minute, the work, and the work was the work because staying was a thing he could not yet do without saying something, and Haran had said *not today.*

The lower city was quiet at the seventh hour now that the crowd was in the yard.

He walked.

He took the canal towpath again. The path was the path it had been on the way down. The boots made the sound boots made on a path the city had given up on, and the canal, on his right, held the small remnant of water the canal had held for twelve years, the dark slick where the rain had pooled at the lowest points and where, in places, ice had begun to skim. The light was gone. The lamps along the towpath had not been lit in twelve years, and his eyes adjusted, and he walked.

He thought.

He thought *I was a citizen.*

He thought *I was a citizen for twenty-three years.*

He thought it the way a man thought a thing he had been told this evening and had agreed with and did not, in the agreeing, feel saved.

He had agreed with the diagnosis. He had agreed with the diagnosis in his hands, on the wall, in the coat, at the moment Davan had said *waiting is what got us here.* He had agreed with it in the chest, the small physical place where he agreed with a thing before his mouth had caught up. He had agreed with the line about charity. He had agreed with the line about citizens. He had agreed with the four mechanisms in their architecture, although he knew, the way he knew the math at three percent, that the mechanisms would not, in twenty winters, do what the platform claimed they would do.

He agreed with the diagnosis.

He did not agree with the prescription.

The distinction was real.

The distinction was real, and the distinction was, between Halver's Square and the eastern wall of the Cooperage, the distinction every powerful person in the history of the city had made when asked to share. *I agree the gap is unjust. I disagree with the proposed remedy.* He had read the sentence in his head a hundred times in a hundred essays in the upper-terrace journals he had read at twenty-one in the small library on the second terrace, and he had, every time, hated the sentence, hated the man who had written the sentence, hated the room the sentence had been written in.

The sentence was now the sentence he was thinking.

He stopped on the towpath.

He stood. The canal's dark was at his right. The buildings of the canal quarter were at his left, dark in the windows, dark on the upper floors, dark in the way the canal quarter had been dark in winter for twelve years because the heat that had once moved up into the canal-quarter risers from the second cistern's exchange did not, since the cistern's failure, move at all, and the people in the rooms behind the dark windows were sleeping in coats.

He had, in the workshop in Stonecurve, built a thing that warmed Block Three.

He had not, in the workshop in Stonecurve, warmed the canal quarter.

He could.

He could open a fraction of his pool against the cistern this winter and the canal quarter would be warm by the second week. He could do it tonight. He could do it at the cost of a percent. He could do it without asking. He had, in the year, done many things at the cost of a percent. The catastrophic restorations. The two villages on the western road. The first night of the earthquake.

He had not done it for the canal quarter.

He had not done it because the canal quarter was the kind of work the Council had not asked him for, and because the Council had not asked him for it because the Council had, a month after the earthquake, given him Stonecurve and called the giving the proof of concept, and the proof of concept was, by Dorenne's design, the place where the Wellspring's pool was not the answer.

Stonecurve was not the answer.

The pool was the answer.

The pool was the answer to Vell's mother's heat tonight and to the canal quarter's heat tonight and to the answer the woman in the paper-quarter cap had decided on, at the wall, in the small click of her latch. The pool was the answer. It was not the answer he had built. It was the answer Davan had built, at the cart, with sentences that were Ash's sentences, used, this evening, on the question of Ash's pool.

He stood on the towpath.

He thought *the woman in the cap is right. He's right.*

He thought *and the prescription will not work.*

He thought it together, the two thoughts, the way a man thought two things that were both, simultaneously, true.

He started walking again.

* * *

He came up into Stonecurve from the western stair.

The square was the square. The basin tap ran. Vell's pump was under its cover. The eastern pump was under its cover. The lamps in the windows of Block Three were on, the small irregular pattern of a building where some flats were still awake at the eighth hour and some were not, and the lamp in the workshop window burned, low, the way Haran left it when he had gone to the bakery to sit a while with the boy who slept above the workshop.

Ash did not go in.

He stopped at the basin.

He turned the tap.

The water came. It came at the pressure it had come at this morning and yesterday morning and the morning before that, and the small clear arc of it ran into the basin's drain and away, and he held his hand under it for the time it took to feel the pressure, and he closed it.

He stood at the basin a minute.

Nobody had channeled the water into the tap. Nobody on the morning crew or the evening crew or the crew of any other crew. The water had run because Vell had primed the pump in the morning at the third hour, and because Reska had checked the manifold at the fourth, and because a boy from the second crew, before he had quit, had wound the stroke-arm with copper wire and rosin in the third week. The water was the water of the proof of concept. The water was the answer Ash had built.

He looked across the basin.

He looked past Vell's pump, past the eastern pump, past the cellar door of Block Three with its small lit lamp on the inside of the doorframe, past the bakery where Othen had slept four nights ago, past the curve of the square's western edge to the place where the square ended and the lower streets began. The lower streets ran down toward the canal. The buildings on the lower streets were the buildings of the lower streets. The windows on the upper floors of those buildings were dark, mostly, at the eighth hour. He counted, by tens, the dark windows. He stopped at sixty.

The pump worked.

The dark windows were dark.

He stood at the basin a long minute.

The proof of concept was the proof of concept. Block Three was warm. Building Four was warm again, after the morning's failure. The cistern indicator stood at its mark. The crank was quiet. Jora was teaching a girl the count, and the girl would teach a girl, and the slate in the rack by the door was filling.

The dark windows were dark.

He did not, in the moment, name the question the dark windows were asking.

He did not need to. The question was the question Davan had built into the platform on the cart and into the line the woman in the paper-quarter cap had said back to her friend and into the silence the woman two paces from him had kept while she read. The question was the question the broadsheets had been writing and the Open Hand had been writing and the platform had, this morning, put under a Council seal.

*Why should they wait?*

He turned the tap once more. The water came. He let it run a count of three. He closed it.

He went up the stair to the room above the workshop and lay on the bedroll and did not light the lamp.

The reservoir sat. He registered it, the small held weight of it at the back of his attention, the way he registered it most nights, the way a man registered a thing that was always there and had been, this evening, named. He had not spent against it tonight. He had stood at a wall in a borrowed coat. He had walked. He had turned a tap.

The shelf in the back of the room he set things on was, by morning, fuller than it had ever been.

He did not, by morning, lift the cloth.

He did not, by morning, write the letters back.

He slept.
