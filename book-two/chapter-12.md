# Chapter 12

The broadsheet was on the bench when he came in.

Haran had folded it once, the long fold, and set it under the small brass weight that had been a clamp before it had been a weight. The weight was where Haran put things he wanted Ash to read. The fold was where Haran put things he had read twice.

Ash hung his coat. He went to the basin and washed his hands in cold water and dried them on his front, because the cloth was at the workshop two doors down and the cloth at this basin was the cloth Haran kept for the small parts. He came back to the bench.

He read standing.

The headline was set in the heaviest face the press kept. Three Council seats had flipped in the lower-quarter ward elections. The names were names he half knew, names from the broadsheets that had been footnotes a year ago and were now bylines. The platforms were the platform. *A pool is not a private estate. A pool is a draw on a common bed.* Underneath the headline, in the lighter face, the ordinance.

He read the ordinance twice.

It was modest. That was the first thing he registered, and the registering of it was the thing that sat down in him afterward and would not get up. The ordinance applied only to designated districts in the lower city. It applied only to elder channelers above a stated pool threshold. It set the contribution at a percentage low enough that a careful drafter could call it negligible, and a careful drafter had. The framework named the receiving infrastructure: the lower aqueduct lattice, the public heating runs of the canal blocks, two named bath-houses, the south hospital's warming circuit. It was constitutional. The lawyers had been thorough. The lawyers were always thorough, when there was time, and there had been time.

It was the first time in the city's history that an expenditure had been compelled by law.

He read the line that said so. The press had set it without ornament, in the running text, because the press knew that a line that did its own work did not need a frame. *The first ordinance of its kind on record.* He read it. He read past it. He came back and read it again.

He set the broadsheet down.

He picked up the gear he had been working on the day before. The teeth had a burr at the third position that he had not had time to file. He picked up the file. He set the gear in the small clamp at the corner of the bench. He filed.

The bell at the door did not ring because Haran had not hung it back after the second hinge had given. Haran came in the way Haran came in, which was the way a man came into a room he had built, which was without announcement.

"Read it," Haran said.

"Twice."

Haran went to his own bench. He took a length of soft wire from the rack and turned it in his fingers and put it back. He did not always take the wire down. The wire was the thing he took down when he was thinking with his hands and did not yet know what the hands were thinking.

"Modest," Haran said.

"Modest."

"Mm."

Ash filed. The burr came off in three passes. He set the file down and turned the gear in the clamp and looked at it from the second angle, the way Haran had taught him to look at a tooth from the angle that was not the angle the eye wanted.

"The framework lawyers wrote it," Haran said. "You can hear them in it."

"Yes."

"It will hold."

"Yes."

A pause.

"They'll come for the upper threshold next year," Haran said. "Or the year after. They'll have the precedent."

"Yes."

Haran took the wire down again. He turned it. He set it back.

"How much is left," Haran said, "and what do you want to build with it."

It was the question. It had been the question for a year. Ash filed the next tooth. The burr came off.

"I don't know yet."

"Mm."

"I know what I don't want to build," Ash said.

"That's a start."

Ash set the file down. He looked at Haran. Haran was not looking at him. Haran was looking at the wire on its rack, at the angle of the wire on its rack, the way Haran looked at things when Haran was giving a person room.

"She'll send for me," Ash said.

"She will."

"Today, you think."

"By the end of the week."

"Today."

Haran's mouth did the thing his mouth did, which was the thing that was not a smile and was the cousin of a smile, and which acknowledged that Ash had been right and that being right about it gave him no pleasure.

"Today, then."

The runner came at the third hour. The note was on the Hall paper, with the Hall seal pressed in the wax, and the wax was the new green wax the Hall had gone to in the spring because the old red was made with a pigment the supplier had stopped grinding. The hand was not Dorenne's hand. The hand was Felden's. *Director Kharren would appreciate it if you would attend her at the fifth hour. Hall, third floor.*

He folded the note. He put it in his coat pocket. He went on filing.

Haran said nothing. The not-saying was not a withholding. The not-saying was the thing Haran did when a man was walking toward a door and did not need company at the door. Ash filed the third tooth and the fourth. He filed until the fourth hour. He cleaned the bench. He put the file away. He took his coat down from the peg.

"Ash," Haran said.

"Mm."

"Whatever she asks for," Haran said.

"Yes."

"Don't decide in the room," Haran said. "Decide on the stair after."

"All right."

"And eat something."

"I will."

He didn't.

* * *

The Hall was the Hall.

The third floor still had the same runners on the boards. The same scuffs at the doorways, the same mark at the corner of the stair-head where a workman had set a crate down too hard in the second year of the new wing and someone, much later, had varnished over the mark instead of repairing it. He had walked these boards every day for eight months. He knew where the second board from the south wall complained and where the runner under the secretary's door had been turned because the seam had frayed.

Felden was at his desk.

Felden stood, which was the thing Felden had not done before the Wellspring and had done every time since, and which Ash had stopped trying to wave off because the waving-off had been the thing Felden could not accept.

"Sir," Felden said.

"Felden."

"She's expecting you."

"I know."

"She's, she's ready."

Felden's eyes went to the door and came back. Felden was a man who had said the same sentence a thousand times in a thousand small variations and was, today, choosing the variation that meant something other than the sentence usually meant. Ash noted it. He did not name it.

"Thank you."

He went in.

Dorenne was at her desk.

She was at her desk and she was at it the way she had always been at it, which was at the angle that allowed her to see the door and the survey and the side-table at once and that gave the person crossing the room three steps in which to decide how he was going to stand when he stopped. The lamp was lit. The lamp was lit at the fourth hour because the window had gone grey and because Dorenne did not work without a lamp, ever, the way a foreman did not work without a level.

"Ash," Dorenne said.

"Director."

The word landed.

It landed not as a slap and not as a step back, but as the small precise thing it was. He had used her name on his last visit. He had used her name through Book One, through the months of the office, through the dinners. He had stopped, at the break. He had not used either, since. He had not been in this room since.

Tonight he had chosen the title.

Her face did not move. Her face had not moved at things larger than this and would not move at this. But her hand, which had been at the corner of a survey, paused, and the pausing was the tell, and he watched it and did not name it.

"Sit," she said.

He sat.

She did not sit immediately. She crossed to the side-table and poured two cups from the pot that was always on the side-table at the fourth hour because Tessaly kept it filled, and she brought the cups to the desk and set one at his elbow and the other at hers, and then she sat. The cup at his elbow was the cup she remembered he took. Black, no honey. The temperature was the temperature a person took a cup at when the person did not want to feel the cup. The care was the affection. He had forgotten how exactly she did it. He had not forgotten that she did.

"You read the ordinance," Dorenne said.

"This morning."

"Of course."

She drew the survey toward her. It was not a survey of the lower city. It was a survey of the aqueduct lattice. The lattice he had walked, two years ago, with a clipboard and a list of valve numbers, when the work she gave him had been peripheral and he had not yet known it.

"The framework," she said, "is straightforward. Six designated districts. Elder channelers at the named threshold contribute a percentage of their daily pool to a coordinated draw. The draw is allocated by the framework office to named infrastructure. The allocation runs on a fortnightly cycle. The hospital warming circuit, the aqueduct lattice, the public heating runs in the canal blocks, the two bath-houses, and the structural reinforcements at the south escarpment."

"I read it."

"You read the press version. The press version is accurate to the surface and silent on the operations. The operations are what concern me."

"Tell me."

She did.

She told him in the way she told things, which was the way a person sequenced a problem, and the sequencing was the thing she had built her career on and the thing the room ran on. The lattice depended on a predictable contribution at the upper feed. The hospital circuit depended on a coordinated channeling at the dawn watch. The bath-houses were on a tighter margin than anyone outside the framework had ever been told, because the bath-houses were used, by mid-winter, by half the lower-quarter elderly for the warming hours, and the bath-houses had been kept running on a system that depended on knowing, to the half-pool, what was coming in. The Levelers' ordinance disrupted the margin. Not by lowering the total. By making the total unpredictable.

"They've replaced a coordinated contribution," she said, "with a compelled one. Compelled is not coordinated. Compelled means a man who would have given freely on Wednesday now gives reluctantly on the cycle, and the reluctance shows in the pool. The pool drops by the expected percentage on paper. In the lattice it drops by more, because a contribution given under duress is a contribution given without care, and the lattice does not run on the percentage, it runs on the care."

She set the survey down.

"If they break the allocation framework, the reconstruction stalls. Districts that depend on coordinated channeling, the hospital heating, the aqueduct network, the structural reinforcements at the south escarpment, all of it becomes unpredictable. I am not telling you this to be heard. I am telling you because you have walked the lattice. You know what unpredictable means in a lattice."

"I know."

"Of course."

She lifted her cup. She drank. She set the cup down. The cup went down at the mark on the desk where the cup always went down, the small ring the lacquer had taken twenty years ago and that nobody had refinished.

"I want you to speak."

He did not move.

"Publicly," she said. "Once. The framework office will draft. You will revise. You will sign your name to it and you will read it on the platform at Halver's Square at the convening of the Council on the fifteenth. The Wellspring, opposing the ordinance. Not the principle of redistribution. The mechanism. The thing that breaks if it is done this way. You can keep the moral ground. You can speak as a man who knows what scarcity feels like. You can argue, as I cannot, that there is a way to address the inequality without dismantling the systems that keep the lower-quarter elderly alive in February."

A pause.

"I would not ask," she said, "if there were another voice that could carry the weight."

He set his cup down.

He set it down at the place his cup had gone down at every visit before the break, and he saw, in setting it, that the lacquer had a second ring at his place, smaller than hers, less worn, but there. He had not known there was a ring at his place. He looked at it and looked away.

"I can't."

"Ash," Dorenne said.

"I can't, Dorenne."

"Tell me why."

He was quiet a moment.

He thought of the broadsheet on the bench. He thought of the ordinance, modest, careful, constitutional. He thought of the children at the canal-quarter pump in the second winter, the ones who had carried water in buckets up the four flights because the lattice had stopped reaching the fourth floor and the elder channeler in the building had said, without malice, that her pool was for her family. He thought of being eleven, leaning across his sister at a basin, putting his hand on the rim and pretending. He thought of his twenty-three years.

"Because if I stand up," he said, "and I tell the powerless to be patient, I will spend the rest of my life knowing I did it. Because the only thing I have that nobody else has is twenty-three years of being on the other side of the lattice. And if I use that to defend the lattice as it is, I have, I have nothing left that is mine."

"The lattice is not the issue."

"It is to them."

"The mechanism is the issue," Dorenne said.

"The mechanism is the lattice, to them. The mechanism is the thing that has, for forty years, decided whose floor was warm in February. You're asking me to defend the mechanism because the mechanism is what you've built. I understand why. I'm not, I'm not arguing that you're wrong about what breaks. I'm telling you that I cannot be the mouth that says it."

She was very still.

She was still in the way she was still when she was deciding which of three answers to give, and he watched the stillness, and he watched her hand, which did not move toward the pencil. The pencil was at her right. The pencil had been at her right his whole tenure here. Her hand stayed on the desk.

"Then you're choosing," she said, "to let the transition framework collapse for the sake of your own comfort with the narrative."

The line landed where she had aimed it.

He felt it land. He did not move. He looked at the cup at her elbow, at the small steam coming off it, at the angle of her wrist on the desk.

"That's possible," he said.

"That's what's happening."

"That's what's happening from where you're sitting," Ash said.

"It's what's happening, Ash. The framework will fail by the equinox if the contribution becomes unpredictable. The bath-houses will close in February. The hospital circuit will stagger. The south escarpment reinforcement will not be finished before the spring rains. People will be cold and people will be sick and people will be standing under stone that has not been reinforced. I am not asking you to defend my career. I am asking you to defend a lattice that holds people up."

"I know what you're asking."

"Then say it differently."

He could not say it differently. He had said it the only way he could say it. He had said it, and she had heard it, and she had named what it cost and what it cost was real, and he could not, from this side of the desk, explain why refusing to oppose the Levelers was different from supporting them, because from her side it was not.

He stood.

He had not asked permission to stand and she had not given it and the standing was, in this room, in this office, the thing that had not happened in his eight months at the desk outside this door. He saw her see it. She did not name it.

"Director," Ash said.

"Ash."

She lifted her cup. The cup had gone cold while they spoke. She drank from it anyway. The drinking was the gesture of a person who had made a habit of drinking from cold cups, and the habit was older than him in this room, and he had, in eight months, never seen it.

"Think about it," she said. "Not for me. For the lattice."

"I have thought about it."

"Think about it again."

He went to the door.

At the door he turned. She was at the desk. She had not moved. The lamp had not moved. The survey had not moved. He saw, at the corner of his eye, that her hand had finally gone to the pencil, and was resting on it, and was not lifting it.

"Dorenne," he said.

"Mm."

"I'm sorry."

She looked up. Her face did not move.

"I'm not asking for your apology."

"I know."

He went out.

Felden stood at the desk. Felden did not say anything. Ash crossed the third floor and went down the stair, and at the second landing he stopped, because Haran had said decide on the stair after, and he was on the stair after, and he stood with one hand on the rail and waited for the deciding to come.

It did not come.

What came was the cold of the rail under his palm, and the small particular ache at the base of the thumb where he had cut himself two days ago and where the cut had reopened in the cold of the rail, and the small persistent picture of her hand resting on the pencil and not lifting it. He stood there until a clerk came past with an armful of papers and he had to step aside. He stepped aside. He went down.

* * *

He took the long way back.

He took it because he could not walk the short way past Haran's door yet, because the Haran-asking-how-it-went was a thing he was not ready to answer. He took it because the cold was useful and because the route through Halver's Square took him past the press-yards and the lower market and the corner where the broadsheets were posted, and he wanted to look at the broadsheets the way a man wanted to look at a thing he could not stop looking at.

The market was the market.

The fishmonger's stall had moved a span west since the last time he had walked through. The salt-cooper had set up where the fishmonger had been. The smell was what the smell had been since he was nine, the smell of the lower market in the third quarter, brine and char and the wet-stone smell of the channel under the cobbles. He bought a pastry at the corner stall because Haran had told him to eat. He ate it walking.

He passed the broadsheet wall.

The wall was the south face of the warehouse at the corner of Halver's Square, the long blank wall the press-boys had taken in the second year and that the Hall had decided not to fight after the first three attempts at clearing it had failed. The wall was layered. The new sheets went up over the old. The oldest layer, at the corners, was the layer from the first Council debate of the year, and the newest, at the center, was today's.

There was a list.

He stopped.

It was set in the heaviest face. The header was *Hoarders of the Sixth Ward.* Underneath, in the running face, the names. Underneath each name, in the lighter face, an address. Underneath each address, in italic, a number. The numbers were pool estimates. The estimates were not from any source the Hall would have recognized as a source. They were estimates, and the act of printing them was the thing.

He read the list.

He did not know any of the names. He read them anyway. The list ran twelve names long. The addresses were addresses he could place: the row above the cooperage, the second terrace at the canal turn, the building at the corner of the square he was standing in. He looked up from the list at the corner building. The lamp at the third-floor window was lit. Someone was home.

A woman beside him at the wall said, to the man with her:

"That's three of the six on Carver Lane."

"Mm."

"They had the bath-house feed before the ordinance," the woman said. "Now they're on the list."

"Mm."

"Good."

The man did not say good. The man said *Mm.* The woman said it again, *good,* into the wall, and they moved on.

He stood at the wall a minute longer.

He did not say anything to the wall. There was nothing to say to the wall. The list was the list. The press had set it. The wall had taken it. The square had walked past it, and one of the people walking past had said good.

He went on.

At the next corner two boys were calling broadsheets. The cry was not the cry he had grown up with. The cry he had grown up with was the headline read once and then the price. The cry now was a phrase. *Hoarder rolls in the Sixth.* *Fresh names.* *Hoarder rolls.* The phrase had become the cry. The cry had become the phrase. The boys were nine and eleven and were not thinking about the word, they were thinking about the coin, and the coin came in for the cry, and the cry kept on.

He walked past.

A woman at a stall pulled a bolt of cloth aside and pointed at the wall.

"Did you see Branner is on it," the woman said.

"I saw."

"I told my husband. Branner."

"I know."

"He warmed our wall, the second winter," the woman said.

"I know."

"And now he's on it."

"I know, Mira."

Ash kept walking.

He went home by the canal.

* * *

The Open Hand was on the second floor above the cooperage.

He had been there twice before. Once with Davan, the first visit, in the second month of the movement when it had been a dozen people and a kettle, and once on his own in the third month when he had wanted to see what it looked like without him in the room. The cooperage had not changed. The stair to the second floor had a new runner, oiled cloth, set down because the old runner had been worn through at the second tread. The runner was a sign. Runners were signs. He read it.

The room was full.

He had not expected the room to be full at the fifth hour on a Wednesday, and the fullness was not the fullness of a meeting in session, it was the fullness of an office, which the room had become. There were two tables along the south wall now, where there had been one. The table at the south wall had ledgers on it, and the ledgers were the kind a printer used, ruled long, and a young woman he half-recognized was working through a stack with a pencil and a ruler and a small abacus he had not seen since his uncle's shop. Liss. He recognized her after a beat. She had cut her hair.

She looked up. She did not stand.

"He's in the back," Liss said.

"Thanks."

"He's with Korel."

"I'll wait."

"You don't need to."

She went back to her ledger.

He waited anyway. He stood at the door with his hands in his coat pockets and watched the room. There were five people he did not know. A man at the north window was sealing letters in a stack, a real stack, two hundred at a glance, with the new green wax the Hall had gone to in the spring, which was the wax the Open Hand was now using too, which meant the Open Hand had a supplier who was supplying the Hall, which meant the Open Hand had moved into a part of the city it had not been in three months ago. Ash noted it. He did not name it.

A door opened at the back.

Davan came out.

Davan was in a coat Ash had not seen him wear before, a heavier coat, plain, the kind a Council clerk wore in winter, which was not the coat of a man at a workbench. Korel came out behind him. Korel raised a hand at Ash and let it fall. Korel did not stop. Korel went past him to the south table and started talking to Liss before he had reached the chair.

"Ash."

"Davan."

"Come back," Davan said.

The back room had been a storeroom. It was now a small office. There was a desk. There was a chair behind the desk and a chair in front of the desk and a third chair against the wall. There were three crates of broadsheets at the wall, bound in twine, addressed in a clean hand to wards Ash recognized. There was a pot of tea on a small iron plate that ran on a wick, not a draw. He noted the wick. He noted the not-draw.

Davan sat behind the desk.

He sat behind it like a man who was conscious of sitting behind it and was making, by the conscious sitting, the small acknowledgment that the desk was new and that he was learning to use it. He did not perform the desk. That was the thing. He did not lean back in the chair the way a man leaned back when the chair was a prop. He sat forward, hands flat on the wood, the way he had sat forward at the workbench in the second month, and the difference was the desk and not the man.

"You read it," Davan said.

"This morning."

"What did Haran say."

"He said it would hold."

"It will hold," Davan said. "The lawyers were good."

"He said they'll come for the upper threshold next year."

Davan smiled. It was not a smile of triumph. It was the small careful smile of a man hearing his own next move named back to him by a person who could read the board.

"Maybe the year after," he said. "Not next year. Next year is consolidation. We have three seats. We don't have a majority. We hold what we have and we make it work, because if it works, the upper threshold is a conversation. If it doesn't, we're finished."

"Mm."

"You didn't come here to talk about the threshold."

"No."

"All right."

Davan did not lean back. He waited. The waiting was different from the waiting at the bench. At the bench the waiting had been the waiting of a man interested in what was about to be said. Here the waiting was the waiting of a man who had a calendar, and the calendar had the next thing on it, and the thing in the chair across from him had a window, and the window was not unlimited.

Ash registered the calendar. He took the chair.

"The list," he said.

"Which list."

"The Sixth Ward," Ash said.

"Ah."

"Davan."

"Say it," Davan said.

"Names. Addresses. Pool estimates that aren't from anywhere. Boys at the corner of Halver's Square calling *hoarder rolls* like it's a price. A woman at a cloth stall pointing at the wall and saying *Branner.*"

"Did Branner contribute under the ordinance."

"I don't know."

"He didn't. None of the twelve did. They had thirty days. The ordinance is in force. They're not refusing because they can't. They're refusing because they're testing whether the ordinance has teeth."

"Davan."

"Say what you came to say, Ash."

"You're not pressuring people who haven't paid a tax. You're putting their addresses on a wall. You're putting numbers on them that aren't numbers anyone can verify. You're, you're letting children call them out at corners."

"Mm."

"That's coercion."

"Is it," Davan said.

"It's coercion."

"Tell me the line."

"What."

"Tell me the line, Ash. Where does political pressure end and coercion begin. Show me the place on the road. Because I have spent four years on this road and I have not seen the line, and you've spent four years building a pump in a district, and you walked out of one office today, and you came to mine, and you're telling me the line is between this morning's broadsheet and the broadsheet that will go up on the wall a month from now. Show me. Show me where it is."

Ash did not answer.

The room held the not-answering.

Davan watched him. Davan did not press. Davan let the silence do the thing the silence did, which was the thing a man learned to let silence do when the man had been in too many rooms and had learned that the silence after the first answer was the place the second answer came from. Ash had seen Davan do this at the Cooperage. He had seen him do it at the workbench. He had not seen him do it from behind a desk.

"I don't know," Ash said.

"You don't know where the line is," Davan said.

"I don't."

"Neither do I."

It was not a concession. It was the next move. Davan said it the way a man set a piece on a board.

"What would you have us do, Ash. Ask nicely? We tried asking. We tried organizing. We tried appealing to the Council. We tried letters and petitions and twelve years of polite delegation. Nothing changed until we made the powerful uncomfortable. Now things are changing. The ordinance passed. Three seats flipped. The lower-quarter heating is, for the first time in my lifetime, on a footing that doesn't depend on a stranger's mood. Are you asking me to stop because the methods are wrong, or because the methods are working?"

The line did its work.

Ash watched it do its work. He had walked here from the canal with the sentence he was going to say, and the sentence had been a clean one, and Davan had taken the sentence and turned it on its edge and shown him the back of it, and the back of it was the question Ash had not put to himself in those words.

"I don't know," he said.

"Then sit with it."

"I will."

"Ash."

"Mm."

"I'm not your enemy."

"I know."

"I'm not asking you to join us," Davan said.

"I know."

"I'm asking you not to oppose us. The Wellspring opposing the ordinance would, would set us back five years. Five years in the lower city is a generation of children carrying water. I think you know that."

"I know."

"Dorenne asked you today."

He did not ask how Davan knew. Davan knew. The Open Hand had an ear at the Hall. He had not known they had an ear at the third floor. He registered it. He did not name it.

"She did."

"And."

"I told her I couldn't."

"Good."

The *good* did not land like the *good* at the wall in Halver's Square. It landed as the small precise thing Davan meant, which was the acknowledgment of a refusal and not a celebration of it. But it landed in the same shape. Ash heard the shape. He did not say so.

"I should go," Ash said.

"Korel will walk you down."

"I can walk myself."

"Korel's going down anyway."

Ash stood.

At the door he turned. Davan had not stood. Davan was at the desk with his hands flat on it, the way Davan had sat forward at the bench, and the position was the position, and the desk was the difference.

"Davan."

"Mm."

"The girl in the harbor quarter."

"What about her."

"You told me about her at the bench," Ash said. "The one who isn't going to be a healer."

"Yes."

"Is she on a list."

Davan looked at him.

"No."

"Will she be," Ash said.

A pause.

"Her mother gave under the ordinance on the first day," Davan said. "Without prompting. They live in the second ward."

"That isn't the question."

"I know."

He did not answer. He looked at Ash and waited, the way he had waited before, and this time he did not let the silence do the work, because the answer was the not-answering, and he let Ash hear it.

Ash opened the door.

Korel was at the south table. Korel rose. Korel walked him down the stair without speaking, which was the kindness of a man who had walked many people down many stairs and had learned which ones did not want a sentence at the bottom.

At the cooperage door Korel said:

"You all right."

"Yes."

"All right."

Korel went back up.

* * *

Stonecurve had its evening shape.

The lamps along the curve were lit and the lamps were lit by Rakel's replacement, who was a younger lamplighter named Sun, and Sun trimmed the wicks the way Rakel had trimmed them before the pool had thinned, which was to say correctly, and the lamps along Stonecurve were the only lamps in the lower city Ash walked past anymore where the flame sat at the right height in the cup. He walked the curve. He noted the lamps. He went to the workshop.

The workshop was not empty.

Tesha was at the bench.

She was at the bench he had given her, which was the third bench, the one against the east wall under the high window where the morning light came in at a useful angle. She had a pump-head open in the clamp and the parts laid out the way Haran had taught her to lay them out, which was the way a person laid them out who intended to put them back in the order she had taken them off. She had a small notebook open at her elbow. She was writing in it.

She looked up.

"Ash."

"Tesha."

"You look like the fifth hour."

"I went to the Hall."

"Mm."

She did not ask what the Hall had been. She went back to the pump-head. He hung his coat. He went to his own bench. He picked up the gear he had filed that morning. The teeth were clean. He set the gear down. He picked it up again.

She said, without looking up:

"Pell's wife is on the Sixth Ward list."

He set the gear down.

"Pell's wife," Ash said.

"Branner's daughter. She's on it."

"Branner."

"Mm."

"How," Ash said.

"They're estimating off the household. Branner is the elder. They list the household. The household is on the list."

"The press is listing households."

"This week, yes."

He sat down at the bench. Tesha did not look up. She was working the seal off the pump-head with the small wooden pick Haran kept for the seals because metal scratched the seat. She worked it carefully. She worked it the way a person worked a thing she had worked twenty times, which she had, and the carefulness was not the carefulness of a beginner, it was the carefulness of a person who had learned that the seal seat was the thing that decided whether the next pump would last a year or a week.

"Tesha," Ash said.

"Mm."

"Are you going to a meeting tonight."

She did not answer for a moment.

She set the wooden pick down. She set it down at the angle a person set a tool down when the person was about to say a sentence that needed both hands free. She put both hands flat on the bench. She looked at him.

"Yes."

"All right."

"I've been going for three weeks."

"All right."

"I didn't tell you because, because I didn't know how to say it without saying I was leaving."

"You're not leaving."

"I'm not leaving."

"All right."

"I want to be clear about that, Ash. I'm not leaving the project. I'm not stopping the work. Mira's coming with me on the Tuesdays. Pell knows. Reska knows. I'm not, I'm not hiding."

"All right."

She picked the pick up. She did not use it. She held it.

"They're talking about my life, Ash. The Levelers. They're talking about my mother and my brother and the building I grew up in and the way the heating in that building has worked or hasn't worked since I was small, and they're talking about it the way you talk about a problem you're going to solve. The project, the project, this," she lifted her chin at the bench and the workshop and the curve outside the window, "the project says learn to live without magic. The Levelers say you shouldn't have to. And I, I don't know how to hold both of those without picking one, and I don't want to pick one."

He was quiet.

"Tesha," Ash said.

"Mm."

"What's the question."

She set the pick down. She turned on the stool to face him. She had a small smear of pump-grease at the corner of her jaw she did not know was there. He did not tell her.

"Why can't we do both?" she said.

He did not answer right away.

He looked at the gear on his bench. He looked at the pump-head in her clamp. He looked at the high window where the morning light came in at a useful angle and that, at the fifth hour, was already grey. He looked at his hands. The cut at the base of the thumb had closed again. The small line of black at the cuticle from the lamp at the canal stair was still there. He had not washed it off. He had walked to the Hall with it. He had walked to the Open Hand with it. He had walked here with it.

"I don't know," he said.

She watched him.

"You don't know."

"I don't know, Tesha. I have an answer that isn't a good one. I have an answer that says one of them takes the room and the other doesn't get built, and I don't think that's the right answer, but it's the answer I have."

"Which one takes the room."

"In the answer I have? The Levelers. Because urgency is louder than building. Because *now* is louder than *next year.* Because a child carrying water tonight is louder than a pump that will work in a winter she may not see."

"And then the project doesn't get built."

"And then the project doesn't get built. Or it gets built later, smaller, by people who used the rest of the magic on the now and didn't have anything left for the building."

"That's a bad answer."

"It is."

"You said it's the answer you have."

"I said I don't think it's the right answer."

"Ash."

"Mm."

"I don't want you to give me the answer you have. I want you to give me the answer you don't have yet. And I'll wait for it."

He looked at her.

She had a smear of grease at her jaw. She had the pump-head open in the clamp and the parts laid out and a notebook with her own hand in it, and she had been going to Leveler meetings for three weeks and had not told him, and she was telling him now, and she was, in the telling, asking him a question that nobody had thought to put to him in the shape she was putting it. The Director had asked him to oppose. The leader had asked him not to oppose. The worker was asking him to imagine.

"All right," he said.

"All right?"

"I'll work on it."

"Take your time," Tesha said.

"I will."

"Not too much time."

"No."

She turned back to the pump-head. She picked up the pick. She worked the seal the rest of the way off, and she set it on the cloth, and she looked at the seat with the lamp she pulled close, and she said, without looking at him:

"Eat something. Haran said you didn't."

"Haran told you."

"He told Mira. Mira told me."

"All right."

"There's bread on the shelf. The brown one."

He stood. He went to the shelf. The bread was the brown loaf his mother had brought down on Tuesday because his mother had decided, after the dinner, that she was going to bring bread to the workshop instead of to Maren's office, because Maren had told her not to bring bread to the office, and his mother had heard the don't and had moved the bread sideways. He cut a slice. He ate it standing.

He looked at the gear on the bench.

He looked at the pump-head in her clamp.

He looked at the broadsheet, which Haran had set under the brass weight, and which someone, in his absence, had refolded and set back. The fold was a different fold than Haran's. The fold was Tesha's. The brass weight had been moved a quarter inch. The headline was still face up.

He thought about the lattice and the bath-house and the south escarpment. He thought about the list on the wall and the boys at the corner. He thought about Davan's hands flat on the desk. He thought about the woman at the cloth stall saying *Branner.* He thought about Pell's wife. He thought about the cracked bowl in his mother's hands at the lit window. He thought about Maren at the basin with the water that had come on the second pulse.

He thought about the question.

*Why can't we do both.*

He set the bread down.

He picked up the file. He picked up the gear. He set the gear in the small clamp at the corner of the bench. He looked at the next tooth and at the angle the eye did not want, and then at the angle the eye did not want from the other side, and he filed.

Tesha, at her bench, worked the seal seat clean.

The lamp by the door burned down a quarter and Sun came in to trim it without speaking and went out, and the file was the file, and the gear was the gear, and the tooth came clean.

He filed the next.
