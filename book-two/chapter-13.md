# Chapter 13

Haran came in with the broadsheets under his arm and a stranger at his shoulder.

The stranger was a woman in a Stonecurve coat, gray at the cuffs, a wool scarf pushed back from her throat. She had the look of a person who had walked too fast and was now standing still and did not yet know what to do with her hands. She kept them in her pockets. Then took them out. Then put them back.

"This is Edda," Haran said. "She came to find me at the curve. I told her she'd want to find you instead."

"Sit," Ash said.

She did not sit. She looked at the bench. She looked at the door she had come through. She looked at Haran, and Haran put the broadsheets on the bench under the brass weight and stepped back from them the way a man stepped back from a thing he wanted another man to read without him standing over it.

"Edda."

"I'm sorry. I, I came up the hill. I haven't, I don't usually."

"Sit."

She sat. She sat on the stool Tesha used in the mornings and did not lean on the bench and did not put her hands on it. She put them in her lap. They were red at the knuckles. Cold, or scrubbed. He could not tell.

"Tell him what you told me," Haran said.

She told him.

She told it the way a person told a thing she had been telling herself on the walk up, in pieces, not in order, with the pieces she could not yet say going by in the small breaths between the pieces she could. The accountability session. That was the phrase she used. The Levelers had called it an accountability session, and she said the phrase the way a person said a phrase she had read on a poster and was now using because she did not have her own phrase yet.

Sera. The elder. Sixty-two. Thirty years on the south block heating runs, the public buildings, the laundry, the two bath-houses on the canal, the long warming circuit that ran under the children's ward at the south hospital. Edda had grown up two streets from her. Edda's mother had washed at the public laundry that Sera had warmed for thirty winters.

The session had been in the square at the foot of the curve. They had set chairs. They had set a small platform, only one step up, the kind a person stepped onto to be seen and not the kind a person stepped onto to speak from above. There had been a banner. There had been three speakers before Sera. Young channelers, all of them. They had each gone to the public conduit at the side of the platform and laid a hand on the brass cap and channeled. Visibly. The crowd had clapped. The banner read *A pool is not a private estate.*

Sera had been invited the week before. Edda had been there when the two young men came to her door with the invitation. They had been, Edda said, polite. Nobody had said anything that was not polite. They had said *you would be welcome to contribute.* They had said *we hope you will join us.* They had said *no one is required.* The phrasing had been the phrasing Edda heard herself use now, repeating it. *No one is required.*

Sera had said yes.

Edda did not know why. She had not asked. Sera was a woman who did not get asked why, by Edda, because Edda had been small in front of her since Edda was small and had not unlearned it. Sera had said yes and on the day she had come to the square in her good coat, the dark one with the trim, and she had stepped up the one step and laid her hand on the brass cap and channeled.

The brass cap was for the south block run. The same run she had warmed for thirty years. She knew the run. She knew where the heat went, which lengths held and which lengths bled, where to push and where to ease. She channeled the way a woman channeled who had channeled this one length of the world a thousand times.

The crowd clapped.

She channeled.

The crowd kept clapping. Then it stopped clapping, the way crowds stopped, in pieces, not all at once. The young men on the platform did not say *thank you.* They did not say *that's enough.* They stood the way people stood on a platform when the thing on the platform was still happening and they had not been given a signal that it was done.

Edda did not know how long. She said she did not know. She said she had tried, on the walk up, to count it, and she could not. It had been long enough that the woman next to Edda had reached for Edda's sleeve and let go of it. It had been long enough that a man behind them had said, low, *that's enough now,* and nobody on the platform had heard him, or had heard him and not moved.

Sera had gone down on her knees first. Not all at once. The knees first, on the platform step, and then the hand that was not on the brass cap had gone to the platform for balance, and then the hand on the brass cap had stayed on the brass cap. Her face had been the color of the inside of a shell. Edda used the phrase. She said it twice. *The inside of a shell.*

Someone had come up the step. A young man. He had put his hand over Sera's hand on the cap and lifted Sera's hand off and Sera had gone the rest of the way down. They had carried her. Edda did not know who. She had not seen the carrying. By the time she had pushed forward, the platform had been emptied and the banner had been taken down and the brass cap had been closed.

"Is she alive," Ash said.

"Yes."

"Where."

"Her sister's, on the south block. The healers' woman came. She said days. Days of bed."

"Days."

"Days, she said. She said days. She didn't say worse."

He looked at Haran. Haran was at his bench, not looking at him. Haran had put the broadsheets down and stepped back and he was now turning a length of soft wire in his fingers without looking at it, the way Haran turned wire when Haran was making room.

"Edda."

"Yes."

"Thank you for coming up."

"I didn't know who else."

"Thank you."

"I'm not, I'm not with them. I'm not against. I, my brother goes. He goes to their meetings. He was there yesterday. He clapped. He clapped for the first three. He clapped for Sera too, in the beginning. He came back and he, he didn't eat. He sat at the table and he didn't eat. I asked him. He said *I clapped.* He kept saying it. *I clapped.* I came up because, I came up because I didn't know what to do with that."

"All right."

"He's a good boy."

"All right."

She put her hands in her pockets and took them out and put them in her pockets again. Haran went to the shelf and came back with the brown loaf and a knife and cut a slice and set it on the cloth at the corner of the bench in front of her and went back to his wire. She did not look at the bread. After a long minute she picked it up and held it in her lap with both hands and did not eat it.

Ash slid the broadsheets out from under the brass weight.

There were three. The press had moved fast. The press had been moving fast for a year now and had learned the speed and the speed was no longer remarkable, and so the broadsheets were on the bench at the third hour of the day after, three of them, with three different headlines, and the headlines were the thing.

The first was a Leveler sheet. *A Generation Steps Up.* Underneath, the smaller line: *Elder channeler joins public works in voluntary contribution.* The body of the column did not name Sera. The body said *a senior channeler from the south block.* The body said *contributed alongside young volunteers to the communal heating grid.* The body did not say *collapsed.* The body said *the contribution concluded after a sustained channel,* and the word *sustained* was the word the column had chosen, and the choosing had been a choosing.

The second was a moderate sheet, the one his father read. *Public Session Ends in Collapse.* Body: facts. The square. The platform. The duration as nearly as the writer could fix it, which was longer than Edda's count and shorter than what Ash had pictured in the telling. The healer's name. The diagnosis as a phrase the healer had given and the writer had set in quotes: *channeling exhaustion, severe.* The writer did not editorialize. The writer placed two facts next to each other and let the placing be the thing. *No one on the platform intervened to end the contribution.* And below it: *No one in the audience stepped forward.*

The third was the older paper, the one with the gold-leaf header that nobody under forty read anymore. *The Cost of Coercion.* The column was a column the column-writer had been wanting to write for a year and had now found the occasion for. Ash read three lines and stopped. The column-writer was not wrong. The column-writer was also not, in the lines he read, useful. He set the third sheet aside.

He read the first again.

He read the word *sustained.*

"Edda."

"Yes."

"Eat the bread."

She ate the bread. She ate it the way a person ate a thing she had been handed and had decided to honor by eating, in small pieces, with the hand that was not in her pocket. He waited until she was done. He waited until she had brushed the crumbs into her palm and set the palm on the cloth and brushed the crumbs from the palm onto the cloth and folded the cloth.

"I'd like to tell people," she said.

"What you saw."

"What I saw. I don't know who. I came up because, because Haran was the one I, the one I knew. But I don't know if I should. My brother. My, my street. I don't know if it makes it better, or if it just, if it just makes it a, a side."

"You should tell who you trust."

"Mm."

"And eat. At home. Eat tonight."

"Yes."

She stood. Haran stood. Haran walked her to the door, which was three steps, and said something at the door Ash did not hear, and she went out, and the workshop was the workshop again, and the broadsheets were on the bench.

Haran came back. He did not sit. He stood at his bench and turned the wire in his fingers and put it back on the rack.

"That'll be the shape of it for a week," Haran said. "Three sheets, three headlines. The rest will pick a side and copy."

"Yes."

"The healer's word will hold."

"Will it."

"For people who read past the first column. Yes."

"That's not most people."

"No."

Ash put the brass weight back over the broadsheets. He put it slightly off-center, because the bench was not flat at that corner and the weight rocked if he set it true. He picked up the gear he had filed yesterday. He set it down. He picked up the file. He set it down.

"I'm going to walk."

"All right."

"To the south block."

Haran did not answer at once. Haran turned, with his hand on the wire, and looked at him from the angle Haran looked at things from when Haran was not looking at the thing he was looking at.

"To see her."

"No."

"All right."

"To see Davan."

"Mm."

Haran took the wire down. He coiled it once around his finger. He uncoiled it. He set it back on the rack at the angle it had been at before he had touched it, which was Haran's particular kind of putting-things-back, and which was, today, the thing it was.

"Decide on the stair after," Haran said.

"You said that yesterday."

"I'm saying it again."

"All right."

"And eat."

Ash took his coat down from the peg. He did not eat. He stopped at the door and looked back at the bench and at the broadsheets under the brass weight, and Haran was at his bench, not looking, and the workshop was the workshop, and he went out.

* * *

The walk to the south block was the walk he had taken in the first weeks of the project, before the curve had been the curve, before Tesha had been at the next bench, before the pump-head had been the pump-head and the seal seat had been the seal seat. It was a walk he had walked in summer and walked again in autumn and was walking now in the late-winter grey that the city did not have a name for because the city pretended it did not have it.

He walked slow.

He did not walk slow on purpose. He walked slow because his hands in his pockets were his hands in his pockets, and his hands were carrying the way the rest of him was not yet carrying, and the slowness was the carrying. He passed the corner with the bakery that opened at the fourth hour and the corner after it where the boys had stood for a week with the chalked list and were not standing today. He passed the cloth stall. The woman at the cloth stall was the woman at the cloth stall. She did not call out. He did not nod. The not-nodding was not a not-nodding. It was the thing his face was doing, which was the thing a face did when the man behind it had not decided what the face was going to do.

He came down the long street toward the square at the foot of the curve.

The square was not the square Edda had described. There was no platform. There was no banner. There was a man with a broom sweeping the place where the platform had been, and the sweeping was the kind of sweeping a man did when he had been told by someone to sweep a place that did not need sweeping, because the not-needing was the message. At the side of the square, against the south wall, the brass cap stood on its short brick column. A length of new pipe ran from it under the cobbles toward the south block. The cap had been polished. He could see the polish from across the square.

He did not cross to it.

He went around. He took the lane that ran behind the square toward the Levelers' meeting house, which had been a chandler's two years ago and was now what it was. There was a sign. The sign was new. The sign was small. *Solathis Mutual Pool.* The phrasing had moved. Last year it had been *the Levelers.* The year before, *the young.* The phrasing had moved and had taken a building with it, and the building had a polished brass plate by the door, and the brass plate was the brass plate.

He stopped on the step.

He did not go up. He stood at the bottom of the step, because going up the step was the thing a man did who had decided, and he had not decided. Haran's voice was somewhere behind his ear. *Decide on the stair after.* He stood. The door opened from the inside before he had decided whether to knock. Davan was in the doorway.

Davan was the way Davan was. He had the grey at the temples that had come in over the year, a year that had given him grey at the temples and a building with a brass plate and a paper that printed three thousand. He had a jacket on, indoors, because the meeting house's front room was warmed by a heating run that someone, somewhere, was channeling for, and it was not warming the front room well. He stood in the doorway and looked at Ash and did not say anything for a moment.

"Come in," Davan said.

"Out here."

"It's cold."

"Out here."

Davan did not argue. He stepped down. He pulled the door to behind him without closing it all the way, and the front room behind him was a low murmur of voices, and the murmur did not stop, and the murmur was the thing the building did.

"You came to talk."

"Yes."

"Walk with me, then."

They walked.

They walked the lane behind the meeting house, which gave on the alley behind the south block, which gave on the long straight street that ran under the warming circuit. The pipe ran under their feet. He could feel the place where the cobbles had been lifted and reset. He could feel the warmth, faintly, where the run was running. He did not look at Davan. Davan did not look at him. They walked with their hands in their pockets like two men who had agreed, without saying so, that the walking was the room they were going to have.

"She collapsed," Ash said. "Your people watched her collapse and they didn't stop it."

Davan did not answer for half a step.

"She was contributing to a community project. Voluntarily. People overextend themselves at work every day."

"She was performing for a crowd that would have called her a hoarder if she'd stopped. That's not voluntary. That's coercion with a civic face."

Davan stopped.

He stopped at the corner where the lane gave on the long street, and he took his hands out of his pockets and put them back, the way Edda had done in the workshop, and the doing of it was the thing Ash registered. Davan's hands did not, usually, not know what to do.

"What did you expect," Davan said.

"Davan."

"No, Ash. What did you actually expect. You have an ocean of power. She had a cup. She can refill it tomorrow. Tomorrow her pool will regenerate and she'll be fine. Your reservoir never regenerates and you're spending it on plumbing. Who's being more reckless with their power."

"This isn't about me."

"It's always about you."

A man came out of a door across the street and stopped, looking at them, and turned and went the other way.

"It's always about you," Davan said again, quieter. "You're the most powerful person alive. You could heat every building in this city for a year and still have power left. Instead you're building hand-crank pumps and teaching people to insulate their walls with fiber. Every day you spend on your workshop project is a day the powerless go without what you could give them. You ask me about one woman who overexerted herself. I'm asking you about every child who's cold tonight because the Wellspring is building toys."

Ash did not answer.

He looked at the cobbles. He looked at the place where the cobbles had been lifted and reset. He looked at his hands in his pockets and did not take them out, because the taking-out would have been a doing, and there was no doing he had that was the doing for this.

"Sera is sixty-two," he said. "She gave thirty years."

"She gave thirty years to whom."

"To the city."

"To the laundry, Ash. To the bath-house. To buildings that took her warmth and gave her, what. The right to be a senior. The right to be asked nicely. She gave thirty years and she lived in a two-room flat on the south block and her sister works at the press for piecework, and we asked her to give an afternoon, and she said yes, and she gave it, and she'll be back in her flat in three days, and the flat will be warmer this winter than last because of what she did."

"She is in her sister's bed."

"She is in her sister's bed for three days."

"She went down on her knees."

"At work, Ash. People go down on their knees at work. People at the press go down at their knees in the third hour of the second shift and nobody writes a column about it. Nobody writes a column about Edda's mother at the laundry. Nobody writes a column about the boy at the lime kiln who lost the use of his right arm last spring. They write a column about Sera because Sera is a channeler, and a channeler going down is the story, and a girl going down at the press is not the story, and the story is the story you have been letting be the story all your life."

The wind came down the long street and lifted the corner of a poster pasted to the wall and let it go. The poster snapped flat. He looked at it. He did not read it.

"You're using her," Ash said.

"We invited her."

"You invited her in a square. With a platform. With a banner. With three young men who had already gone before her and a crowd that was already clapping. You invited her the way the Hall invites the men in the third-floor offices. You invited her with a script and a stage and a *no one is required* that everyone in the square knew was a yes."

"You're saying she didn't choose."

"I'm saying you built the room she chose in."

"And you don't build the rooms people choose in. The Wellspring doesn't build rooms. The Wellspring is a private workshop with a brass weight on a broadsheet. Is that right."

"That's not fair."

"It's not. I know."

Davan looked at him. He looked at him the way Davan looked at things, which was the way of a man who had decided some time ago that looking at the thing was the work, and the work was the work, and he was going to do it.

"Ash."

"Mm."

"You're asking the drowning to be patient while you decide which lifeboat to build."

He did not have an answer.

He had an answer that began with *the lifeboat that floats* and dissolved before the period. He had an answer that began with *the river, not the swimmer* and dissolved at the comma. He had an answer that began with *Sera* and stopped at the name. He had answers. None of them were not the answer of a man with an ocean in his chest standing on a street where the pipe under his feet was warmed by a woman in her sister's bed.

He looked at Davan.

Davan looked tired. Davan had looked tired for a year. The grey at the temples was not the tiredness. The tiredness was at the corners of the mouth, at the way Davan's shoulders had set, at the way Davan's hand had gone, twice in this conversation, to the inside of his jacket where, Ash knew, Davan kept a folded sheet of paper that was not for Ash to see. Davan was carrying a building and a paper and three thousand readers and a square with a brass cap and a woman in her sister's bed, and he was carrying it because nobody else was going to, and he had, at the corner of his mouth, the look of a man who had stopped expecting to be told he was carrying it well.

"Davan."

"Ash."

"I don't have an answer that isn't, that doesn't sound like privilege."

"No."

"I came down to tell you to stop."

"I know."

"I'm not going to."

"No."

"But I came down to."

"I know you did."

They stood. The wind came again. The poster did not lift this time. A boy went past on the other side of the street with a basket and did not look at them. Across the street, in the door the man had come out of, a woman stood in the doorway and watched, and saw what she was watching, and stepped back into the dark of the room and pulled the door to.

"Will you go up to her," Ash said.

"I went last night."

"How is she."

"She is in her sister's bed."

"Davan."

"She's tired. She's going to be tired for a week. The healer says no lasting harm. The healer also said to her, when she could speak, that she had channeled past the line a person her age channels past, and that next time she should stop sooner, and Sera said, the healer told me, Sera said *I didn't want to be the one who stopped first.*"

He did not answer.

"That's the part I'm carrying," Davan said. "You can carry the part you're carrying. I'll carry that one."

"All right."

"Ash."

"Mm."

"This is the break, isn't it."

"Yes."

"All right."

"Davan."

"Mm."

"I respect you."

"I know."

"I don't know how to do this and respect you. But I respect you."

"I know, Ash."

They did not embrace. They did not shake hands. Ash took his hand half out of his pocket and put it back, because the taking-out had been a reflex from a country they did not live in anymore, and Davan saw the half-motion and the putting-back and did not say anything, and that was the thing.

Davan turned. He went back up the lane. He went up at a normal pace, neither fast nor slow, and he did not look back, and when he reached the door of the meeting house he went in and the door closed and the murmur inside, briefly, dipped, and rose again.

Ash stood at the corner where the lane gave on the long street.

He stood for a count he did not count. The pipe under the cobbles ran. The wind did not lift the poster. He took his hand out of his pocket and looked at it, and the cut at the base of the thumb was the cut at the base of the thumb, and the small line of black at the cuticle from the lamp at the canal stair was still there, and he had not washed it off, and he put his hand back in his pocket.

He walked.

* * *

He did not go back up the way he had come.

He went the long way. He went along the south block first, because the south block was where the warming circuit ran, and he wanted to walk the length of it before he went anywhere else.

The communal grid was warm.

He did not need to put his hand on the cobbles to know. He could feel it through his boots, in the places where the run came near the surface, the way a man could feel the difference between a stretch of street with a pipe under it and a stretch of street with no pipe under it, after he had walked enough of both. He walked it. The vents at the side of the south block buildings, the small brass grates the Levelers had installed, were running. A woman in a doorway had her hands held out toward one. A child sat on the step of the next building and had taken his shoes off and had his bare feet pressed to the brass. The child was reading a paper. The paper was a Leveler sheet. The child was about nine.

At the far end of the south block, at the short street that ran to the canal, he passed the public laundry. The doors were open. Steam came out the open doors in a slow roll. Inside, women worked at the long stone basins, and the basins were warm, and the warmth was not Sera's today, because Sera was in her sister's bed, and the warmth was the warmth of the new arrangement, the pooled young, the ones who had clapped before her on the platform. He could see one of them in a corner of the laundry, sitting on a stool, her hand on a brass cap inside, her face calm. She was not pushing. She was easing. She channeled the way a person channeled who had not yet learned the depth of her own pool, who was still in the part of the work where the work was generous.

It was working.

He stood at the open door and watched it work for the count of a minute. The women at the basins did not look at him. A woman near the door looked at him once, a woman his mother's age, and looked away, and the looking-away was not a hostility. The looking-away was the looking-away of a woman at work who did not have the time to manage what a man in her doorway might be about. He stepped back from the door.

He walked.

He came up out of the south block and across the canal at the second bridge and up the long climb toward the curve. He took the climb at the pace he could take it without thinking about the pace. He did not think about the pace. He thought about the child with his bare feet on the brass. He thought about the young woman in the corner of the laundry with her hand on the cap. He thought about Sera in her sister's bed. He thought about *I didn't want to be the one who stopped first.* He thought about the brass cap polished in the empty square.

It was working.

It was also Sera in her sister's bed.

Both were true. Both stayed true the rest of the climb. He did not fix them. He did not put one above the other. He carried them up the curve, one in each pocket with his hands, and the carrying was the climb, and the climb was the carrying, and the two things did not become one thing.

* * *

At the top of the curve he did not go to the workshop.

He went to Building Six.

Building Six was the building where the seal had failed in the autumn and Tesha had taught the residents the seal with the wooden pick and the lamp at the close angle. The pump in the yard at the back was the pump that ran at eighty percent of its design pressure, because the elevation of the cistern had been six inches lower than the survey had said, and the six inches had been a thing they had argued about for a week and had finally accepted, because the alternative was tearing out the cistern, and tearing out the cistern was not the alternative.

The pump was running.

He stood at the gate of the small yard. He did not go in. The pump was running and the man at the pump was Branner, who was the man whose daughter was on the Sixth Ward list, and Branner was working the handle in the long slow rhythm that Tesha had taught him, and the rhythm was the rhythm, and the water was coming up the pipe and going into the cistern and the cistern was filling at the rate the pump filled it at, which was the rate of eighty percent.

It was working.

It would work in ten years. The seal would have been replaced four times by then, and Branner would have taught his daughter to replace it, and his daughter would have taught the boy in the next building, and the pump in the yard would still be the pump in the yard, and the rhythm at the handle would still be the rhythm, and the water would still come up at eighty percent, and the cistern would still fill, and the building would still drink.

It was also Branner working the handle in the late afternoon, alone, with a daughter on a list, and a Leveler grid two streets away running warm through brass vents that a child could press his bare feet against, and a woman at a stone basin whose hands were warm because a girl Sera's grandniece's age was sitting on a stool with her palm on a cap, and the girl was easing, not pushing, and the easing was working too, and the easing was working now, and the pump was working in ten years.

Both true.

Both insufficient.

He stood at the gate.

Branner saw him. Branner did not stop the rhythm. Branner nodded at him over the pump handle, the small tilt of the chin a man gave another man at work, and Ash nodded back, and Branner kept working the handle, and Ash did not go in.

He stood at the gate a count he did not count.

The reservoir sat in his chest the way it sat. He could feel the edges of it. He could feel the volume that was, at the edge of his attention, thirty-seven of one hundred, the way a man could feel the level of water in a cup he was carrying without looking at the cup. He did not channel. He had not channeled in a week. He had not channeled since he had filed the gear for the third tooth and the fourth, and the not-channeling was not a refusing, and the not-channeling was not a saving, and the not-channeling was the thing his chest was doing while the rest of him walked, and the rest of him had walked today to a square and a lane and a brass cap and a sister's flat he had not gone to and a laundry and a yard, and the walking had not fixed the not-channeling, and the not-channeling had not fixed the walking.

Branner worked the handle.

Ash did not go in.

He turned at last. He went up the curve. He went up past the new sign on the chandler's that was now what it was, which he saw from the upper street and did not stop at. He went up past the corner where the boys had stood for a week and were not standing today. He went up past the bakery that had closed for the afternoon, the door pulled to, the cloth in the window. He went up to the workshop.

The lamp by the door was lit. Sun had trimmed it. The bell that Haran had not hung back was still not hung. He pushed the door.

Haran was at his bench. Tesha was at hers. Neither looked up. Tesha was working the seat of a different pump-head, a smaller one, with the pick. Haran was bending wire. The broadsheets were under the brass weight, slightly off-center, where Ash had left them.

He hung his coat.

He went to the basin and washed his hands. The black at the cuticle came off this time. He had used the small brush. He dried his hands on his front. He came to the bench.

"Davan," Haran said. He did not look up.

"Yes."

"And."

"It's the break."

"Mm."

Haran bent the wire one more time. He set it on the rack. He did not say *I'm sorry.* He did not say *that's hard.* He turned, with one hand still on the rack, and looked at Ash from the angle Haran looked at things from when Haran was not looking, and he said:

"Eat."

"I will."

"Now."

He went to the shelf. The brown loaf was the brown loaf. He cut a slice. He stood at the shelf and ate it. He ate it in small pieces, the way Edda had eaten it. He brushed the crumbs into his palm and brushed them onto the cloth and folded the cloth.

He went to his bench.

He picked up the gear. The teeth were clean. He set it down. He picked up the file. He set it down. He looked at the pump-head in Tesha's clamp. He looked at the broadsheets under the brass weight. He looked at the high window where the late light had gone grey and was going greyer.

Tesha did not look up.

"Ash."

"Mm."

"You want to talk."

"No."

"All right."

She worked the seat. The pick made the small dry sound the pick made when the seat was nearly clean.

He sat. He put his hands flat on the bench. He looked at his hands. The cut at the base of the thumb had closed. The line of black was gone. The hands were the hands.

*Both true.*

He did not say it. He did not need to. The thinking of it was the thing the thinking was, and the thing went down into him and sat down where the not-channeling sat, and the two of them sat together, not arguing, not resolving, the way two things sat when a man had walked them up a long curve at the end of a long day and had not yet been given the room to set them down.

He picked up the file.

He picked up the gear. He set it in the small clamp at the corner of the bench.

He looked at the next tooth. He looked at it from the angle the eye wanted, and then from the angle the eye did not want, and then from the angle the eye did not want from the other side, and he filed.

The burr came off in two passes.

He filed the next.

Tesha worked the seat. Haran bent the wire. The lamp by the door burned. Outside the window the curve went down to the south block and the south block went down to the canal and the canal went under the second bridge and the second bridge gave on the long street under the warming circuit, and the warming circuit was warm, and Branner was at the handle in the yard, and Sera was in her sister's bed, and the city, between them, was the city.

He filed the third tooth.

The burr came off.

He did not have an answer.

He had the shape of two things that were both true, and he had carried them up the curve, and they were still both true, and they were on the bench now next to the gear, and they did not become one thing, and they were not going to become one thing tonight, and the not-becoming was, he understood, the work.

He filed the fourth.
