# Chapter 10

The boy at the door was breathing hard.

He had run. Ash registered the run before he registered the boy: the chest moving, the cap held in the hand and not on the head, the small wet shine on the upper lip where a runner's breath had condensed in the cold. The boy was nine, ten. The boy was Reska's. Ash had seen him in the square at the third hour with a basket.

"Building Six," the boy said.

"Mm."

"Tova said come."

"Tova said come now, or come when I could."

The boy thought about it. The boy had been told one thing and was, in the small honesty of a nine-year-old, going to give the one thing.

"Now," he said.

Ash took his coat off the peg.

Haran did not look up from the bench. Haran was at the small clamp with a piece of tin and the shears, and the shears were the shears, and the tin was a length of stovepipe collar he had been cutting since the second hour, and he did not look up because not-looking-up was, this morning, the courtesy.

"Six," Ash said.

"Mm."

"I'll head over. Should be back before noon, probably, depends what Tova's, yeah. Back before noon."

"Mm."

He went out after the boy.

* * *

The square at the second hour was the square at the second hour. Frost on the stones at the north edge where the sun did not yet reach. The tap running. Vell at the basin with a bucket and the morning slate already chalked. She lifted her chin a quarter when he passed and did not stop pumping.

The boy ran ahead.

The boy ran the way a boy ran who had been told to run and was, in the running, a boy who had a job. He cut between the bakery and the cellar door of Block Three and out into the lane behind, and Ash followed at a long stride, and the lane was the lane, and Building Six sat at the end of it in the morning light with its southern face the southern face Jora had reseated in the fifth week and the cistern stack on the roof catching a thin first sun.

Tova was at the door.

Ash did not know Tova well. He knew her name. He knew she had come on the morning crew in the seventh week, late, after two of the original crew had quit, and he knew she had been trained on Building Six by Reska across four mornings, and that the training had been operation, not repair. He had signed off on the training the way he had signed off on twelve other trainings in the year. He had not, in the signing, asked himself the question Davan had asked him on the bench three nights ago.

She was waiting with her arms crossed.

She uncrossed them when she saw him. She did not, in the uncrossing, smile. She did the small tight motion of a person who had been standing alone with a problem for an hour and was, in the seeing of him, allowed to stop being the only person standing with it.

"Pump," she said.

"Show me. I mean, yeah, show me."

She turned. He followed her in.

The mechanism was in the cellar. He had built the mechanism in the second week of the second month. He remembered the building of it the way he remembered every piece of work he had laid Wellspring power into, which was the way a person remembered a room they had carried a heavy thing into, the angle of the door, the catch of the threshold, the place the light fell. He came down the cellar stairs and the lamp was lit on the wall hook and the pump sat under it on the brick pad, and the seal at the lower joint had failed, and he saw the failure before Tova said the word.

"Seal," she said.

"Yes."

"It went at the half-hour. I primed her twice. The second time it sprayed."

"How much."

"A pint. Maybe two."

"On you?"

"On the floor."

"Mm. Okay. Mm."

He crouched at the pump. He laid a hand on the housing. The brass was cold. The seal had not been a brass seal, in the building of it. It had been a fused boundary, channeled, a place where two materials had been made to behave, under his hand, as one material. He had done that on every joint in the western pump line because doing it had been faster than ordering gaskets from the harbor and faster than teaching the third building's crew how to set them.

The faster had been, he registered now with the small particular pull of a man finding a thing he had already found and was finding again, the wrong faster.

"Step back," he said. "Just, sorry, give me, give me the room."

She stepped back.

He set his palm on the joint. The reservoir was where the reservoir was, behind the back of his attention, steady, full, the way a room was full of air. He drew. He drew the small careful draw of a man who had spent a year learning the small careful draw, and the seal re-fused under his hand the way a seam closed under heat, and the brass warmed at the joint and the warmth ran a quarter-inch up and down the housing and stopped where he had told it to stop, and he lifted his hand.

He blew on the joint.

Habit. The blowing did nothing. He had blown on hot solder as a child in his father's kitchen and the habit was the habit.

"Try her. Sorry, I mean, give her a crank."

She came forward. She set her hand on the crank. She ran the count Jora had taught the morning crew, *one, two, three, prime,* and the prime took, and the water came up the tube, and the housing did not weep at the joint, and the pump cycled. Once. Twice. The third stroke set the manifold ticking.

"It's good," she said.

"It's good."

"Twenty minutes."

"Mm."

She looked at him. She held the look for a beat longer than the look needed to be held. She was, in the holding, making a decision about whether to say a thing. The thing was a thing he could see arriving in her face and he did not, in the seeing, help her with it, because the helping would have been the helping a powerful person did when he wanted to spare himself the shape of what was being said.

She did not say it.

She said: "Thank you."

"Welcome. Yeah. Welcome."

He went up the cellar stairs and out into the lane and back across the square the way he had come, and the square was the square, and Vell was off the basin and Reska was on with a younger woman Ash did not know by name, and the tap ran, and the boy who had fetched him was nowhere, and Ash walked back to the workshop with the small twenty-minute warmth of a thing fixed in his hand and, in his chest, a thing that was not warmth at all.

*She sent for the Wellspring.*

He thought it walking. He thought it the way a man thought a sentence that had been waiting for the morning a small specific morning would finally seat it. *She did not send for Reska. She did not send for Jora. She did not send for Haran. She sent for me.* The pump was not a magical pump. The pump was a mechanical pump. He had built it, in the building of it, with one channeled seal at the lower joint and a second at the upper and a third at the manifold, because the building of it that way had taken him a morning, and the building of it the other way would have taken Jora's crew a week, and a week, in the tenth week of the project, had felt like a week he did not have.

He had built it the way he thought.

He thought it in his father's voice for half a step. *You built it the way you think, Ashlyn.* His father had not said the sentence. His father would not say the sentence. The sentence was the sentence in the air between his father and him for years before his father had stopped saying anything that resembled it, and he heard it, this morning, with no one's voice attached, and the sentence walked beside him across the square.

He went into the workshop.

Haran was at the bench. The tin collar was in the clamp. The shears were down. Haran had a small file in his hand and was working a burr at the lip of the collar, and he did not look up.

"Six," Haran said.

"Twenty minutes. The lower seal had let go and I, yeah. Twenty minutes."

"Mm."

Ash took his coat off.

He stood at the bench. He laid his palm on the beech. The beech was warm where the lamp had been. He did not, in the laying, say the thing that was in his chest. He did not say it because saying it to Haran would have been asking Haran for an answer, and he had not, this morning, earned the answer.

He picked up the wire on the back bench. He wound it. He wound it for the count of a hundred. The wire did not need winding.

* * *

The flue in Block Three drew poorly on a Threeday.

A woman on the second floor sent her daughter down with a note. The note was three lines. The note was not a note Ash needed to read twice. He read it once, at the bench, with Haran working tin in the corner of his eye, and he set it down.

"Block Three. Flue, sounds like. I'll go."

"Mm."

He went.

He went up the back stair to the second flat. He stood in the kitchen with the woman, whose name was Ema, and her daughter, whose name was a name Ash would not later be able to remember because he had been, at the moment of the introduction, looking at the flue. The flue was the flue Jora had set in the third week. Jora had set it well. The flue was not the problem. The problem was the small cap above the second elbow, where the rise turned, and the cap had been a cap Ash had channeled to seat against the brick when the brick had refused to take a wedge, and the seat had loosened, and the cap was riding a sixteenth of an inch high, and the draw was, at a sixteenth of an inch, the draw of a flue with a leak.

He laid a hand on the cap. He drew. The cap reseated.

The fire in the stove, which had been smoking, stopped smoking inside of three breaths.

Ema thanked him. He said welcome. He went down the back stair and across the square, and the small particular thought he had carried across the square three days ago walked beside him across the square again, and this time he did not need to think it. The thought was there. It had been there since Tuesday. It had not, in three days, gone.

He went into the workshop. Haran did not look up.

"Three. The cap above the second elbow, it, yeah. It rode."

"Mm."

"The cap above the second elbow."

"Mm."

"It rode."

"Mm."

Haran filed.

* * *

The eastern row took a cold snap on Fourth-day night and the insulation on the upper run cracked across two flats.

He went at the second hour. He took the wrap apart with his hands, which was the part Jora's crew could have done, and then he reset the lath and the rag wool and the pitch, which was the part Jora's crew could also have done, and then he laid a channeled seam down the inside of the new wrap to bind the rag to the lath at the joint, which was the part Jora's crew could not do, because they had not been taught the seam, because the seam had been the part Ash did, on every wrap, in the year of building, because the seam was how the wrap held in a cold snap.

He did not, in the laying of the seam, hurry.

He did not hurry because hurrying would have meant skipping the small accounting he was, this morning, doing in his head. *Half a percent. Pump on Tuesday. Flue on Threeday. Wrap this morning.* He ran the numbers under the surface of the work. *Half a percent in two weeks. Sustainable for one district. Impossible for ten. Impossible for a quarter.*

The wrap held.

He went home across the square.

* * *

The cistern valve stuck on Sixth-day.

It stuck the way a valve stuck when its housing had been reground at installation by a man with a Wellspring for a finishing tool, which was to say it stuck because the tolerances were tighter than any tolerance a wrench could correct. He went. Reska met him at the cistern door with a wrench in her hand and the wrench was the wrench she had used to try to clear it, and her knuckles were bleeding a little where the wrench had skipped the head of the bolt, and she did not, when he came up the ladder, say anything at all.

She held out the wrench.

He did not take it.

"Show me what you tried. I mean, sorry, walk me through, yeah."

She showed him.

She had tried the bolt. She had tried the housing. She had tried, when neither of those moved, to crack the seat with the heel of the wrench, the way a person cracked a seat when a seat had iced. She had tried the heel four times before she had sent for him. He could see the four marks on the iron.

"It's not iced," he said.

"It's not iced."

"It's the seat."

"The seat won't take the wrench."

"No."

She looked at him. She did not look at him with anger. The not-anger was, in the way her face held, harder than anger would have been.

"I sent the boy."

"I know."

"I waited the hour."

"I know. I know. Yeah."

"Mm."

He laid a hand on the housing. He drew. The valve cleared. The cistern sang the small high note a cistern sang when it was, after an hour of being held, allowed to flow.

He climbed back down the ladder.

Reska did not climb down with him. He heard her, at the top of the ladder, set the wrench in the wall hook with a small clean sound. He heard her not climb down. He went back across the square.

*Half a percent. Two weeks.*

He went into the workshop.

"Cistern. It was the seat, the, yeah. The cistern."

"Mm."

"Reska's hand was bleeding."

Haran set the file down. Haran did not look up. Haran picked up the file again, after a beat, and went back to the brass. The beat was the beat. Ash registered the beat. He went up the stair to the room above the workshop and lay on the bedroll for ten minutes with his hands flat on his chest, and he did not sleep, and the reservoir sat where the reservoir sat, and he came back down and called the meeting for the seventh hour.

* * *

They came at the seventh.

Reska first. Jora behind. Tesha with her sleeves still wet from the basin. Vell at the back, by the door, with her cap on. Tova came late, with the cellar lamp's smoke still on her sleeve. Two of the newer crew came together. A woman Ash had not learned the name of, who he made a small point of, in the introducing, learning, came in last. *Mira.* He said her name once, looking at her. He needed to remember it. He had, in the year, made a small private discipline of remembering names, and he had, in the last month, let the discipline slip.

They sat on the crates and the bench and the long box at the back wall. The lamp was on the high hook. Haran was at the back bench, winding wire on the former, doing the work he did when he was in the room and not in the room.

Ash stood at the front bench.

He did not, in the standing, give a speech.

He had thought about a speech walking back from the cistern. He had, in the thinking, drafted a speech. He had a paragraph about the proof of concept. He had a paragraph about the project's reach. He had a paragraph that referenced, lightly, the visit on Fourth-day and the question that had been asked at the door. He had revised the paragraphs three times in his head crossing the square. He had stood at the bench at the sixth-and-half hour with the paragraphs lined up, ready, and he had looked at Haran's back at the back bench and he had taken every paragraph apart.

He asked a question instead.

"Who can fix the pump seal in Building Six," he said, "without channeling?"

Silence.

It was the silence of a question whose answer the people in the room were, in the same beat, all considering. He saw Reska look at the floor. He saw Jora look at her hands. He saw Tesha look at Reska. He saw Vell, at the back, hold her face the way she held her face on the crank, level, looking at him, not answering, not looking away.

Nobody raised a hand.

He let the silence sit. He let it sit longer than the silence wanted to sit. The silence was, for what he was about to do next, the only silence the question deserved.

"The flue cap above the second elbow in Block Three," he said. "The one that rode this week. Who can reseat it?"

A hand.

Jora's. Up to the shoulder, not above the head. Her face was the face of a person who had raised a hand and was, in the raising, not sure yet what the raising committed her to.

"Uncertain?" Ash said.

"I've watched you do it."

"That's not the question."

"I know."

"Could you do it tomorrow if I were not in the workshop."

A pause.

"Maybe," Jora said.

"Mm."

He let her hand come down. He did not, in the letting, make her work to put it down. He moved to the next.

"The wrap on the upper eastern run. The one I reset on Fourth-day. Who can lay the seam?"

Two hands. Tesha's, fast. Mira's, slower, half-up, then more fully. Tesha was confident. Mira was not. He registered both. He registered, behind them, that Reska had not raised her hand, and Reska had been on the eastern row in the third week and had laid wrap with him for two days, and Reska's hand was bleeding under a bandage from the cistern bolt the wrench had skipped on, and Reska was looking at the floor.

"Tesha?"

"Yes."

"Tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow."

"Mira?"

"With Tesha."

"With Tesha."

"Yes."

"Mm."

The third question was the question he did not want to ask because he knew the answer to it. He asked it anyway. He asked it because asking it to himself was not the same as asking it in a room with twelve people on crates.

"The cistern valve," Ash said. "The one this morning. Who can clear the seat?"

Nobody.

The silence this time was a different silence. It was not the silence of consideration. It was the silence of a question that did not have an answer and that everyone in the room had, by the asking, recognized as a question that did not have an answer. Reska's hand went, in the silence, from her lap to her bandage. She held the bandage. She did not look up.

Ash stood at the bench.

He felt the bench under his palm. He felt the small ridge along the edge where Haran had run a chamfer years ago because the bench had caught a forearm too many times. He felt the cool of the vise at the heel of his hand.

He let the silence stand for a count of ten.

Then he said, quietly, the only thing he had that was true:

"I built it wrong."

Heads came up.

Not all at once. Reska first. Then Jora. Then Tesha. Tova at the back, with the lamp smoke on her sleeve. Vell, who had been looking at him already. They were not, in the looking up, surprised. They were, in the looking up, allowed.

He did not, this evening, say more than that.

"We'll start again on Firstday," he said. "Same time. Same crews. Different work."

Reska nodded. She was, in the nodding, the first person to give him a nod since he had walked into the room. The nod was small. It was enough.

They went out.

Vell went last. She stopped at the door with her hand on the frame the way she had stopped at the basin the day Davan came. She did not look back. She did not, in the not-looking-back, perform anything. She went out into the square.

Ash stood at the bench.

Haran wound wire.

* * *

He did not sleep that night.

He lay on the bedroll with the dark windows the dark windows and the reservoir the reservoir and the small accounting in his head running and re-running. *I replaced the old dependency with a new dependency. The form is different. The structure is the same.*

He turned it over. He looked for the place where it was wrong. The place where it was wrong was not in the sentence. It was earlier. It was in the morning he had built the western pump in a morning instead of in a week.

He had built the system to work without channeling. He had built the system *with* channeling. The two sentences sounded, in the year, like the same sentence. They were not the same sentence. The first was the deliverable. The second was the method. The deliverable had been correct. The method had made the deliverable opaque to every person who lived with it.

He turned on the bedroll.

He thought: *they don't understand how the pieces fit because they didn't build the pieces.*

He thought: *they watched the Wellspring pour into a wall and what came out was a wall.*

He thought: *I am the man with the wrench they don't have.*

He thought it the way he had thought, on the towpath two weeks ago, *it is not enough.* The thought arrived. It did not, after arriving, leave.

He went down to the workshop at the first hour, before Haran was up, and he made tea, and he sat on the crate by the bench with the cup in his hands and he waited for Haran to come down.

* * *

Haran came down at the second hour.

He came down the stair the way Haran came down the stair, slowly, with one hand on the rail, and he saw the tea on the bench and the cup in Ash's hand and he went to the stove and he poured himself a cup, and he sat on the stool at the back bench, and he picked up the wire on the former.

He wound for the count of twenty.

"So," Haran said.

Ash held the cup.

"I built it too well," Ash said. "I mean. That's, yeah. Too well."

"Mm."

"I built it the way I think."

"You did."

"Elegant. Efficient. Optimized. All the, yeah. All the things you don't say but, "

"Mm."

A pause.

Haran wound the wire. The wire turned on the former with the small clean sound the wire made. He set the former down. He looked, for the first time that morning, at Ash. The look was the look of a man who had been waiting, in the year, for the morning a thing would arrive, and was not, in the looking, glad it had arrived, and was not, in the looking, sorry. The look was a look that registered.

"You built it the way you think," Haran said. "Elegant, efficient, optimized."

"Yes."

"You should have built it the way they can fix it."

Ash held the cup.

"Rough," Haran said. "Obvious. Redundant."

"Yes."

A beat.

"A pipe that a person with a wrench can understand," Haran said, "is worth more than a pipe that works perfectly until it doesn't."

He did not, in the saying, weight any word more than the others. He said it the way he said *clock spring,* which was the way he said the names of things that were what they were.

Ash drank tea.

"Mm," Ash said.

He had said *mm* the way Haran said *mm.* He had been saying *mm* the way Haran said *mm* since he was fourteen. He registered, this morning, what he was registering with the *mm.*

Haran picked up the wire.

He did not, in the picking up, say more.

* * *

Firstday came.

He had spent two days at the bench before it came. He had taken apart the drawing for the second cistern's intake, which he had been working on for the next phase, and he had drawn it again. The first drawing had been three sheets and elegant. The second drawing was eight sheets and ugly. Every joint was numbered. Every joint had a part list. Every part on the part list was a part a person could buy at the harbor or scavenge from a yard. There were three places where the first drawing had a fused seam and the second drawing had a bolted flange and a gasket and a wing nut. The wing nut was the wing nut a person could turn with their fingers in a flat. The wing nut had been, in the first drawing, a decision he had not made because the fused seam had been faster.

He pinned both drawings to the back wall.

He stood and looked at them. The first was beautiful. The second was a working drawing. He did not, in the looking, mourn the first. He registered the wanting to mourn it. He set the wanting down on the bench beside the cup the way he set down a tool he was not going to use this morning.

The crews came at the seventh.

He walked them through the second drawing.

He walked them through the wing nut. He walked them through the flange. He walked them through the way the flange would weep, in the first month, and the way the weeping was the diagnostic. *If it weeps, you tighten. If it tightens and weeps again, you replace the gasket. The gasket is here.* He walked them to the box of gaskets he had laid out on the long box at the back, fifty of them, ordered at the harbor at the cost of two weeks of project budget he had been holding for something he could no longer remember.

He paired them.

Tesha with Mira. Jora with Tova. Reska with one of the newer crew, a man whose name was Pell, who had been quiet since the seventh week and who, Ash had registered without acting on the registering, had once been an apprentice fitter in the canal quarter before the canal had stopped working. Reska had her bandage off. The knuckle was a clean pink.

He stepped back from the work.

He stepped back physically. He stood at the wall, three paces from the bench Tesha and Mira were at, and he watched Tesha set a flange wrong, and he watched Mira not, in the setting wrong, correct her, because Mira was not yet in a place to correct Tesha, and he watched the flange come together wrong and seat wrong and he watched Tesha, in the seating, register that it was wrong, and look at him.

He did not move.

He looked back. He looked the way Haran looked, which was the look of a man who had, in the look, said *this is yours.* Tesha looked at the flange. Tesha took the flange apart. Tesha set it again. Mira moved her hand on the second pass and held the gasket where the gasket needed to be held, and the flange came together right.

It took an hour.

It took an hour because two people took an hour to do a thing he could have done in a minute, and the minute was the minute he had built the original western pump in, and the hour was the hour the western pump should have been built in, and the hour he was watching, this morning, was the hour the western pump's life had been short by.

He stood at the wall and watched.

He watched Jora and Tova at the second bench take twice as long as Tesha and Mira had on the first. He watched Reska teach Pell the count for priming, slowly, the count Jora had taught her, *one, two, three, prime,* and Pell repeated the count under his breath the way Vell had repeated it on the morning Davan came. He watched Pell get the count wrong on the first try and right on the second.

He went outside at the eleventh hour.

He stood in the square in the cold and he watched the western pump from the bakery wall. The pump was running. Tova was on the crank. The pump ran at the pump's rate. It cycled. He could see, from the bakery wall, the small specific stutter at the third stroke that meant the lower seal was working at, his hand registered before his head did the math, eighty percent. The upper joint was new. The crews had reset it on Tenday with a flange. The flange was the flange he had stood and watched them set. It was not the seal he would have laid. It was a flange. It worked. It worked at eighty.

He stood at the bakery wall.

He did not move toward the pump. He stood with one hand on the cold brick and the other in the pocket of his coat and he watched Tova crank, and the pump cycled, and the pump cycled at eighty, and he could, with a touch, with a draw so small the reservoir would not, in the drawing, register, take the pump to one hundred.

He did not.

He stood at the wall.

The hand wanted to move. He held it in the pocket. The pump cycled. The water came up. The water went into the basin. The basin was the basin.

It worked.

It worked at eighty.

He let it stand.

He went back into the workshop.

* * *

They worked until dark.

They worked at the second bench, with Pell, who Davan asked the names of three things from before he asked Pell where Pell had fit, and Pell said *the canal quarter,* and Davan said *the lower mains,* and Pell said *the lower mains,* and Davan did not, in the saying, do anything more with the saying than nod the quarter-inch and go back to the gasket. The third, the hard one, came together on the second try.

Davan was good with his hands.

Ash had not expected this. He had expected the rhetorician. He had expected the listener. He had not expected the man who knew how a flange seated and how a gasket folded and how a wing nut tightened to the half-turn before the threads bound. Davan was the man at the bench who fit. Davan was, Ash registered, a man who had fit for six years as he had said on the crate at the bench on the day of the visit, and the six years were in his hands the way Haran's forty years were in Haran's, plainly, without performance.

At the seventh hour they put the tools down.

Davan washed his hands at the corner basin.

"Tea," Haran said, from the back bench, without turning.

"Thank you," Davan said.

Haran poured. Three cups again. The brown pot. The cup with the small chip Haran had not, in twenty years, replaced. Haran set the pot back on the bench and picked up the wire.

"I'll be in the back," Haran said.

He went. He went the way he had gone the night Davan first came, which was the same not-going, the same sitting on the stool eight feet away with the wire on the former. The not-going was, Ash registered, the not-going of a man who had decided, two weeks ago, to be in the room.

There was bread.

Haran kept bread in the pine box on the high shelf because Haran had kept bread in the pine box on the high shelf for forty years, and the bread was the bread the boy from the bakery brought down at the evening hour on Fifthdays, and there was a small wedge of cheese in the cool cupboard, and there was a jar of the harbor's sour pickle, which Ash did not eat and Haran did, and a knife, and a board, and Ash brought all of it to the bench and set it down and cut the bread.

They ate.

They ate the way two people who have worked together for an afternoon ate, without ceremony, with the cups on the bench and the lamp at the elbow and the second drawing's eight sheets pinned to the back wall behind them. The light at the high window was the light at the high window at the seventh hour in the eleventh week of the season, gold-thin, thinning. Davan ate the pickle. He ate it without comment. He had grown up, Ash registered, on the harbor's pickle, the way Ash had grown up on his mother's bread, and the not-commenting was not a courtesy. It was a man eating a thing he had eaten his whole life.

"My sister," Davan said, after a long while.

Ash looked up.

Davan was looking at the bread on the board.

"You haven't asked," he said. "You've asked about the mother. You've asked about the father. You haven't asked about the sister."

"I didn't know there was a sister."

"There is."

"What's her name."

"Nessa."

He said the name plainly. He said it the way he had said *Korel* and *Liss* in the square at the fourth hour the day of the visit, which was the way he said names that were not, in the saying, required to do anything for him.

"Younger."

"Six years."

"Mm."

"Her pool is."

He stopped. He picked up the cup. He held it. He set it down without drinking.

"It is what mine is, less. It is what mine is less the six years of decline between her birth and mine. It is, on a good morning, enough to warm the water in a basin if she warms it slowly. On a bad morning it is not that."

"Bad mornings are most mornings."

"Most."

"Mm."

A pause.

"She lives with my mother," Davan said. "She works in the same room my mother works in. She winds bobbin. The bobbin needs a hand. The hand needs a wrist. The wrist." He stopped. He started again. "The wrist is mechanical. The wrist is what a wrist is when a wrist is the only tool you have. By the seventh hour she cannot, on a normal day, lift the bobbin to the second shelf."

Ash held his cup.

"She is twenty-one," Davan said.

"Twenty-one."

"Yes."

He looked up.

He looked at Ash the way he had looked at him at the door two weeks ago, and the look this time was the look he had not given that day, because that day the looking had been the looking of a man who had not yet been into the room. He had been into the room. He had been into the cellar. He had set the flange. He had eaten the bread.

"She doesn't need your proof-of-concept," Davan said.

He said it without sharpness.

He said it the way he had asked the question of the lift at the pump and the question of the wrap at the riser and the question at the door. He said it as a sentence whose answer he wanted, not as a sentence whose effect he was measuring. The effect was the effect anyway.

Ash set the cup down.

He looked, for a beat, past Davan, at the eight sheets on the back wall, at the wing nut on the third sheet, at the flange that had come together on the second bench at the eleventh hour with Tesha's hands and Mira's hands and the cost in time and the hour the western pump was short by, and he looked back.

"I've been watching Haran," he said.

It was not, in the starting, the answer he had thought he would give. He had thought he would give the answer about the year and the district and the time. He gave a different answer. He gave the answer he had been working out at the bench for the four days before Davan came.

"Watching him work."

"Mm."

"He uses his pool. He uses it differently than anyone I've ever watched use a pool. He uses, on most days, a fraction of the draw a person his age would use to do the same thing. He uses, on the heating, less than my father would. On the lamp, less than Hellen. On the wall, less than Maren, when Maren could." He stopped. He started again. "He's not careful. He's not stingy. He's efficient. The efficiency is in the work. He chose the lever. He chose the angle. He set the brass cold and moved it warm, and the moving warm took a draw a man like me would have used four times to do."

"Mm."

"There's a gap. Between what he does and what most people do. The gap is in the technique. The gap is in the thinking. The gap might be." He stopped. "The gap might be teachable. If it's teachable. If you could close even half of it, in a generation, the math at three percent changes. It doesn't change the end. It changes the slope."

Davan listened.

He listened the way he had listened on the bench the first night, which was to say he listened to the answer until the answer ended. Then he was, in the small visible turning of his attention, with the answer for a beat. Then he asked the next thing.

"You're describing a solution," Davan said, "for people who still have something."

"I'm describing a multiplier."

"For people who still have something."

"Yes."

"My sister has nothing to be efficient with."

He said it without sharpness.

He said it with the small calm of a man who had spent six years in a great many rooms saying versions of the same sentence to people who did not have, in the moment of the saying, an answer to it. He said it the way a man said a fact. The fact was the fact. He had named it. He let it sit.

Ash sat with it.

He sat with it the way he had sat with *it is not enough* the night of the first visit, which was to say he sat with it knowing that the answer he had to it was not, in the moment, an answer he could give without the answer becoming the sentence the powerful gave the powerless. He had thought, in the four days before Davan came, that he had a different answer than the one he had thought he had. He looked at the answer in his hand. The answer was the answer. It did not, in the looking, become a different answer because Davan's sister was twenty-one.

"No," he said.

"No."

A pause.

The pause was the pause that had been in the room three nights before at the door. It was the pause of two people each hearing what the other had meant. Davan heard, in Ash's *no,* the small honest acknowledgement that the multiplier did not, in this winter, help Nessa. Ash heard, in Davan's *no,* the sentence Davan had not said, which was *and the multiplier is your way of changing the subject from what you have to what other people might learn to have less of.*

Neither of them said it.

They drank tea.

After a long while Davan said:

"It's good bread."

"It's the boy from the bakery."

"Mm."

He cut another piece. He ate it. He brushed crumbs from the bench into his palm and put the palm into the pocket of his coat, the way a man cleared crumbs in a room he had decided to be careful in.

"I should go," he said.

"Stay. I mean, you don't have to, but, yeah, stay."

He shook his head.

"Korel will want me."

"Mm."

He stood.

He stood the way he had stood the first night, slowly, with one hand on the bench. He looked at Haran at the back bench. He looked at the eight sheets on the wall. He looked at Ash.

"Thank you for the day."

"Welcome. Yeah."

"I'll be back."

"Come back. Please."

At the door he stopped. He stopped with his hand on the frame. He did not, this time, turn and ask the question. He had asked the question two weeks ago. He had the answer he had gotten to the question two weeks ago. He did not, this evening, need to ask it again.

He said, instead:

"The flange's a good design."

"It works."

"Works at."

"Eighty."

"Mm."

He held the *mm* a beat. He nodded the quarter-inch. He went out.

The door closed.

* * *

Ash stood at the bench.

The cups were on the bench. Three of them. The bread board was on the bench. The pickle jar was on the bench, lidded. The eight sheets were on the wall. The lamp was on the bench. The reservoir was where the reservoir was, behind the back of his attention, steady, not quite as full as it had been at the morning, because he had drawn for the cistern valve on Sixth-day and for the seal on Tuesday, and because in the running of the four days he had not, in his head, been able to let the running go. He registered the level. He had registered the level the way a man registered a level since the math night a year and a half ago, which was the way a man registered a thing he was responsible for. *Thirty-seven.* He had been at thirty-eight in the morning. The cistern had cost half. The flue and the wrap and the seal had cost the rest.

Half a percent.

In two weeks.

For one district.

Haran wound wire.

He wound for the count of forty. The wire turned on the former with the small clean sound the wire made. He set the former down. He did not, in the setting down, turn.

"He's right," Haran said.

"He is."

"And so are you."

Ash looked at the back bench. Haran was, at the back bench, with his side to the front bench, doing the thing he did when he had said the sentence he was, this evening, going to say.

"The two things don't cancel," Haran said.

"No. I mean. No. They don't."

"Mm."

He picked up the wire.

Ash stood at the bench.

He looked at the eight sheets on the back wall. He looked at the wing nut on the third sheet, at the flange on the fifth, at the gasket schedule on the seventh. He looked at the second drawing, which was ugly, and which would be fixed by the crews on Firstday at the third joint Pell had said was hard, and which would, when it was finished, be the drawing of a system a person with a wrench could understand. He looked at the drawing the way a man looked at his best work, which was a way of looking he was, this evening, not familiar with, because his best work, until this month, had been the drawing on the wall before the second drawing had gone up, and that drawing was, this evening, in the bin at the back of the workshop, folded once, not because he had been angry with it but because there was no longer a wall it belonged on.

He thought: *I built a monument.*

He thought: *I built a monument and called it a school.*

He thought: *the school is the thing I am building now.*

He stood at the bench. His hand was on the bench. The lamp was on the bench. The pump in the square was warm. The dark windows of the canal quarter were dark, and Nessa was twenty-one, and the gap between the answer he was building and the answer she needed was a gap a wing nut and a flange and a bolted gasket would not, in this winter, close.

He picked up the cups.

He carried them to the corner basin. He rinsed them. He set them on the rack. He picked up the bread board and brushed the crumbs into the small tin Haran kept beside the stove for the birds in the square. He lidded the pickle. He set the jar on the cool cupboard's lower shelf. He took the lamp from the bench and carried it to the back bench and set it at Haran's elbow, where the light would fall on the wire.

Haran did not look up.

"Mm," Haran said.

Ash went up the stair.

He lay on the bedroll. He did not light the lamp. The reservoir sat. He registered it.

He thought about Nessa.

He had not met her. He thought about her anyway. He thought about the bobbin and the second shelf and the seventh hour in a weaving room in the mill quarter on a Fifthday in the eleventh week of the season. He thought about Tova sending the boy. He thought about Reska's bandage. He thought about the western pump cycling at eighty in the cold, with his hand in the pocket of his coat at the bakery wall, and the not-doing in the hand.

He thought about the sentence he had, in the meeting, said aloud to the crews.

*I built it wrong.*

The sentence sat the way *it is not enough* sat. The first was, by the meeting on the seventh hour, a sentence the crews had been allowed to hear. The second was a sentence he had said only once, to Davan, on a crate, with Haran winding wire eight feet away.

He thought: *I will say the second one again.*

He thought: *not tonight.*

He turned on the bedroll.

The pump was warm. The flange would weep in the first month, and the gasket would be replaced, and the replacement would take a person with a wrench an hour, and the hour would be the hour the work was supposed to take.

Below, in the workshop, Haran wound wire.

The lamp burned.

The door, in the way Haran left the door at the eighth hour when there was a thing on his mind, was unbolted.

He did not, by morning, bolt it.
