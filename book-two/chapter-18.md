# Chapter 18

Ash came up to the south block on foot, with no roll of paper under his arm and nothing in his hands but a stub of pencil and a cheap paper book the size of his palm, and he stopped at the head of the lane and looked at Building Six the way a man looked at a thing he had not, until that morning, looked at.

The morning was the colorless morning of late autumn in the city, the sky low, the cobbles holding the night cold the way cobbles did when the wind had gone soft. The lane ran up the slope and then up again. He had walked it many times. He had walked it with rolls of paper, and with a foreman, and with the small attendant pride of a man who had drawn a thing and was about to see it stand. He had not, on those walks, looked at the slope.

The slope was a slope.

The pump was at the foot of it.

He stood at the head of the lane and looked down at the pump housing and up at the doors that opened on the upper terrace, and he counted, in the small accounting a man did when he was, at last, paying attention, the number of buckets a woman might carry up the slope in a day. He counted twelve. He counted twenty. He stopped counting.

He went down to the pump.

Tesha was already at the pump. She had her sleeves rolled to the elbow, and a length of waxed cord in her teeth, and the cover plate of the housing open against her knee, and she was doing the small ungentle thing one did to a packing washer that had gone to leather. She did not look up when he came up to her. She knew his step. She had known his step for a while now.

"Morning."

"Morning."

She took the cord out of her teeth. She seated the packing the way one seated a packing, with the small impatient firmness of a person who had done the seating more times than she cared to count, and she closed the cover, and she sat back on her heels, and she looked up at him.

"No paper today," she said.

"No paper."

"Mm."

She wiped her hands on the rag at her belt. She stood. She came up to her full height, which was a finger less than his, and she looked at him with the steady, evaluating look she had, which was the look of a woman who had spent six months figuring out what worked and was not, on any given morning, in a hurry to be told.

"What are you here for, then."

"Questions."

"Mm."

"If you have time."

She looked at him a count. She looked at the pump. She looked up the lane.

"I'll have time," she said, "when Pell comes in. Pell's at the heating row. Mira's at Building Four. We're down to the seal on the third valve and there's a man on the upper terrace whose stove won't catch and I told him I'd come at noon. So. Between Pell and the man. Is when I'll have time."

"All right."

"Walk with me."

"All right."

She picked up the wooden tool box at her feet, the box she had built herself out of a crate, and she settled it against her hip the way she settled it, and she started up the lane, and Ash went with her.

She did not, going up, slow her pace for him.

He liked that.

* * *

They went up the slope past Building Six, and at the door of Building Six she stopped and set the box down and pointed at the doorsill, and she did not say anything, and he looked at the doorsill, and he saw what she was pointing at.

The pump delivered to a basin set in the alcove at the foot of Building Six. The basin filled. From the basin, a person filled a bucket. From the basin, a person carried the bucket up the inside stair to the rooms on the upper floors. The inside stair had thirty-one treads. He had counted, when he had drawn the building, the treads of the inside stair, because the treads had mattered to the load-bearing of the inside wall. He had not, at the time, thought of the treads as treads a person walked up with a full bucket.

"How many buckets a day," he said.

"For this building."

"Yes."

"Twenty. Thirty in laundry weeks. Forty if there's a sick child."

"Mm."

"The basin's at the bottom because the pump's at the bottom because the pump runs off the falling line from the cistern. I read that on your drawing. I read it twice."

"Mm."

"The cistern's up there." She pointed, with the flat of her hand, at the high ridge above the upper terrace, where the city's old line ran. "The water comes down through the line. The line ends at the pump. The pump pushes a little more pressure into the basin. The basin is here because that's where the pump is. The pump is here because that's where the line ends."

"Yes."

"And the people are up there." She pointed up the inside stair. "The basin is down here. The line ends down here. The pump is down here. The buckets go up there."

She looked at him.

He looked at the basin.

He had, when he had drawn it, thought it was elegant. He had thought it was elegant because the falling line and the pump were a clean piece of engineering, and the basin sat at the natural terminus of the system, and the water came in at the basin the way water came in at a basin in a clean diagram. He had not, when he had drawn it, walked up the inside stair with a full bucket.

He took the cheap paper book out of his pocket.

He opened it to the first page.

He wrote, with the stub of pencil, in the careful hand, *Building Six. Basin in wrong place. Pump too low. Water has to be carried up.*

He wrote, under that, *How.*

He looked at Tesha.

"Where would you put it."

She looked at him a count, the way she had looked at him at the pump, and then she looked up the slope past the door of Building Six, at the small landing where the inside stair met the upper terrace, and she looked at the wall of the inside stair from the outside, and she looked back at him.

"You're asking me."

"I'm asking you."

"Mm."

She set the box down again. She walked to the side of the building. She walked the side of the building to the back. He went with her. She stopped at the back wall, where the line came down from the ridge and joined the housing at a small dressed-stone collar, and she put her hand on the collar.

"You could break the line here."

"Yes."

"You could run a length up the back to the upper terrace."

"Yes."

"You could put a second basin at the upper terrace. Smaller. With a stop. The pump still runs at the bottom, for the lower floors. The upper terrace gets its own."

"Yes."

"Two basins. Two stops. Twice the maintenance."

"Yes."

"And the people on the upper terrace stop carrying buckets up thirty-one steps."

"Yes."

She looked at him.

He looked at her.

He was aware, standing at the back wall with his hand a finger's width from the dressed-stone collar where the line came down, of the small pull at his shoulder that was the pull of a man who had drawn a thing and did not want, at this hour, to admit that he had drawn it badly. The pull was an old pull. He had been feeling the pull for a year and a half. He had been feeling it, until this morning, without quite knowing what it was.

He let it go.

He let it go the way Haran let go of a thing at the end of a working, which was not a stopping but a letting-go of the held thing into the letting-go, and the pull went, and what was under it was the small, plain, uncomplicated thing he should have been looking at all along, which was a back wall, and a collar, and a second basin, and a smaller pump, and the people on the upper terrace not carrying buckets up thirty-one steps.

"You're right," he said.

"Mm."

"It should be at the top. Or there should be a second one at the top. You're right."

"Mm."

"I should have seen it months ago."

She looked at the back wall.

"You weren't looking at it," she said. "You were looking at the drawing."

He wrote in the book.

He wrote *Tesha. Second basin at upper terrace. Break line at back collar. Smaller stop, smaller pump.* He wrote it carefully. He underlined *Tesha*.

She watched him write.

"You writing my name down."

"Yes."

"What for."

"So when it works, I know whose idea it was."

She made the small sound she made when she was not going to argue with a thing but was not, on the other hand, going to thank a man for it either, and she picked up the box, and she went back around the side of the building.

He followed.

* * *

They went up the slope to the heating row at the north edge of the south block, and on the way Tesha named, in the flat voice she used for naming, three other things he had drawn that did not fit the way the people in the buildings used them. The maintenance hatch on the heating element of Building Two opened inward into the kitchen, which meant the hatch could not be opened when the family was eating. The drain in the wash-yard of Building Four sat at the high corner instead of the low corner, which meant the yard had to be swept toward the drain instead of the drain doing the work of a drain. The training they had had in the spring had taught them how to operate the heating elements, but it had not, when the elements did the small unscheduled thing they did in the second autumn, taught them how to think their way to the cause.

He wrote.

He wrote each of them down.

He did not, while writing, say *I'll redesign it*. He did not say *I'll come back this week with a plan*. He wrote them down and underlined the names of the people who had told him, and he asked, after each, where they thought it should be, and Tesha, and Pell when they came up to Pell at the heating row, and Mira when Mira came down from Building Four for the noon, told him.

Pell was at the heating row with his hand on the second plate.

Pell looked up when they came up and did the small lift of the chin he did, which was Pell's whole greeting, and he went back to his hand on the plate. The plate was the plate that had failed in the spring at the upper post. Tesha had brought it back in an afternoon. The plate was warm under Pell's palm, the steady patient warm of a heating element doing the work of a heating element, and Pell stood with his hand on it and watched the line of plates run on up the row and listened to the small sound the row made, which was the sound of a row of heating plates that was running well.

"How is it," Ash said.

"Running."

"Mm."

"Plate three's tight on the wrap. I'll have it off tomorrow. Plate seven's noisy. Plate seven's been noisy a month. I don't know what's making it noisy."

"What does the noise sound like."

Pell looked at him.

"Like a noise."

"All right."

"It's not a wrap noise. It's not a contact noise. It's a, "

He stopped. He worked his jaw a count. He looked at the plate.

"A breath. Like the plate's breathing. In, out. Slow."

Ash wrote *plate seven, noise like breathing, slow*. He wrote it under Pell's name.

Pell watched him write.

"You writing that down."

"Yes."

"Mm."

He went back to his hand on the second plate.

* * *

They came back down the slope at the middle of the afternoon, the three of them, Tesha and Pell and Ash, with the cheap paper book most of the way through its first ten pages and with the noon man's stove on the upper terrace caught and warming and the seal on the third valve on the order list for the carter, and they stopped at the foundation of the new annex at the end of Building Four, where the work that did require him was waiting.

The annex was a small thing. It was a single room added to the back of Building Four, to make a workshop space for two of the residents who had, in the slow way of people in a district that had been at it a year, started taking on the small repairs of their neighbors. The foundation had been poured in the autumn. The reinforcement was the reinforcement Ash did, which was a catalytic working into the stone at the joint between the new pour and the old wall, to bind them so the new work did not, in the first hard frost, pull away from the old.

He had done a dozen of these.

He had done them, until this autumn, the way he had done everything, with the wide loose press, the way water came at a tap, and the stone took up the press and the joint bound and the work was done, and the spending was the spending of a man who had not yet known he was wasting.

He stood at the joint.

He set his palm a finger's width from the line where the new pour met the old wall.

Tesha and Pell stood at the corner of the annex. They had stood at corners while he worked before. They had, in the spring and the summer, stood at corners and watched him channel and felt the small warm pressure of his channeling at their pools the way a person felt sun on the skin, and he had, at the time, thought it was the way a thing was watched. He had not, at the time, thought of what they saw.

They were watching now.

He breathed.

He thought about the offcut. He thought about the upper post. He thought about the thin line in his palm at the bench in the dark before the grey, holding for three breaths, four. He thought about the joint. He thought about what the joint needed, which was not a wide loose press into the whole length of the new pour, which was what he had been doing, but the small clean binding along the line of the joint itself, the catalytic touch that asked the stone to take up the bond and then let go.

He narrowed.

He narrowed the way he had narrowed at the bench, slowly, to the edge, and at the edge he held, and at the holding he found the thin line, and he set the thin line at the joint.

The joint took it.

The joint took it the way a piece of dry tinder took a small clean flame, in the small accepting way of a thing that had been waiting to take a thing, and the bind ran along the line of the joint and the stone settled into it and the working was the working it was, which was a working a fraction the size of any working he had done at a foundation in a year and a half, and the joint was bound, and he held it a count, and he pulled back.

He pulled back the way Haran pulled back, into the letting-go.

The thin line went.

He stood with his hand at his side.

The joint sat quiet.

He breathed.

He turned. Tesha and Pell were at the corner. They had not moved. Pell had his arms folded. Tesha had one hand on the cornerstone the way she put her hand on a thing when she was thinking.

"That was different," she said.

"Yes."

"Smaller."

"Yes."

"You spend less."

"Yes."

"How much less."

He looked at the joint.

"Maybe a third. Maybe more. I don't have the feel of it yet."

"Mm."

Pell unfolded his arms.

"Less waste," he said.

"Less waste."

"More precision."

"More precision."

Pell nodded the small nod he nodded when a thing had landed in him in the place a thing landed.

"We been doing that," he said, "with the coal."

"Yes."

"With everything."

"Yes."

Pell looked at the joint. He looked at Tesha. He looked at Ash.

"All right," he said.

* * *

The communal heating element went in at the end of the south block at the close of the afternoon.

It was a thing the district had asked for in the summer, and which Ash had drawn in the summer, and which had sat in the to-do of the work because he had not wanted, until this autumn, to decide what it was. A communal element was a Levelers' thing. A communal element was the thing the redistribution programs had been running on the spring grids until the grids weakened in the autumn, and the thing the elder channelers had been pouring their pools into until the pools thinned. He had not wanted to draw a Levelers' thing. He had also not wanted to draw a thing that the residents of the south block could not, between them, keep going.

He had been drawing it, in his head, as one or the other.

He set it down at the end of the lane that afternoon as both.

The element was a single iron plate set in a stone housing, the housing low, the plate the size of two hands laid flat. He brought it on a barrow with Pell. He set it in the housing with Tesha. The housing had been built by two of the residents in the autumn, and the residents were there, four of them now, standing at the edge of the lane with the small attentive standing of people who had built the housing and wanted, in the way a person wanted, to see the thing they had built begin.

He laid his palm on the plate.

He breathed.

He narrowed.

He set the thin line at the plate and he held it and the catalytic working went down into the iron and the iron took it up the way iron took up a thing that was meant for it, and the plate warmed, and the warming was a clean warming, and the warming was the warming of the thing that would be, after this afternoon, the thing the residents kept going.

He held it three breaths.

He held it four.

He pulled back.

The plate sat warm.

He stepped back from the plate. He looked at the residents. He looked at Tesha. He looked at Pell.

"It's set," he said.

"Mm."

"It'll hold the catalytic for a week. A week and a half. After that it's yours. You channel into it the way you channel. Small amounts. Whatever you have. You don't have to fill it. You just have to keep it warm."

The oldest of the residents, a woman with grey at her temples and a mended scarf at her throat, came up to the housing. She put her hand on the plate. She did not channel. She stood with her hand on the plate a count, and then she nodded the small nod a person nodded when a thing had been taken in.

"Like the kettle," she said.

"Like the kettle."

"Mm."

She turned to the woman beside her, and the woman beside her put her hand on the plate, and Ash stepped back, and Tesha stepped back with him, and they stood at the edge of the lane and watched the residents, in the small unhurried way of people with their hands on a warm thing, take turns at the plate.

Tesha looked at him sidelong.

"That's not your design," she said.

"No."

"That's not the Levelers' grid."

"No."

"That's a third thing."

"Yes."

"Mm."

She watched the residents a count.

"It'll work," she said.

"It might."

"It'll work."

He walked back through the district at evening.

The light had gone from grey to the colored grey of a city evening with the cooking fires lit, and the lanes were the lanes they were, narrow and a little crooked and warm at the doors where the doors stood open and women called in their children and the children, in the way of children, ignored the calling and kept at whatever the small important thing was that they were at. He walked up the slope past the new basin at the back of Building Six. The basin was not in yet. The mark for the basin was in, in chalk, on the back wall, where Tesha had drawn it that afternoon. The mark sat at the upper terrace and the residents who had walked past it had stopped at it and looked at it, and the looking, in the way looking went in a district, was already the beginning of the thing.

He walked past the heating row.

The plates ran. Plate seven made its breathing noise, in, out, slow. Pell stood at plate three with the cover off and his hand on the wrap and a length of waxed cord in his teeth, and he did not look up.

He walked past the new communal element at the end of the lane.

A man he did not know was at the plate. The man had his hand on the iron and his eyes closed and the small careful look of a man with a small pool channeling in the small careful way a man with a small pool channeled, and the plate sat warm under the man's hand, and Ash walked past without stopping, because the man did not need a man to stop and watch him.

He came down to the lower lane.

The doors were open. The cooking smells came out of the doors. Somewhere up the slope a child was being called in for the third time. The wind had gone soft in the late afternoon and now the wind had gone away, and the lanes held the small contained warmth of a district that was, at this hour, at about seventy percent of what a fully channeled district would have been at this hour, which was less, and which was enough, and the people in the doors were in the doors the way people stood in doors when the inside was warm enough to come out of for a count and the outside was cold enough to go back into, and that was the thing.

That was the thing.

He walked down with his hands in his coat pockets. The book was in the inside pocket against the bench paper and Joren's letter. Maren's note was in the outside pocket on the other side. He did not, walking, take any of them out.

He passed Tesha's workshop.

It was the converted storage room at the back of Building Four, the room she had cleared in the summer, which had been a place for old crates and which was, now, a place with a bench at the wall and a row of pegs above the bench and a small clean working space in the middle. The door was open. The lamp was lit. Tesha was at the bench, and at the bench with her, on either side, were two women he had not seen at the work before. They were neighbors. He could tell from the way they stood at the bench, which was the way a person stood at a thing she had not done before but had, in the slow social way of a district, watched her neighbor do.

Tesha had a pressure valve in her hand.

She was showing them the seat of the valve.

She was saying, in the flat voice she used for naming, *here, and here, and you feel the give there, you feel that, that's gone, you feel that in the give, and that's when you change it*, and the two women were watching the valve, and one of them was reaching, as she watched, to take the valve from Tesha, and Tesha was, in the small unceremonial way of a person handing a tool to a person, handing it to her.

He stood at the doorway.

He did not, for a count, go in.

He stood at the doorway in the lane outside the open door, with the lamp warm at the bench inside and the two women bent over the valve and Tesha's hand at the seat showing the give, and he stood the way a man stood at a door he did not need to go through, because the thing inside was the thing inside, and was not a thing his going through would help.

Tesha did not look up.

Tesha did not need to look up.

He walked on.

He went down the slope to the lower lane and out through the gate at the end of the south block, and at the gate, in the small accidental gesture of a man who had not, in the walking, known he was going to make it, he turned and looked back up the lane.

The lamps were on at the doors.

The heating row ran.

The communal plate sat warm at the end of the lane with a person's hand on it whom he did not know.

Tesha's lamp was at the back of Building Four.

He stood at the gate a count.

He walked on.

The workshop was warm when he came in. Haran was at the long bench with the letters, and the lamp was at the corner, and the forge was banked the way Haran banked the forge in the evening, and on the corner of the bench at the going-in place was a folded square of cheap paper that had not been there in the morning.

"South block."

"Mm."

"Element went in."

"Yes."

"Mm."

He turned back to the letters. He had a stack of three. He had the small paring knife he used to open the seal on a country letter. He did not say what was in any of them. He never did. He read them in the order they had come, and he set them in the rows on the long bench in the order he set them, and the bench was the bench it was.

He came to the bench.

Ash sat at the stool with Maren's note folded square on his knee, and the warmth of the south block sat in his coat, and the cold of the gate sat in his coat under it, and he held the note a count, and a count, and he put the note in the inside pocket of his coat, where it had not been before, against the bench paper and the book and Joren's letter, and the outside pocket on the other side was, for the first time in a season, empty.

He had heard the crier without listening. He had not, walking down, wanted to listen. The south block was not on the list now.

The south block was on a third thing.

They went up the curve and across the lower terrace and into the south block at the gate, and at the gate she stopped, and she looked up the lane the way she had looked at the workshop, which was with the unhurried evaluating look, and she went up the lane, and Ash went with her.

She walked past Building Six and looked, at the back wall, at the chalk mark where the second basin would go. She walked up the slope and stopped at the heating row and stood at plate three, where Pell had the cover off and his hand on the wrap, and Pell looked up and did the small lift of the chin, and Dorenne did, in return, the small precise nod she did, and she went on. The plate was warm. A child was at the plate. The child was a girl of six or seven, and she had her hand on the iron the way the residents had had their hands on the iron the day before, and she was not channeling, because she had not, at six or seven, anything to channel, but she had her hand on it, in the small grave way a child put her hand on a warm thing in a cold lane, and Dorenne stood at the edge of the lane and watched her.

The child looked up.

The child looked at Dorenne the way a child looked at a stranger, which was direct, and unafraid, and unimpressed, and after a count she took her hand off the plate and went up the lane to her door.

She turned. She walked back down the lane. She walked into the alley.

The alley was narrow. The wall of Building Four on one side, the wall of the next building on the other, the cobbles wet from the morning's rain. The alley was empty. There were no aides. There was Dorenne in her dark wool coat and Ash in his, and the sound of Tesha's voice, low, at the bench inside, and the sound of the woman's hands on the valve.

Dorenne stopped at the middle of the alley.

She did not turn to face him.

She faced the wall.

She took one of her gloves off. She put the glove in the pocket of her coat. She put her bare hand against the brick of the wall and she stood with her hand on the brick a count, and the brick was the brick of a building Ash had not built, which had been there before he was born, which would be there after the magic was gone, and she stood with her hand on it and she did not, for a count, speak.

When she spoke, she spoke to the wall.

"They've called a vote," she said.

"Yes."

"Yes."

"To dissolve the authority. Not to remove me. To dissolve the office. The function goes to the council. The council votes by district. The districts vote by pool size."

"Mm."

"Which means the elder districts will keep the votes, and the elder districts will keep voting the way they vote, until the elder districts no longer have the pools to vote, and then the function will go to whatever is there. Which will not be much."

"Mm."

"It is a coherent plan. It is a bad one. It will pass."

"Mm."

"I spent thirty years," she said, "building systems that are going to die with the magic."

"You spent six months building systems that might not."

He did not, for a count, answer.

He stood at the wall a pace from her, with his hands in his pockets and the cold of the alley on his face, and he listened to the sound of Tesha's voice at the bench inside, low, even, and the sound of the woman's hands on the valve, and he did not, in the count, fill the pause.

"I know."

She faced him.

She faced him for the first time since the alley, and her face was the composed face, and her eyes were the tired eyes, and her mouth was the careful mouth.

"I don't know how to do what you're doing," she said.

"Mm."

"I don't know how to build things I can't control."

He looked at her.

He had, for two years, looked at this woman across the long table in her office, and the small council rooms, and the bridge, and the warehouse on the lower terrace, and he had looked at her in the Small Hall on the day she had asked him for a thing he had not given, and he had not, in the looking, ever seen the thing he saw now, which was the small visible cost of a sentence said by a woman who did not, ever, say sentences that cost her.

He did not, for a count, say anything.

He held her eye.

"I know," he said.

She nodded once.

She put the glove back on. She did it the way she did things, which was with the precise unhurried motion of a woman whose hands knew what her hands were for. She straightened the cuff of the coat. She did not, in the alley, ask him for a thing. She did not, in the alley, offer him a thing. She did the small straightening, and she turned, and she walked out of the alley the way she had walked into it, which was without looking back.

Ash stood at the wall a count.
